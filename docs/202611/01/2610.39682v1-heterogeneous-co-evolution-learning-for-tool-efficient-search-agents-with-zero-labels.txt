Abstract 

Deep search agents extend language models by interleaving reasoning with external retrieval. However, effective search-agent training requires addressing two challenges beyond final-answer optimization. First, the capability frontier of agents is largely determined by training questions, whereas fixed or labeled task collections cannot adapt as agents im-prove. Second, outcome-only reinforcement learning treats all successful trajectories equally, even when some correct answers rely on redundant searches, creating an inherent ten-sion between answer accuracy and tool efficiency. To address these issues, we introduce ZEST-Search, a zero-label and tool-efficient framework for search-agent training via het-erogeneous co-evolution. Starting from unlabeled reference questions, ZEST-Search constructs validated search tasks online through an answer-first and evidence-path-guided gen-eration process. A heterogeneous proposer adaptively con-structs training tasks around the solver’s evolving capability boundary, while the solver learns search-augmented reason-ing through reinforcement learning. To jointly optimize task difficulty and search efficiency, ZEST-Search employs an intermediate-difficulty reward for the proposer and a task-local tool-aware reward for the solver, encouraging informa-tive tasks and correct answers with fewer redundant searches. Experiments on seven general-domain and multi-hop question answering benchmarks demonstrate that ZEST-Search con-sistently improves search-agent performance while achieving higher tool efficiency without labeled search-training data or expert search trajectories. Further analyses show that hetero-geneous generation, adaptive difficulty control, and tool-aware optimization enable more effective search-agent training. 

## Introduction 

Deep search agents tackle challenging questions by inter-leaving reasoning with external retrieval. Unlike standard retrieval-augmented generation, they must determine not only which evidence to use, but also when and how to search. Retrieval-augmented models established external ev-idence as central to knowledge-intensive question answer-ing (Guu et al. 2020; Karpukhin et al. 2020; Lewis et al. 2020), while multi-hop benchmarks highlighted the need to integrate evidence across documents and entities (Yang et al. 2018; Trivedi et al. 2022). More recent tool-using and retrieval-aware agents integrate search into the reasoning

loop, enabling adaptive tool use during inference (Nakano et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023; Asai et al. 2024; Trivedi et al. 2023). Accordingly, training search agents requires optimiz-ing both final-answer correctness and search behavior to pro-duce evidence-grounded trajectories. Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a prominent approach for training such search behavior. Early studies train language models to invoke search during reasoning us-ing outcome-based rewards derived from final-answer cor-rectness (Jin et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025b). Subsequent studies further explore search-cost mod-eling, stepwise feedback, and data-efficient RL for search agents (Sun et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2025b). Despite this progress, existing methods still fail to jointly control two key determinants of search-agent learning. First, training questions largely define the frontier of learnable ca-pabilities. A fixed question set can become a static curricu-lum, preventing RL from exposing new agent weaknesses. Second, outcome-only rewards cannot distinguish concise successful search trajectories from redundant ones. Conse-quently, optimizing answer correctness alone may improve accuracy while increasing tool-use costs. To address the first limitation, recent self-play methods for-mulate learning as an adaptive proposer–solver game (Zhao et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025a; Fang et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025). In search-agent training, the proposer generates verifi-able questions, while the solver answers them through search. The performance of the solver then guides proposer updates, allowing task difficulty to evolve with the solver throughout training (Lu et al. 2025; Yue et al. 2026; Wu et al. 2026). However, task generation is typically constrained by fixed predefined sources, such as answer pools, limiting explo-ration beyond the initial task space. Assigning both roles to the same model further couples online generation cost to solver scale and limits signal complementarity, since shared knowledge priors can confine generated questions to famil-iar regions. This coupling is especially problematic early in training, when base models may produce fewer valid and in-formative questions, yielding fewer effective training groups and slower optimization. Moreover, adaptive task generation alone does not resolve the second limitation and may even ex-acerbate tool inefficiency. As generated tasks become harder, a solver optimized solely for final-answer correctness may re-sort to increasingly extensive search, even when additional arXiv:2610.39682v1 [cs.LG] 29 Oct 2026 queries provide little marginal value. To address these limitations, we propose ZEST-Search, a heterogeneous proposer–solver co-evolution framework for 

Zero-label, tool-Efficient Search-agent Training. Rather than sampling answers from a fixed pool, the proposer first gener-ates target entities conditioned on unlabeled reference ques-tions. Each target is validated for semantic compatibility, en-tity type, extractability, and retrieval support. ZEST-Search then constructs source-supported multi-hop evidence paths terminating at the validated targets and conditions ques-tion generation on these paths. Programmatic verification filters malformed, answer-leaking, and insufficiently sup-ported tasks before solver training. These target-validation, evidence-construction, and task-filtering stages rely only on lightweight retrieval and NLP components, requiring no ad-ditional LLM inference. Decoupling task generation from solving enables lightweight or cross-family proposers to gen-erate complementary tasks around the solver’s evolving capa-bility boundary. Role-specific objectives coordinate their co-evolution: an intermediate-difficulty reward steers the pro-poser toward validated questions near the solver’s learning frontier, while a task-local tool-aware reward favors correct trajectories with lower relative search costs. Together, these components expand the training space beyond predefined answer pools and enable zero-label learning of evidence-grounded and tool-efficient search behavior. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We propose a zero-label, answer-first pipeline that con-structs validated target entities, source-supported multi-hop evidence paths, and verifiable search tasks without labeled QA pairs or expert search trajectories. 

• By formulating search-agent training as heterogeneous proposer–solver co-evolution, our framework enables lightweight or cross-family proposers to generate com-plementary tasks around the solver’s evolving frontier. 

• Role-specific objectives jointly optimize task difficulty and search efficiency, favoring informative questions near the solver’s learning frontier and correct trajectories with fewer redundant searches.

Entity Answer Generation 

To avoid reliance on a fixed data source, we derive target entities online from abundant unlabeled questions. Given a reference question x, the proposer determines whether its expected answer is entity-valued. If so, it generates an entity of the implied semantic type. Otherwise, it identifies a salient entity in x and generates an entity of the same type. This procedure maps both cases to entity-valued targets suitable for entity-centric retrieval, without reference answers. Formally, the proposer samples

The prompt requests one concrete, distinct, and retrievable en-tity rather than a direct answer to x, avoiding the complex and error-prone process of answer inference. The candidate 

ea is then screened by a lightweight validation pipeline based on deterministic parsing, entity recognition, and answer-side retrieval verification. These non-generative checks require no additional LLM inference and efficiently filter out mal-formed outputs and non-entity candidates. Candidates pass-ing all checks are accepted as a⋆. The validated target anchors evidence-path construction and serves as the verification tar-get for the generated search task. 

Evidence-Path Guided Question Generation 

Given a retrieval-supported target entity a⋆, we construct a source-grounded evidence context for multi-hop question generation. We treat a⋆ as the answer node of a compact ev-idence graph and initialize the document pool with retrieval results obtained for a⋆.At each hop, each selected frontier entity is normalized and disambiguated before retrieval. Retrieved documents are segmented into sentences, and those mentioning both the selected entity and another valid entity are retained as evi-dence. Each co-occurring entity defines an evidence-backed edge and becomes a frontier candidate for the next hop. This process recursively expands the frontier until no valid can-didate remains or the maximum hop budget is reached. An evidence path from an outer clue to a⋆ is then constructed: 

p = ( v0 → v1 → · · · → a⋆) , (10) where the entities are ordered toward the target answer. Each edge is paired with its source document and supporting sen-tence. Paths with repeated entities, discontinuous transitions, or insufficient evidence are discarded. Remaining candidates are ranked by evidence quality and retrieval relevance, and the top-ranked path is selected. The resulting evidence con-text is represented as 

e = ( p, S, D) , (11) where S contains the supporting sentences and D contains the corresponding documents. The proposer then samples a question conditioned on the target and evidence context: 

uq ∼ πϕp (· | Pq (a⋆, e )) , q = Eq (uq ), (12) where Pq is the evidence-conditioned generation prompt and 

Eq extracts the question from the structured output. The prompt instructs the proposer to conceal the target while preserving the relational clues encoded by the path, yielding a multi-hop search task with the intended answer a⋆.Evidence path construction uses only lightweight retrieval and NLP components, while the proposer is invoked for ques-tion generation. Validated questions are then added to the ref-erence pool to support later rounds and progressively expand the training distribution.

Asymmetric Adversarial Rewards 

ZEST-Search formulates search-agent training as heteroge-neous proposer–solver co-evolution. The proposer generates tasks around the solver’s evolving capability boundary, while the solver learns from these tasks. Unlike self-play methods, the two roles can be instantiated with heterogeneous mod-els. We therefore use an intermediate-difficulty reward for the proposer to encourage informative tasks and a task-local tool-aware reward for the solver to favor correct answers with less unnecessary tool use. 

Intermediate-Difficulty Reward for the Proposer. The proposer should generate questions that remain informa-tive for the current solver. Prior curriculum-learning studies suggest that questions with intermediate pass rates provide stronger optimization signals than those almost always solved or missed (Leng et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2025a; Zeng et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2026). We therefore define the proposer reward using the grouped solver accuracy Acc( q):

Rproposer = 4Acc( q) [1 − Acc( q)] . (16) Invalid questions receive a fixed negative reward βinvalid < 0.For valid questions, Eq. 16 is maximized at Acc( q) = 0 .5 and vanishes for all-correct or all-wrong groups, thereby favoring tasks near the solver’s current learning frontier. A monotonic alternative, 1−Acc( q), assigns its maximum reward when all solver rollouts fail. However, complete fail-ure may reflect an incorrect target, an ambiguous question, or insufficient evidence rather than meaningful difficulty. By assigning zero reward to all-wrong groups, the intermediate-difficulty reward avoids this failure mode. 

Tool-Aware Reward for the Solver. External search should be used only when the solver’s parametric knowl-edge and available evidence are insufficient. Unnecessary retrieval increases inference cost and may introduce irrele-vant information. For each task, we define a task-local tool reward by normalizing search calls across its K rollouts:

Main Results 

RQ1: Can ZEST-Search improve search-augmented reasoning without labeled search-training data? We com-pare each solver before and after ZEST-Search training. Since existing self-play search-agent methods often adopt different model APIs, datasets, LLM-as-Judges, or retrieval environments, direct comparisons under identical training budgets remain challenging. We therefore additionally eval-uate ZEST-Search through continual training on search-specialized agents. Because training uses only unlabeled questions and constructs tasks online, these comparisons as-sess whether search-augmented reasoning improves without labeled QA pairs or expert trajectories. As shown in Table 1, ZEST-Search substantially improves search-augmented rea-soning using only unlabeled questions for task construction. Across all seven benchmarks, solvers of different scales and model families consistently benefit from training under di-verse proposer configurations, demonstrating robust gains on both general-domain and multi-hop question answering. The same procedure also improves search-specialized solvers, in-cluding Search-R1 (Jin et al. 2025) and ZeroSearch (Sun et al. 2025), showing that ZEST-Search can further enhance exist-ing search agents. These results show that ZEST-Search can both develop search-augmented reasoning from base mod-els and further improve agents that have already undergone search-oriented training. Overall, ZEST-Search improves the performance of search agents without labeled training data. 

RQ2: Can ZEST-Search effectively train solvers with lightweight proposers? We evaluate whether proposers smaller than the solver can generate effective training tasks, as reported in Table 1. Smaller proposers provide effective training signals for larger solvers. In particular, Qwen3-4B performs comparably to the larger Qwen2.5-7B proposer, while Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct also produces strong gains. The smaller but newer proposers, such as Qwen3 and LLaMA-3.2 models, can generate higher-quality training tasks, providing more effective supervision for solver improvement. These results show that online task generation can be delegated to lightweight models without matching the solver’s parameter scale. RQ3: Does ZEST-Search improve the trade-off between answer accuracy and search cost? As shown in Table 1, ZEST-Search improves answer accuracy while reducing av-erage search calls for most solvers. This indicates that the correctness-gated tool-aware objective encourages selective retrieval while preserving answer accuracy. Qwen2.5-3B-Base is the only exception, showing a slight increase in search calls alongside a substantial accuracy gain. Because this weak base model initially underuses search, the increase indicates more appropriate tool invocation rather than degraded ef-ficiency. After training, the solver learns to better balance parametric knowledge and retrieval. Overall, ZEST-Search improves the accuracy–cost trade-off by promoting selective search and avoiding redundant queries. 

RQ4: How does proposer heterogeneity affect task gener-ation and training dynamics? To analyze how proposer het-erogeneity affects training, we compare proposers with differ-ent model scales, families, and instruction alignment. First, cross-family proposers such as Qwen3-4B and LLaMA3.2-3B-Instruct introduce complementary knowledge priors and reasoning patterns, enabling larger solvers to benefit from di-verse task distributions without requiring the matching scale of the proposer. This indicates that task generation qual-ity is not strictly bounded by solver capacity. Beyond task diversity, proposer heterogeneity also affects how quickly useful supervision emerges during training. As shown in Figure 2(a), newer or instruction-tuned proposers generate a higher proportion of valid and informative questions at the beginning of training, providing stronger optimization sig-nals and accelerating solver improvement. Overall, hetero-geneous proposer–solver training decouples task generation cost, knowledge priors, and generation quality from solver capacity.

Table 3 compares direct answer-retrieval generation (AR), which conditions question generation on documents containing the target answer, with the proposed evidence-path-guided gener-ation (EPG). Even before proposer optimization, EPG yields substantially more multi-hop questions among examples, indicating that explicit evidence paths promote composi-tional generation beyond direct answer–document associa-tions. However, its lower initial validity and higher answer leakage indicate that structural guidance alone is insufficient for generating well-formed tasks. After training, EPG im-proves validity and leakage control while further increasing the proportion of multi-hop questions. Thus, evidence paths provide compositional structure, while proposer optimiza-tion converts them into valid and non-leaking QA pairs. 

Task Generation Case Study. Figure 3 illustrates how ZEST-Search constructs a multi-hop search task from an unlabeled reference question. Instead of transforming the ref-erence question, the proposer identifies the expected answer type and generates a target entity, Cinderella. Starting from this target, the framework constructs a source-supported evi-dence path connecting it to its director Kenneth Branagh, then to Belfast and Northern Ireland. Conditioned on this path, the proposer generates a multi-hop question requiring the solver to recover the target through intermediate evidence. This ex-ample demonstrates that evidence-path guidance encourages compositional question generation while maintaining veri-fiable answer grounding, enabling ZEST-Search to create challenging search tasks without labeled QA pairs. 

## Related Work 

Search-Augmented Language Models. Retrieval-augmented language models use external knowledge to improve generation on knowledge-intensive tasks (Guu et al. 2020; Karpukhin et al. 2020; Lewis et al. 2020). Later systems integrate retrieval directly into reasoning, enabling models to issue queries, inspect evidence, and refine their answers (Nakano et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023; Asai et al. 2024; Trivedi et al. 2023). Search-agent reinforcement learning further optimizes when and how to search, initially through final-answer rewards and later through search-cost modeling, retrieval simulation, process supervision, and step-wise feedback (Jin et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025b; Shi et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2025b; Ji et al. 2025). Unlike these methods, which mainly optimize solver behavior on given questions, ZEST-Search also brings evidence-grounded task construction into the training loop and explicitly optimizes trajectory-level search efficiency. 

Proposer–Solver Training for Search Agents. More broadly, LLM self-improvement methods generate training data online, with some casting task generation and solving as interacting roles (Wang et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025a; Zhang et al. 2025b; Castanyer et al. 2026). For search-agent training, EvolveSearch, Search Self-Play, and Dr. Zero adapt generated tasks according to solver feedback, allowing task difficulty to evolve during training (Zhang et al. 2025a; Lu et al. 2025; Yue et al. 2026). Recent work further incorporates structured evidence or finer-grained process sig-nals. For example, knowledge-graph paths can guide ques-tion generation and provide waypoint rewards, while source-level contribution scores can assign credit to retrieved evi-dence (Wu et al. 2026; Arai and Ichikawa 2026). In contrast, ZEST-Search generates retrieval-supported target entities and source-supported textual evidence paths online, rather than restricting task construction to fixed answer pools or precomputed graph paths. It also decouples the proposer and solver policies and coordinates their co-evolution through ob-jectives for intermediate task difficulty and search efficiency. 

## Conclusion 

We introduced ZEST-Search, a zero-label framework for tool-efficient search-agent training through heterogeneous proposer–solver co-evolution. Rather than relying on pre-defined answer pools or labeled QA pairs, ZEST-Search constructs validated tasks online through answer-first gen-eration and source-supported multi-hop evidence paths. Lightweight, non-generative components perform target val-idation, path construction, and task filtering without ad-ditional LLM inference. Decoupling the two roles allows lightweight, instruction-tuned, and cross-family proposers to generate complementary tasks around the solver’s evolv-ing capability boundary. Role-specific objectives coordi-nate their co-evolution by steering the proposer toward intermediate-difficulty tasks and rewarding correct solver trajectories with lower relative search costs. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show that ZEST-Search improves answer accuracy and tool efficiency without labeled search-ing data. The framework remains effective with smaller and cross-family proposers, while analyses validate the reward design and task generation. Overall, ZEST-Search provides a scalable approach to expanding the training frontier and optimizing search efficiency. References

B.3 Reward Dynamics 

Reward Dynamics. Figure 5 shows distinct but coupled reward dynamics for the proposer and solver. The solver re-ward rises rapidly during early training and then stabilizes at a high level, indicating that the solver progressively ac-quires and maintains correct, tool-efficient search behavior. In contrast, the proposer reward first increases and then grad-ually decreases to a stable positive range. This decline should not be interpreted as degraded proposer performance. Un-der the intermediate-difficulty objective, questions that are initially informative become easier as the solver improves, reducing their proposer reward and continuously shifting the preferred task distribution toward the solver’s evolving ca-pability boundary. The sustained positive proposer reward suggests that the proposer continues to generate useful fron-tier tasks, while the stable solver reward indicates that this adaptive task generation does not destabilize solver optimiza-tion. Short-term fluctuations arise naturally from stochastic online task generation and grouped rollouts. Since the two roles use different reward definitions, their absolute reward values are not directly comparable; the key observation is their stable and coordinated evolution over training. 

B.4 Comparison with Self-Play Methods. 

A strictly controlled comparison with existing self-play methods is difficult because they differ substantially in task sources, validation mechanisms, and search protocols. Many methods rely on predefined supports, such as answer pools (Lu et al. 2025) or knowledge graphs (Wu et al. 2026), and additionally use LLM-based judging or reasoning to verify the validity and correctness of generated questions and answers. Such verification is sensitive to the choice of judge model, and different LLMs may produce different fil-tering decisions and results. Moreover, these methods did not release trained checkpoints, making it difficult to reeval-uate them under a unified search protocol and evaluation standard. We therefore compare with the reported results of Dr. Zero (Yue et al. 2026), which is closest to our zero-label setting, while treating this comparison as indicative rather than fully controlled. Dr. Zero uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for both roles and allows up to five search turns, whereas ZEST-Search trains a Qwen2.5-7B-Base solver with at most three search turns. Despite using a base rather than instruction-tuned solver and a smaller search budget, our default 3-hop setting improves the average score from 37.2 to 38.2. In-creasing the target path length to four hops further raises the average to 39.3 and outperforms Dr. Zero on all seven bench-marks. As reported in the original paper, Dr. Zero improves over Search-R1 on several individual datasets but achieves a slightly lower average performance. In contrast, our main results demonstrate substantially larger improvements over Search-R1 and ZeroSearch (Sun et al. 2025) without any labeled search data. 

## Appendix C: Lightweight Evidence-Path Construction and Static Question Validation 

C.1 Overview 

This section describes the implementation of the evidence-path guided question construction pipeline used in our train-ing framework. The goal of this module is to automatically construct corpus-supported candidate questions from un-labeled references without invoking additional LLM-based judges or human annotation. Given a reference question, the pipeline first generates a searchable target entity, verifies its existence through dense retrieval, constructs an evidence-supported entity path using lightweight NLP components, and finally generates and validates a new question. Unlike approaches that employ an additional language model or NLI-based verifier to assess generated questions, our validation procedure relies on retrieval grounding, entity-level consistency, and deterministic rules. This design sig-nificantly reduces verification overhead while maintaining reliable control over the generated training distribution. The complete pipeline consists of four stages: (1) tar-get entity generation and retrieval-based verification, (2) lightweight evidence-path construction, (3) evidence-path guided question generation, and (4) static question valida-tion before solver rollout. 

C.2 Retrieval-based Target Verification 

Dense retrieval backbone. All retrieval operations in the evidence construction module use the same dense retrieval service. Specifically, we adopt intfloat/e5-base-v2 

with a pre-built FAISS index over the Wikipedia-2018 cor-pus. Each query is encoded with the required query: pre-fix, truncated to 256 tokens, mean-pooled using the attention mask, and ℓ2 normalized before nearest-neighbor search. For target verification and evidence expansion, the re-triever returns the top-8 passages. This setting is independent from the solver search environment, where each search action retrieves top-3 passages. The retrieval results are only used as corpus grounding signals and do not directly determine answer correctness. 

Target entity generation. The first proposer call does not answer the reference question. Instead, it is instructed to in-fer the semantic type of the expected answer and generate a different searchable entity with a similar semantic category. For example, if the reference question expects a person, or-ganization, location, or work, the proposer generates another entity belonging to the same category. For questions whose answers are numerical or temporal values, the proposer iden-tifies the corresponding entity or topic that determines such values and uses it as the target anchor. The generated target a⋆ must satisfy two requirements: 1. it should represent a concrete searchable entity; 2. it should be supported by the retrieval corpus. Before retrieval verification, candidates undergo determin-istic surface filtering to remove malformed outputs, includ-ing empty strings, placeholders, non-entity values, URLs, question-like generations, and residual generation tags. 

Retrieval support verification. A candidate is considered retrieval-supported if its normalized form satisfies at least one of the following conditions: (i) it exactly matches a re-trieved title; (ii) it and a retrieved title are mutually contained, with both normalized strings containing at least four charac-ters; (iii) its token-set Jaccard similarity with a retrieved title is at least 0.8; or (iv) it contains at most six tokens and appears within the first 1,200 characters of a retrieved passage. This stage only verifies that the target entity has corpus support. It does not perform entity linking, factual verifica-tion, or relation verification. 

C.3 Evidence Path Construction 

After obtaining a retrieval-supported target entity a⋆, we con-struct a lightweight evidence graph from retrieved passages. The objective is not to build a complete knowledge graph, but to extract local evidence chains that provide controllable generation context for the proposer. The evidence graph is represented as: 

G = ( V, E ),

where each node v ∈ V corresponds to a normalized entity mention and each edge e ∈ E denotes a sentence-level entity association supported by co-mention and lexical cues. Each edge stores its source passage, verbatim supporting sentence, entity endpoints, and lexical support indicators. 

Entity Extraction and Normalization Entity extraction combines lightweight NLP tools and deterministic rules. When spaCy NER is available, we retain mentions labeled 

PERSON , ORG , GPE , LOC , FAC , WORK_OF_ART , EVENT ,and PRODUCT . Numeric and temporal mentions are removed because they are less suitable for constructing stable entity-centered paths. When a document title appears in a sentence, it is treated as a high-confidence entity mention. A capitalization-based extractor is used only as a fallback when no valid NER entity is detected. Unlike external entity-linking systems such as Wikidata-based linking, our method performs local within-corpus nor-malization. Entity mentions are normalized by Unicode nor-malization, case folding, punctuation removal, and surface matching. Each entity receives a deterministic identifier de-rived from its canonical representation. This lightweight design avoids dependency on external knowledge bases while providing sufficient consistency for evidence-path construction. 

Evidence Edge Construction Starting from the target en-tity a⋆, retrieved passages are segmented into sentences. If a sentence contains both a⋆ and another valid entity v, we construct a directed edge: 

v → a⋆.

The edge direction follows the reverse traversal order of question construction, where the final node is always the tar-get answer. Each edge preserves the original evidence sen-tence and its document metadata. During expansion, the cur-rent frontier entity is used as a new retrieval query. Newly discovered entities form additional edges: 

vi → vi+1 .

To avoid shortcut paths, documents containing the final answer entity are excluded after the initial seed retrieval stage.

Path Sampling and Certification The evidence-path builder expands the graph iteratively from the target entity. Instead of exhaustively enumerating all possible paths, which can lead to combinatorial growth, we adopt a lightweight sampling-based expansion strategy. At each expansion step, candidate entities are ranked ac-cording to the edge quality score. Among the top-8 can-didates, the highest-ranked candidate is always preserved, while one additional candidate is randomly sampled with a fixed training-step-dependent seed. Therefore, the default branching width is two. With a maximum hop budget of three, each target produces at most 23 = 8 candidate paths before certification. The hop budget is treated as an upper bound rather than a fixed requirement. When no reliable frontier entity can be expanded, the current shorter path is retained. This allows the system to avoid introducing unsupported relations while maintaining sufficient generation coverage. Each candidate path is represented as: 

P = ( v0, e 1, v 1, . . . , e h, v h),

where vh = a⋆ is the target answer entity. If no supported edge is found, the retrieved answer passages form an answer-only grounding context. If no can-didate passes the structural checker, the highest-ranked can-didate may still be retained as an explicitly logged uncertified fallback. Accordingly, context availability should not be con-flated with successful path certification. Importantly, path certification only measures structural and lexical consistency with retrieved corpus evidence. It does not perform external fact checking, natural language in-ference, or LLM-based judgment. Therefore, certified paths should be interpreted as retrieval-supported generation con-texts rather than formally verified knowledge representations. 

C.4 Evidence-path Guided Question Generation 

After path construction, one evidence path is selected for each target entity and provided to the proposer for question generation. The input context contains: 

• the target answer entity a⋆;

• the ordered entity chain from the outer entity to the target; 

• intermediate bridge entities; 

• supporting evidence sentences; 

• source document information and relation indicators. Conditioned on the target entity, selected path, and sup-porting documents, the proposer generates a question whose answer is expected to be exactly a⋆. The generation objec-tive is to produce a self-contained question whose answer is exactly a⋆, while avoiding direct leakage of the answer en-tity. The proposer is encouraged to utilize multiple evidence steps when supported by the context, but shorter questions are allowed when a shorter composition provides clearer and more reliable evidence. The generation prompt specifies two desiderata: the ques-tion should be consistent with the supplied evidence context, and the target answer should not appear explicitly in the ques-tion. The deterministic validator directly enforces the second requirement through normalized alias matching. The first is encouraged through evidence-conditioned generation but is not separately verified by an entailment model or an LLM judge. The complete evidence path is therefore used as a con-trollable generation signal rather than merely as additional retrieval information. 

C.5 Static Question Validation 

Before entering solver rollout, generated questions undergo a deterministic validation procedure. The purpose of this stage is to remove malformed generations and prevent trivial answer leakage. C.6 Computational Efficiency 

The evidence construction and validation stages introduce no additional LLM inference beyond the two proposer calls used for target and question generation. As quantified in Table 7, evidence-path construction requires 59.24 seconds over 500 reference questions, compared with 142.53 seconds for target generation and validation and 111.15 seconds for question generation and static verification. Thus, the non-generative evidence stage contributes substantially less wall-clock time than either LLM-generation stage while preserving explicit evidence provenance for each accepted candidate. Static acceptance is deliberately separated from solver-based evaluation. The former removes malformed candidates and direct answer leakage before rollout, whereas the latter supplies dynamic correctness, difficulty, and tool-use signals only after acceptance. This ordering avoids spending solver rollouts on candidates that already violate inexpensive deter-ministic constraints.
