Abstract 

LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) rely on collaboration among specialized agents to solve complex tasks. Existing methods im-prove MAS by refining the prompts, skills, and execution strategies of individual agents based on task feedback. However, improving individ-ual agents does not necessarily lead to better team collaboration. Recent studies have there-fore extended evolution to the collaboration level, using collective execution experience to adjust team organization, and inter-agent in-teractions. Nevertheless, evolving collabora-tion across heterogeneous tasks remains chal-lenging, as existing approaches often constrain team reorganization within a shared structure and reuse interaction rules across incompati-ble team contexts. In this paper, we propose EVO TEAM , a collaboration-centric evolution framework that jointly evolves team organi-zation, interaction rules, and individual agent skills through execution experience. It main-tains a library of reusable teams, selecting or creating agent teams and guiding collaboration through team-specific interaction rules. Col-laboration experience accumulates within com-patible team contexts, while individual skills remain reusable across teams. Experiments across four benchmarks demonstrate its effec-tiveness. Our code is available at https: //github.com/DEEP-JLU/EvoTeam .

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM)-based multi-agent systems (MAS) organize specialized agents into collaborative teams to effectively combine their complementary capabilities and accomplish com-plex tasks that require diverse expertise, sustained reasoning, and extensive tool interactions (Guo et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Such collaboration has enabled applications rang-ing from information retrieval and software engi-

As these applications expand, enabling MAS to con-tinuously improve through accumulated execution experience has become an important research di-rection (Hao et al., 2026b; Lin et al., 2026). To enable such continual improvement, existing research has explored experience-driven evolution in multi-agent systems. A prominent direction fo-cuses on improving the capabilities of individual agents through accumulated execution experience. By learning from previous task trajectories and feedback, agents can refine their prompts, acquire reusable skills, and improve their reasoning and tool-use strategies (Hao et al., 2026b; Zheng et al., 2025). These improvements can be retained across tasks, allowing individual agents to progressively strengthen their task-solving capabilities (Li et al., 2025). Such experience-driven approaches provide an important foundation for MAS evolution by en-abling agents to learn from previous executions rather than repeatedly solving tasks from scratch. However, evolving better individual agents does not necessarily produce better collaborative teams. Multi-agent collaboration depends on how comple-mentary capabilities are organized and coordinated toward a shared objective (Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026). Even when agents possess necessary expertise, inappropriate responsibility assignments may prevent their capabilities from being effec-tively utilized, while incomplete information hand-offs or insufficient verification may undermine cor-rect individual contributions (Cemri et al., 2025). Effective MAS evolution must extend beyond im-proving what individual agents can do to improving how they organize, communicate, and collaborate. Recent studies have begun to extend experience-driven evolution from individual agents to multi-agent collaboration. However, dif-ferent tasks may require distinct combinations of agents, divisions of responsibility, and interaction rule (Yue et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2026). Collaboration patterns learned from previous executions may not remain appropriate as task requirements change. This raises a critical question: how can MAS continuously evolve col-laboration while adapting to heterogeneous tasks and preserving useful experience? 

Despite recent advances, existing approaches face two critical limitations in collaboration evolu-tion. (i) Constrained team reorganization. Exist-ing approaches often adjust agent membership and responsibilities within a shared team, limiting their ability to maintain distinct collaboration patterns for tasks with different requirements. (ii) Inappro-priate reuse of collaboration experience. Inter-action rules learned from previous tasks are often tied to specific agent responsibilities and informa-tion dependencies. Reusing or updating these rules across incompatible collaboration contexts may in-troduce unsuitable handoffs or stopping conditions. Our preliminary study in Fig 2 further illustrates these difficulties: continued evolution across het-erogeneous tasks can reduce task performance rela-tive to target-only evolution, while representative execution trajectories reveal mismatches in team organization and interaction rule applicability. In this paper, we propose EVO TEAM , acollaboration-centric evolution framework that jointly evolves team organization, interaction rules, and individual agent skills through execution ex-perience. E VO TEAM organizes collaboration ex-perience around task-appropriate teams, allowing compatible tasks to reuse and refine interaction rules while preserving individual agent skills across teams. Specifically, E VO TEAM consists of three key stages. (i) Adaptive Agent Team Composi-tion maintains a library of agent teams, selecting a compatible team or creating a new one according to task requirements. (ii) Team-Scoped Collab-oration coordinates task execution through task-specific responsibility assignments and applicable team-specific interaction rules, allowing tasks with compatible collaboration requirements to reuse ac-cumulated coordination experience while keeping rule updates independent across teams. (iii) Multi-Level Experience Evolution leverages execution traces and task feedback to refine team organiza-tion, update team-specific interaction rules, and im-prove individual agent skills. While collaboration experience evolves within corresponding teams, individual skills remain reusable across team con-texts. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify interconnected challenges on collaboration-centric multi-agent evolution and propose E VO TEAM , a framework that maintains and evolves reusable teams and their interaction rules while preserving indi-vidual agent skills across team contexts. • E VO TEAM consists three key components that enables team organization and coordination rules to improve jointly through accumulated execution experience. • We conduct experiments across four bench-marks and six task categories, demonstrat-ing the effectiveness of E VO TEAM under individual-benchmark and mixed-benchmark evolution. Ablation studies, cross-backbone evaluations, and case analyses examine its col-laboration mechanisms and experience reuse.

2.3 Discussion 

Our preliminary study suggests that retaining prior experience alone is insufficient for effective MAS evolution across heterogeneous tasks. Although accumulated agent skills may remain useful across team contexts, reusing the original team template and interaction rules without accounting for new task requirements can constrain subsequent evolu-tion. (i) Team adaptation should maintain dis-tinct collaboration structures. Selecting or creat-ing task-appropriate teams enables reorganization to meet changing collaboration requirements. (ii) Interaction rules should be shared and refined only among tasks with compatible collaboration requirements. A clear reuse scope helps prevent rule updates for one task type from disrupting the coordination required by others. Guided by these principles, we develop E VO TEAM to support the accumulation and selective reuse of collaboration experience across heterogeneous tasks. 

3 The Framework of E VO TEAM 

Our preliminary study indicates that experience reuse across heterogeneous tasks requires flexible team formation and selective reuse of interaction rules. Motivated by these requirements, we develop EVO TEAM , a framework that selects or creates task-appropriate agent teams to reorganize agents and evolves interaction rules within each team. Figure 3 summarizes its three stages per task. (i) Adaptive Agent Team Composition: EVO TEAM selects or creates a task-appropriate agent team and recruits agents from its candidate roster. (ii) Team-Scoped Collaboration: Agents use accumulated skills to complete subtasks and consult the selected agent team’s interaction rules. (iii) Multi-Level Experi-ence Evolution: Execution evidence refines agent teams, updates team-specific interaction rules, and evolves agent skills for reuse across agent teams.

EVO TEAM maintains team-specific interaction rule sets to narrow the scope of reuse. The selected agent team Tq determines the available interaction rule set RTq , while recruited agents assess the ap-plicability of individual rules to their current co-ordination needs. 4 Experiments 

In this section, we conduct experiments to an-swer four questions: Q1 (Overall Effectiveness): 

How does E VO TEAM perform against existing MAS methods across benchmarks under individual-benchmark and mixed-benchmark evolution? Q2 (Cross-Backbone Transfer): Do evolved teams and interaction rules remain beneficial when evo-lution and evaluation LLMs differ? Q3 (Ablation Study): How do team organization and interaction rules affect E VO TEAM ’s performance? Q4 (Cost Efficiency): How does E VO TEAM compare with existing MAS methods in performance and cost? 

4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We use GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024) (multi-step web-based reasoning and retrieval), Lo-CoBench (Qiu et al., 2025) (long-context code im-plementation and cross-file refactoring), LOCA-Bench (Zeng et al., 2026) (stateful application in-teraction via MCP), and BeyondSWE (Chen et al., 2026) (repository-level software maintenance). Im-plementation details are in Appendix B.1. 

Evaluation Metrics. We report GAIA answer accuracy, LOCA-Bench final-state success, Be-yondSWE resolution rate (required tests pass with-out regressions), and LoCoBench Score (LCBS; functional correctness and software quality) on a common [0 , 100] scale. Detailed metric definitions are provided in Appendix B.2. 

Baselines. Baselines include hand-crafted SA (ReAct) (Yao et al., 2023), non-evolutionary MAS, and OWL (Hu et al., 2025a); performance-driven search via AgentSquare (Shang et al., 2025); and experience-driven ReCreate (Hao et al., 2026b), Meta-Team (Hao et al., 2026c), and Skill-MAS (Lin et al., 2026). Baseline details are pro-vided in Appendix B.3. 

Evolution and Evaluation Setup. Individual-benchmark evolution optimizes AgentSquare, the experience-driven baselines, and E VO TEAM on each benchmark/subset’s evolution split; SA, MAS, and OWL run without prior evolution. Mixed-benchmark evolution uses a shuffled interleaved sequence combining all four evolution splits for the experience-driven baselines and E VO TEAM ,each using a consistent configuration. Evaluation uses disjoint held-out splits. By default, all experi-ments use GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026); avg@3 averages three independent rollout scores per test instance. Detailed data partitions and evolution protocols are provided in Appendix B.4.

4.3 Cross-Backbone Transfer (Q2) 

To address Q2, we examine whether E VO TEAM ’s evolved team and interaction rules can be reused across LLM backbones. We use DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GPT-5.6-Luna as the evolution and eval-uation LLMs, and evaluate GAIA and the two Lo-CoBench tasks. For each configuration, we com-pare E VO TEAM with the unevolved MAS baseline using the same evaluation LLM. Table 2 reports the same- and cross-backbone evaluation results. We summarize two observations below. 

Obs. 3. Evolved collaboration transfers across backbones in both directions. When evolution and evaluation use different LLMs, E VO TEAM con-sistently outperforms MAS in all six configurations. On GAIA, both backbones benefit from artifacts evolved by the other model. The gains reach 5.00 and 18.00 points for GPT-5.6-Luna and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash respectively. Both LoCoBench tasks also improve in each transfer direction, showing that the effect extends across task settings. These bidirectional gains demonstrate that the collabora-tion experience accumulated through E VO TEAM ’s team-indexed evolution transfers effectively be-tween the two evaluated LLM backbones. 

Obs. 4. Cross-backbone transfer retains perfor-mance close to the same-backbone setting. For each evaluation task, we hold the evaluation LLM fixed and vary only the LLM used to evolve the col-laboration artifacts. Across all six paired compar-isons, cross-backbone performance remains within 4.00 points of the corresponding same-backbone performance. On GAIA, cross-backbone transfer achieves 77.00 with GPT-5.6-Luna, compared with 78.00 under same-backbone evolution. The two LoCoBench tasks also show similarly small perfor-mance gaps for both evaluation backbones. This ob-served stability highlights E VO TEAM ’s robustness to changes in the evolution LLM and shows that its team-indexed collaboration experience remains effective across different backbone configurations.

5 Related Work 

Individual Agent Evolution. Experience-driven approaches improve agent capabilities through scaf-fold revision, skill acquisition, and cross-task ex-perience retrieval (Hao et al., 2026b; Zheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). By retaining reusable knowl-edge from previous task executions, agents progres-sively improve their reasoning and tool use. How-ever, individual improvement does not necessarily resolve problems in responsibility assignment and information exchange during collaboration. 

Collaborative Multi-Agent Evolution. Meta-Team uses collective execution experience to evolve agent behaviors, inter-agent coordination, and team organization (Hao et al., 2026c). Skill-MAS refines orchestration knowledge through rollout and reflection, while EMAS validates prompt and topology revisions using execution ev-idence (Lin et al., 2026; Fei et al., 2026). Our work focuses on sustaining collaboration evolution across heterogeneous tasks. E VO TEAM maintains and evolves reusable teams with separate interac-tion rules, allowing coordination experience to ac-cumulate within compatible team contexts while individual skills remain reusable across teams. Fur-ther details are provided in Appendix F. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented E VO TEAM , a frame-work for continual collaboration evolution across heterogeneous tasks. It maintains a library of reusable teams and jointly evolves their organiza-tion, team-specific interaction rules, and individual agent skills, accumulating coordination experience within compatible team contexts while preserving skill reuse across teams. Across four benchmarks and six task categories, E VO TEAM leads all six metrics under mixed-benchmark evolution, averag-ing 59.58 versus 55.55 for the strongest baseline. Cross-backbone evaluations and ablations further support the transferability of evolved collaboration experience and the value of jointly evolving team organization and coordination practices. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation does not systematically examine collaborative evolution in multimodal interactive environments. In such settings, agents need to interpret visual observations, communicate rele-vant evidence, and coordinate actions as the envi-ronment changes. Further investigation is needed into how multimodal execution traces can guide the refinement of team organization, interaction rules, and agent skills. Future work could extend EVO TEAM to these settings and evaluate how accu-mulated collaboration experience supports tasks in-volving visual perception and interaction. Addition-ally, E VO TEAM accumulates experience through explicit updates while keeping the LLM parameters fixed. The effective use of this experience depends on the base model’s ability to correctly interpret and apply the stored guidance. Combining experi-ence reuse with model adaptation could help inter-nalize frequently useful coordination practices. 

Ethical Considerations 

EVO TEAM aims to improve multi-agent collabora-tion through reusable teams and accumulated ex-ecution experience. Like other LLM-based agent systems, it may inherit biases from underlying mod-els and produce inaccurate outputs or unintended tool actions. Persistent experience accumulation also requires attention to sensitive information re-tained in execution traces and reusable knowledge. Applications beyond the evaluated benchmarks should therefore incorporate appropriate access controls, privacy protection, and human oversight for consequential actions. Accumulated interaction rules and agent skills should be reviewed for their suitability before reuse in new contexts. The use of underlying models, datasets, and tools should re-spect their respective licenses and usage policies.

11 A Algorithms 

Algorithm 1 summarizes the sequential evolution procedure of E VO TEAM , following the notation in Section 3. The framework maintains a global agent pool A, a library of reusable agent teams T , and team-specific interaction-rule sets {R T }T ∈T . For each evolution query, it selects a compatible agent team or creates a provisional one with an empty rule set. The Chairman recruits an execution team from its candidate roster and assigns subtasks. The selected team’s rules are injected into the recruited agents’ contexts as soft coordination guidance. EXECUTE includes subtask execution using ac-cumulated agent skills, peer coordination, native task evaluation, and trace collection. Agents select applicable rules according to their descriptions and current coordination requirements. The Chairman monitors progress and adjusts πq by reassigning subtasks within Eq when needed. The resulting composite trace contains individual agent traces and the corresponding inter-agent handoff trace. After execution, the three reflectors update the agent team, its interaction rules, and individual agent skills. Team reflection revises the candi-date roster when needed and updates the team de-scription accordingly. For a provisional team, the same reflection pass determines admission based on whether its roster supports the required division of work and its organization is reusable. Existing teams retain their identities when revised. Rules are retained with their associated teams, while up-dated skills remain with the corresponding agents regardless of provisional-team admission. After the final query, all persistent updates are frozen for evaluation on the disjoint holdout split.

(i) GAIA. (Mialon et al., 2024) evaluates general-purpose assistants on real-world questions requir-ing reasoning, web browsing, tool use, and, for some questions, multimodal understanding. Agents can read and write files, run shell commands, search online, and fetch webpages.

Environment-description length measures the tokenized information avail-able through the task environment’s tool outputs; it is distinct from the model’s context-window limit. The evolution and holdout configurations are disjoint in both environment seeds and description lengths, testing cross-seed and cross-scale generalization for the selected task types. Each case runs with iso-lated local mock MCP services and is scored by the corresponding native environment-state evaluator. 

(iv) BeyondSWE. (Chen et al., 2026) evaluates software engineering beyond single-repository bug fixing. Its 500 instances are drawn from 246 real-world GitHub repositories and cover cross-repository issue resolution, domain-specific fixes, dependency migration, and repository generation from specifications. We use the CrossRepo and DepMigrate tracks. CrossRepo requires reasoning with information from related external reposito-ries, while DepMigrate requires repository-wide changes to accommodate breaking dependency up-dates. Following Meta-Team, we randomly select 20 instances per track for evolution and hold out the rest: CrossRepo has 200 instances (20 evolu-tion, 180 holdout) and DepMigrate has 178 (20 evolution, 158 holdout). Each track is evolved sep-arately and evaluated on its own holdout split. Each instance runs in an isolated repository environment, and submitted changes are evaluated with native Fail-to-Pass (F2P) and Pass-to-Pass (P2P) tests.

A shared system evolves se-quentially over the mixed task stream and is frozen before evaluating the holdout groups. Each task retains its source benchmark’s tools, environment, output contract, and evaluator. Results are reported using the respective source-native evaluation met-rics for each of the corresponding holdout groups. 

B.2 Evaluation Metrics 

We follow each benchmark’s native evaluation protocol. All aggregate scores are reported on a 

[0 , 100] scale, with higher values indicating better overall performance across different benchmarks. 

(i) GAIA. We report answer accuracy using the official evaluator (Mialon et al., 2024). A predic-tion is correct only if the complete final answer matches the reference under the evaluator’s nor-malization rules. These rules handle differences in capitalization, whitespace, punctuation, and nu-meric formatting as appropriate to the answer type. For list-valued answers, the number of elements and all corresponding elements must match. Both semantically similar but unmatched answers and partially correct answers are scored as incorrect. 

(ii) LOCA-Bench. We report task success rate under the official execution-based evaluation pro-tocol (Zeng et al., 2026). Task-specific evaluation scripts check whether the final environment state satisfies the required outcomes, such as correctly updated records or generated artifacts. Each run receives a binary score: one if the task passes the evaluator and zero otherwise. Success therefore depends on the resulting environment state, rather than the agent’s mere textual claim of completion.

F2P tests verify that the target issue has been addressed, whereas P2P tests check that previously working functionality remains in-tact. Passing only a subset of the specified required tests cannot be counted as a resolved instance.

B.3 Baseline Details 

We compare our method with the following seven baselines. All these methods use GPT-5.6-Luna for task execution and, where applicable, for re-flection and optimization. They share the same benchmark-specific tool access, resource budgets, and evolution/holdout splits as E VO TEAM .

(i) SA uses a single agent with a ReAct-style work-flow (Yao et al., 2023), alternating between reason-ing and tool use to solve each given task, without involving any form of experience-driven evolution. 

(ii) MAS uses a multi-agent team with the same collaboration structure as ours. The team config-uration remains unchanged across tasks, with no experience-driven evolution. MAS is evaluated di-rectly and independently on the holdout split. 

(iii) OWL (Hu et al., 2025a) organizes task exe-cution through a Planner–Coordinator–Worker hi-erarchy. We use the official implementation with its original structure under the common backbone, tool-access, and budget settings. OWL does not perform experience-driven evolution and is evalu-ated directly on the corresponding holdout split. 

(iv) AgentSquare (Shang et al., 2025) searches a modular agent design space comprising planning, reasoning, tool use, and memory. We use its offi-cial implementation and released search configura-tion, with the candidate scaffold pool adopted from Meta-Team. Search is conducted on the designated evolution split, and the selected configuration is kept fixed for the subsequent holdout evaluation. 

(v) Skill-MAS (Lin et al., 2026) evolves a meta-skill that guides task decomposition, agent con-struction, and workflow orchestration. It combines multiple trajectory rollouts with selective reflection to distill reusable orchestration principles from exe-cution experience. We use the official implementa-tion with k = 2 , sampling two separate trajectories per task in each evolution round. Evolution uses only the designated evolution split, and the evolved meta-skill is frozen before holdout evaluation. 

(vi) ReCreate (Hao et al., 2026b) is an experience-driven framework for optimizing agent scaffolds. Its agent-as-optimizer mechanism inspects execu-tion trajectories, submitted artifacts, and evaluation feedback to propose changes to prompts, tools, and memory. Instance-level proposals are synthesized into reusable updates to the shared scaffold. We use the official implementation under the common model, tool-access, and budget settings. D.1 Team Selection 

Prompt 1 (Figure 9) determines whether an incom-ing task can reuse an existing agent team or requires a cold start. The decision is based on the task’s spe-cific collaboration requirements, complementary agent roles, and verification needs, rather than topic similarity alone. This stage selects the collabora-tion structure while leaving individual agent recruit-ment to the Chairman. When no existing team is suitable, it produces a provisional team description for subsequent team composition; admission to the team library is decided after task execution.

Recruitment and work assignments remain Chair-man’s responsibility; this prompt does not prescribe an execution order or generate interaction rules. 17 Prompt 1: Team Selection

F Extended Related Work 

F.1 Individual Agent Evolution 

Research on agent improvement explores how feed-back can refine task-solving behavior and pro-duce reusable knowledge. Early approaches es-tablish feedback-based refinement through itera-tive output revision and reflection-guided repeated attempts, with different scopes of experience re-tention (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023). Self-Refine improves generated outputs through self-generated feedback, while Reflexion retains verbal reflections in episodic memory to guide sub-sequent trials. ExpeL extends experience reuse across tasks by extracting natural-language insights from past executions and recalling these insights alongside the relevant previous experiences during subsequent task solving (Zhao et al., 2024). Beyond response-level refinement, automated agent optimization extends to code-defined de-signs, functional modules, and symbolic com-ponents such as prompts and tools (Hu et al., 2025b; Shang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024). ADAS uses a meta-agent to propose new agent designs based on an archive of previous discover-ies, while AgentSquare evolves and recombines reusable planning, reasoning, tool-use, and mem-ory modules. Agent Symbolic Learning further optimizes prompts, tools, and their overall com-position through natural-language feedback and symbolic updates to these components. Another line of work converts interaction expe-rience into reusable procedural knowledge and re-vised execution scaffolds (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2026b). Voyager accumu-lates executable skills through open-ended environ-ment interaction, while SkillWeaver enables web agents to discover and practice skills, distilling in-teraction experience into reusable APIs. ReCre-ate reasons over interaction histories to identify causes of success and failure, translating execu-tion experience into scaffold revisions and reusable domain patterns. Mem 2Evolve connects experi-ence accumulation with capability expansion by using experience memory to guide the creation of reusable tools and expert agents (Cheng et al., 2026). Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) instead maintains evolving context playbooks through gen-eration, reflection, and curation, using incremental updates to preserve useful knowledge across exe-cutions (Zhang et al., 2026). Within multi-agent collaboration, MAEL maintains individual experi-ence pools and retrieves high-reward, task-relevant examples to improve agents’ subsequent reasoning steps (Li et al., 2025). Together, these studies im-prove agent designs and retain reflections, skills, and execution knowledge beyond a single response. However, analyses of multi-agent failures and scal-ing behavior show that collaborative performance also depends on inter-agent alignment and the com-patibility between coordination structures and task requirements (Cemri et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026). This leaves a distinct question for collaborative evolution: how should accumulated capabilities be organized and coordinated when tasks require different responsibilities and information handoffs? 

F.2 Collaborative Multi-Agent Evolution 

At the collaboration level, improvement extends to interactions and the structures connecting agent be-haviors. Studies of large-scale collaboration high-light the role of interaction topology in shaping collective performance (Qian et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026). Related approaches adjust team com-position and agent participation to improve task solving, including dynamic group composition, contribution-based agent selection, and elimination of redundant agents and interaction (Chen et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Liang et al., 2022). These approaches provide mecha-26 nisms for adapting collaboration to a task’s require-ments. Automated optimization further treats agent prompts, graph connectivity, and executable work-flows as searchable components (Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025c). GPTSwarm optimizes node-level prompts and graph connectivity, MASS interleaves prompt and topology optimization, and AFlow searches over code-represented workflows using execution feed-back.EvoMAC uses textual backpropagation from environmental feedback to revise multi-agent col-laboration networks, while EvoFlow evolves a population of diverse workflows through retrieval, crossover, and mutation (Hu et al., 2025c; Zhang et al., 2025a). ScoreFlow extends workflow op-timization through score-based preference learn-ing, while EvoAgent uses evolutionary opera-tors to generate diverse agents from an initial agent framework (Wang et al., 2025a; Yuan et al., 2025). To accommodate varying task require-ments, query-dependent approaches learn to se-lect or generate collaboration architectures, agent roles, and model assignments according to the in-put query (Zhang et al., 2025b; Gao et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025). These methods make collabo-ration structures explicit optimization targets and support task-dependent system construction. Experience-driven approaches investigate how execution knowledge can support subsequent col-laboration, including retrieval-based agent mem-ory, collective reflection, and reusable orchestration knowledge (Yang et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2026c; Lin et al., 2026). AgentNet combines retrieval-based memory with decentralized coordination, adapting connectivity and task routing according to local expertise and context. DecentMem gives each agent separate memory pools for consoli-dated experience and generated exploratory can-didates, adjusting their relative weights through online feedback (Hao et al., 2026a). Meta-Team preserves individual execution contexts and enables post-task evidence exchange, supporting evolution of agent behaviors, inter-agent coordination, and team organization. Skill-based approaches con-nect reusable knowledge with changes to multi-agent collaboration: SkillMAS couples skill evo-lution with evidence-guided MAS restructuring, while Skill-MAS represents orchestration knowl-edge as an evolvable Meta-Skill refined through multi-trajectory rollout and selective reflection (Pan et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026). EMAS uses recurring execution diagnoses to propose prompt and topol-ogy revisions, accepting updates through paired val-idation against the current system (Fei et al., 2026). MASFly and MANTA combine prior experience with execution feedback to support task-specific collaboration structures and execution-time recon-figuration (Liu et al., 2026; xun Huang et al., 2026). These studies demonstrate several ways to im-prove collaboration through structural optimization and experience reuse. (Feng et al., 2026) Our work focuses on sustaining such improvement across het-erogeneous task requirements, where coordination knowledge must remain appropriate to the team using it. E VO TEAM maintains a library of reusable teams, selecting or creating teams according to collaboration requirements. It jointly refines team organization and associated interaction rules, allow-ing compatible tasks to accumulate coordination experience within the same team while keeping rule updates independent across teams. Individ-ual agent skills remain reusable across these team contexts. 

G Frequently Asked Questions 

Q1. Is EvoTeam only a task-dependent team-selection method? A. Team selection is the entry point to a broader process of collaboration evolu-tion. A reusable team specifies supported collab-oration requirements and a candidate roster, from which the Chairman recruits a task-specific exe-cution team. Tasks can therefore reuse the same team while activating different members and as-signing different responsibilities. Beyond selec-tion, E VO TEAM uses execution experience to refine team organization, associated interaction rules, and individual agent skills. The reusable team provides a persistent context in which tasks with compatible collaboration requirements accumulate and refine coordination experience (Section 3; Algorithm 1). 

Q2. Are teams organized by benchmark or task category? A. Teams are organized according to collaboration requirements, rather than predefined benchmark or task-category boundaries. The LLM-based Team Selector assesses whether a team’s de-scription and candidate roster support the required division of work, complementary roles, and verifi-cation needs. Topic similarity alone is insufficient to justify reuse. Consequently, tasks from different benchmarks can reuse the same team when their collaboration requirements are compatible, as illus-trated in Appendix E.3. When no compatible team 27 exists during evolution, the Composer constructs a provisional team from the global agent pool. Post-execution reflection determines its admission based on whether the roster supports the required collab-oration and the resulting organization is reusable (Section 3.1). 

Q3. Do interaction rules impose a fixed execu-tion workflow? A. Interaction rules provide soft coordination guidance about the information and evidence required at a handoff. They do not pre-scribe a complete execution sequence. The Chair-man establishes task-specific assignments and can reassign subtasks when progress is blocked. For each handoff, the sending agent assesses rule appli-cability using the sender–recipient pair, the rule’s trigger, and the current information-transfer re-quirements. When a rule applies, the agent adapts its message to the current task while following the relevant information and verification requirements. If no rule applies, the agent communicates directly without imposing an unrelated rule (Section 3.2; Appendix D.4). 

Q4. Why are interaction rules team-specific while agent skills remain reusable across teams? A. Interaction rules depend on how responsibilities and information dependencies are organized within a team. A handoff requirement that supports one collaboration pattern may be inappropriate for an-other. Individual skills instead encode procedures for an agent’s own execution and can remain useful across teams when their applicability conditions are satisfied. E VO TEAM therefore updates coordi-nation knowledge within the corresponding team while retaining skills with the agents that use them. This separation allows compatible tasks to share and refine interaction rules without directly modi-fying other teams’ rule sets. The isolation applies to team-specific rules; individual skills and recruit-ment profiles remain shared across team contexts (Section 3.3; Appendix E.1). 

Q5. What evolves, and does evolution continue during holdout evaluation? A. Evolution up-dates three forms of persistent knowledge: team candidate rosters and their corresponding descrip-tions, team-specific interaction rules, and individ-ual agent guidance. The latter includes prompt patches and reusable skills in our implementation. The underlying LLM parameters remain fixed. Per-sistent updates are performed on the designated evolution split and frozen before evaluation on the disjoint holdout split. Task-specific recruitment, planning, rule application, and reassignment re-main part of execution. Thus, freezing evolution prevents holdout tasks from changing the persistent knowledge used by subsequent evaluation tasks, while preserving adaptation within each task (Al-gorithm 1; Appendices B.4 and D.7). 

Q6. Does the team library support cross-benchmark reuse, or merely isolate experience for individual tasks? A. Teams are organized by collaboration requirements rather than task iden-tities or benchmark labels. Appendix E.2 follows one team across four LoCoBench and BeyondSWE tasks, including accumulated rule updates and candidate-roster expansion. Appendix E.3 further shows a shared team reused for holdout tasks from three benchmarks. Together, these cases illustrate how compatible tasks across benchmarks accumu-late and reuse coordination experience within a shared collaboration structure. 

Q7. How do the ablation studies and case analy-ses support team-specific interaction rules? A. 

The ablation studies show that learned interac-tion rules contribute to performance beyond task-conditioned team organization. Appendix E.1 fur-ther illustrates how task-derived coordination ex-perience updates the selected team’s rules while leaving other teams’ rules unchanged. These analy-ses provide complementary evidence: the ablations assess the value of learned coordination guidance, while the case analysis illustrates how team-scoped evolution incorporates new experience while pre-serving interaction rules associated with other col-laboration structures. 

Q8. What do the reported comparisons show about EvoTeam’s performance–cost trade-off? A. Figure 5 and Figure 7 report task-level performance–cost comparisons in the eval-uated settings. Under mixed-benchmark evolution, EVO TEAM achieves stronger performance at lower reported per-task cost than Meta-Team and ReCre-ate on GAIA and BeyondSWE DepMig. These results show that E VO TEAM combines strong task performance with competitive per-task costs, sup-porting its practical effectiveness across different collaboration requirements. 28
