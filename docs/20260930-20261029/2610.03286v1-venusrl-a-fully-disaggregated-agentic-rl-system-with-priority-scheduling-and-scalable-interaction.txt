## Abstract 

Agentic Reinforcement Learning (RL) trains LLM agents through multi-turn interactions with external tool environ-ments. Its multi-turn nature exposes two systems-level bot-tlenecks unaddressed by existing agentic RL frameworks. First, end-to-end training throughput is constrained by the slowest trajectories to complete, yet optimizing per-GPU uti-lization alone scatters rollout progress across many groups, delaying the completion of enough groups to unblock the next training step. Second, tool sandboxes are statically over-provisioned by their declared memory ceilings, leaving most physical memory stranded while replicating near-identical state across sandboxes launched from the same prompt. We present VenusRL, a fully disaggregated agentic RL system that addresses both bottlenecks. VenusRL’s priority-aware action scheduler uses length-prediction heuristics to iden-tify sample groups whose completion is most likely to un-block the next training step, and pushes them ahead of others across batch admission, KV Cache residency, and cross-worker request orchestration. VenusRL’s environment resource manager combines a memory-aware admission threshold with a template-keyed page-sharing pool, packing more sandboxes per node while preserving strict memory isolation via write-protected page table entry aliasing and copy-on-write. Across representative agentic RL workloads, VenusRL achieves up to 4.24× end-to-end training speedup over state-of-the-art baselines and reduces environment cost by up to 89%. 

## 1 Introduction 

Agentic Reinforcement Learning (RL) trains Large Language Models (LLMs) to solve complex tasks through iterative inter-action with external environments. Driven by this paradigm, the applications of LLMs have evolved from simple language assistants into sophisticated agents capable of solving com-plex, real-world problems [14, 19, 27, 32, 34, 37]. This para-digm also motivates the emergence of industry products such as Claude Code [2], OpenClaw [8] and Deep Research [37]. 

> Menghao Zhang and Shuyong Zhu are the corresponding authors.

Similar to conventional single-turn RL, agentic RL train-ing is dominated by the rollout stage, which accounts for over 70% of total wall-clock time [18, 23, 41]. Existing RL systems mainly optimize this phase by mitigating straggler-induced skewness bubbles [16, 18, 41]. Beyond this shared characterization, the multi-turn nature of agentic trajecto-ries introduces unique system challenges: an efficient roll-out scheduling policy for maximizing end-to-end training throughput, and scalable environment interaction to support such flexible scheduling policy. First, for rollout scheduling, mainstream RL frameworks [10, 68] typically adopt trajectory-level scheduling, where each trajectory is treated as an indivisible execution unit. This coarse granularity ignores generation–interaction state tran-sitions, thereby leaving GPUs underutilized, which we call 

turn-level agentic bubbles . Recent works address this problem by decoupling generation controllers from environment con-trollers, allowing one trajectory’s generation to overlap with another’s tool interaction [5, 33]. Though this action-level scheduling policy maximizes rollout GPU throughput, it may not accelerate training process since the trainer only waits for enough completed trajectory groups to form the next training batch, while this policy scatters rollout progress across samples. Moreover, KV Cache pressure and eviction are amplified by multi-turn execution. A trajectory may first generate, suspend during tool execution, and later resume after its KV Cache has already been evicted by other active trajectories [6, 28, 31]. This causes repeated recomputation overhead. What’s worse, the action-level scheduling, while effective for eliminating GPU idle time, increases the number of interleaved active trajectories and thereby aggravates this problem. Both problems pose challenges for an optimized rollout scheduling policy. Second, for environment interaction, the challenge is not merely the safe execution and isolation of each tool sand-boxes, but to sustain the high environment concurrency required by fine-grained rollout scheduling. Action-level scheduling increases the number of active trajectories so that rollout GPUs can consume ready trajectories while oth-ers wait for tool execution. However, if the environment side cannot support this concurrency, generation workers are arXiv:2610.03286v1 [cs.DC] 2 Oct 2026 blocked by interaction-side stalls, preventing action-level scheduling from fully realizing its benefit. Thus, scalable environment interaction in fact places a strong demand on high-density sandbox deployment. However, in production, sandboxes are conservatively packed by their configured memory ceilings, even though their actual resident set is far smaller, which leads to "memory stranding" [17, 25, 30]. Worse yet, existing systems treat sandboxes as independent instances and overlook the structural property of agentic RL: a sample group consists of multiple rollouts sharing the same prompt. Therefore, trajectories within the same sample group are launched from identical templates and execute nearly identical setup code, so a fraction of their pages are byte-identical. This leads to "state duplication" [24, 43] across sandboxes that could otherwise share memory. From the analysis above, we conclude an efficient agen-tic RL system requires two complementary capabilities: an effective rollout scheduling policy that maximizes training throughput, and a scalable environment manager that sup-ports such scheduling policy. To the end, we propose VenusRL, a fully disaggregated system for agentic RL training. VenusRL decouples training, rollout generation, and environment in-teraction into independent components, and introduces two key mechanisms: a priority-aware action scheduler for end-to-end training throughput, and an environment resource manager for cost-efficient environment interaction. The priority-aware action scheduler retains the benefits of action-level scheduling by separating generation workers from environment workers. Additionally, it incorporates pri-ority into its scheduling decisions. VenusRL defines critical groups as unfinished groups whose completion is most likely to unblock the next trainer batch. At runtime, VenusRL uses length-prediction-based heuristics to identify such groups and prioritizes their execution. To enforce this priority, VenusRL co-designs the RL framework and rollout engine to provide priority-aware GPU-slot execution, ensuring high-priority samples are scheduled ahead of others. Beyond GPU schedul-ing, VenusRL introduces priority-aware KV Cache residency. It employs trajectory-aware radix cache and uses a three-tier KV Cache model for admission control and eviction decisions. This preserves KV Cache entries of high-priority trajecto-ries on a best-effort basis. Furthermore, VenusRL performs priority-aware request orchestration to mitigate the imbal-anced distribution of high-priority samples across rollout workers. It monitors KV Cache pressure across rollout work-ers and migrates requests with their reusable prefix cache to reduce KV contention of high-priority samples. The environment resource manager provides scalable in-teraction by page-grained and high-density sandbox deploy-ment. At cluster scope, VenusRL provides scalable interac-tion by a global controller to allocate sandboxes from a shared environment pool and hide deployment details to the framework. At node scope, VenusRL introduces page-level sandbox placement and dynamic admission control to maximize deployment density while preventing memory overcommitment. To exploit similarity among agentic tra-jectories, VenusRL further builds a group-level page sharing pool. Trajectories from the same sample group share snap-shot pages whenever possible: read-only pages are mapped to the same write-protected physical pages, and private copies are created lazily through copy-on-write upon modification. We implemented VenusRL in 24k lines of Python, Rust and Golang. We conduct extensive evaluations to validate the effectiveness of VenusRL. Experimental data shows that VenusRL outperforms state-of-the-art RL systems, achieving 1.07–3.26 × end-to-end training speedup over Slime [68], 1.06-4.24 × speedup over RollFlash [33], and up to 2.67 × speedup compared to ThunderAgent [28]. Meanwhile, VenusRL re-duces environmental costs by up to 89% compared with E2B [13], a representative and widely-use sandbox platform. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

• We identify two systems challenges introduced by agentic RL training: multi-turn rollout scheduling on the generation side, and external environment man-agement on the interaction side (§2). 

• We propose VenusRL, a fully disaggregated system for Agentic RL training (§3). VenusRL introduces a priority-aware action scheduler (§4) for maximizing end-to-end training throughput, and an environment resource manager (§5) to provide scalable and cost-efficient environment interaction. 

• We implement VenusRL and evaluate it on represen-tative Agentic RL workloads (§6). Experimental re-sults show that VenusRL achieves up to 4.24 × training throughput improvement and reduces environment resource cost by up to 89% compared with existing systems. 

## 2 Background and Motivation 2.1 Agentic RL 

Agentic RL evolves LLMs into autonomous agents capable of solving complex real-world problems, including AI coding, deep research or embodied AI [12, 39, 48, 56, 64]. Departing from static and single-turn reasoning tasks [3, 36, 40], agents act through dynamic interaction with external environments. In this paradigm, LLM agents synthesize plans, invoke tools, observe feedback, and refine subsequent actions within a long-horizon execution process. A typical workflow of agen-tic RL training comprises three phases: rollout, reward and training. ## 2.2 Challenges of agentic RL 

As quantified in previous works [18, 23, 41], rollout domi-nates the RL training pipeline, accounting for over 70% of total wall-clock time. The rollout phase involves repeatedly transition between GPU-side LLM generation and environ ment-side tool execution. This iterative loop introduces two unique system challenges: multi-turn rollout pipeline sched-uling on the generation side and environment resource man-agement on the interaction side. 

Multi-turn Rollout Scheduling. Agentic trajectories alter-nate between GPU-side LLM generation and environment-side tool execution. Mainstream frameworks [10, 68] typi-cally schedule rollouts at the trajectory level, and are un-aware of the state transitions inside each trajectory. As a result, when a trajectory pauses for tool execution, it still occupies a generation slot, leaving GPUs underutilized. We refer to this inefficiency as the turn-level agentic bubble. We profile the active generation batch size on an online SWE-agent [54] workload. As shown in Figure 1a, although the configured generation batch size of each rollout worker is 64, the actual active batch size fluctuates substantially and reaches only 76.5% on average. Meanwhile, the multi-turn nature of agentic trajectories also introduces persistent KV Cache pressure and unexpected eviction as tokens accumulate across turns. To mitigate this problem, hierarchical storage backends [9, 42] extend KV Cache capacity by offloading KV pages to lower-tier storage. However, they introduce extra reload overhead, which prior work has shown still inefficient [6]. Continuum [31] keeps KV Caches alive for a fixed time window using TTL-based management, while ThunderAgent [28] evicts trajectory KV Cache with a shortest-first policy to preserve the continuity of long-context trajectories. However, these designs are still agnostic to the inner characteristics of agentic RL training. 

Environment Resource Management. Agentic RL relies on external environments for tool interaction. Such interac-tions require strong isolation to prevent unsafe tool actions, dependency conflicts, and cross-task interference. Existing systems [11, 49, 54] therefore commonly place each task in an isolated sandbox. Modern agentic sandboxes are de-ployed as microVMs [1, 7] with pre-built images and fixed memory allocations. In production, memory capacity is the primary bottleneck to sandbox density (Details in Appen-dix A). Unlike CPU and network resources, which can be oversubscribed with graceful performance degradation, sand-box memory is typically provisioned as a hard capacity con-straint: once memory is exhausted, no additional sandboxes can be created. Prior works improve memory deployment density through execution-state reuse and resource elasticity. ## 3 VenusRL Design Overview 

To address the challenges above, we propose VenusRL, a fully disaggregated system for agentic RL training, as shown in Fig-ure 4. Fundamentally, VenusRL follows the multi-controller paradigm adopted by prior works [50, 62]. Specifically, the data coordinator serves as the centralized control plane for dataset loading, prompt preprocessing, and sample schedul-ing. Each rollout worker continuously fetches samples from the data coordinator using a dedicated background thread and executes generation asynchronously. When a sample group finishes generation, the rollout worker computes its advantages and streams the group to actor workers for im-mediate gradient computation. Once enough sample groups form a global batch, actor workers accumulate all computed gradients, and trigger a synchronized gradient computation and update across all available GPUs. The updated actor weights are then synchronized back to rollout workers be-fore the next RL iteration. Unlike previous works [16, 65, 68] that only decouple roll-out from training, VenusRL further separates environment interaction from rollout execution. Building on this architec-ture, VenusRL introduces two key components: a priority-aware action scheduler to maximize end-to-end training throughput, and an environment resource manager to pro-vide scalable and cost-efficient environment interaction. 

Priority-aware Action Scheduler. The priority-aware ac-tion scheduler decouples generation workers from environ-ment workers, scheduling samples at action granularity. The scheduler incorporates priority into scheduling policy, and uses a lightweight fine-tuned predictor to estimate each sam-ple’s remaining generation length at turn boundaries for identifying high-priority sample groups. Once high-priority groups are identified, the scheduler prioritizes their samples across GPU-slot execution, KV Cache residency and runtime request orchestration. 

Environment Resource Manager. The environment re-source manager provides scalable interaction by page-grained and high-density sandbox deployment. At cluster scope, the manager maintains a global controller that allocates sand-boxes from a shared environment pool, hiding the deploy-ment details from the RL framework. At node scope, VenusRL introduces page-level sandbox placement and dynamic ad-mission control to maximize node deployment density while preventing memory overcommitment. Additionally, VenusRL exploits the strong similarity among agentic RL sandboxes through a group-level page pool to reuse as many as possible snapshot pages between trajectories. 

## 4 Priority-aware Action Scheduler 

To mitigate the inefficiency of rollout execution discussed in §2, we propose priority-aware action scheduler . We begin by formalizing the optimization problem for end-to-end train-ing. Subsequently, we propose a length-prediction heuristic to identify high-priority sample groups that contribute to end-to-end training throughput. We then design priority-aware action scheduler to prioritize high-priority samples across GPU-slot execution, KV Cache residency and request orchestration.

## 4.2 Critical Groups Identification 

Optimizing training throughput is in fact choosing the best scheduling policy 𝜋 for identifying high-priority groups. A naive solution is the first-come-first-served (FCFS) policy, which is widely used in scheduling systems. However, FCFS policy is not the best practice in agentic RL training due to stochastic response dynamics. Each prompt is replicated into a group of trajectories for advantage estimation. To encour-age exploration and response diversity, a high temperature (e.g., 0.7 for SWE-bench-verified [26]) is often set, which increases output-length variance. Moreover, this variance is further amplified by environment interaction: dynamic and unexpected observations can change the subsequent rea-soning path of each trajectory. As a result, the group arrival order provides limited information about completion order and is insufficient for identifying high-priority groups. Considering the exponential search space, selecting this critical group set at runtime is fundamentally NP-hard (De-tailed proof in Appendix B). Since complex scheduling logic can itself compromise training throughput, VenusRL instead adopts a lightweight online heuristic. We propose a length-prediction heuristic policy for identifying critical groups. The design is based on two observations. 

Observation 1 : per-token latency is nearly constant. Action-level scheduling is necessary to keep rollout GPUs satu-rated. With the fully asynchronous streaming pipeline, roll-out workers continuously receive enough samples for gener-ation with a stable effective batch size. Thus, the per-token latency is nearly constant and the remaining output length becomes trustworthy for estimating remaining GPU time. 

Observation 2 : length prediction accuracy improves as turns accumulate. As trajectory history accumulates, it ex-poses richer signals about the agent’s reasoning path, thereby improving the reliability of remaining-length prediction [59]. Based on these two observations, VenusRL proposes a length-prediction heuristic scheduling policy for identifying high-priority groups. We collect online trajectory traces and convert each sample into a (messages, length) pair. The resulting dataset is used to fine-tune a lightweight model, i.e., Qwen3-0.6B. The model is deployed as a microservice to simplify integration and scale independently from rollout workers. Meanwhile, to keep prediction off the critical path, VenusRL introduces an asynchronous priority policy update 

mechanism. As shown in Figure 5, each trajectory overlaps length prediction with tool execution. The prediction result is then asynchronously reported to the data coordinator. The coordinator updates priorities only when the predicted or-dering of sample groups changes. It then pushes the updated policy to rollout workers without interrupting their genera-tion loops. Rollout workers continuously perform priority-aware scheduling using the latest available policy. Therefore, priority decisions do not block the training pipeline or affect the critical path of training progress. 

Staleness-boundary promotion. Fully asynchronous RL typically enforces a weight-version threshold to bound the maximum number of model updates that any trajectory may span. However, length-prediction-based scheduling raises a potential concern that long-running trajectories may be repeatedly demoted, eventually becoming stale enough to be discarded and thereby biasing the training-data stream. VenusRL therefore adds a strict safeguard: once a trajectory reaches its last valid weight version, it is immediately pro-moted to the highest priority until completion, consistent with prior asynchronous RL systems [16, 68] that preferen-tially serve stale trajectories. In practice, fully asynchronous RL often adopts low staleness ratio to preserve training sta-bility; for instance, prior works [35, 65] recommend that accumulated stale samples should not exceed one global batch size. Such conservative staleness settings limit how long a trajectory can be overtaken by newer requests, sub-stantially restricting priority-induced delay. We provide a detailed analysis and proof in Appendix C, together with experimental evidence in §6.2.

Their generation histories are preserved as partial rollout states [66], and the preempted samples are re-enqueued to wait for future GPU slots. However, enforcing execution priority only at the RL framework is insufficient. A fixed number of framework-level GPU slots does not perceive contention inside the rollout en-gine, especially the token-budget contention. Modern rollout engine [29, 63] typically adopts continuous batching [57], where requests are admitted and executed at batch granu-larity. Without priority awareness, the rollout engine forms new prefill batches in a FCFS order. This can cause priority inversion: a high-priority sample may fail to enter the batch due to insufficient token budget, whereas lower-priority sam-ples have already consumed part of the available token bud-get. Thus, VenusRL extends preemptive scheduling into the rollout engine. During prefill batch formation, the rollout engine prioritizes high-priority samples before admitting low-priority ones. This prevents low-priority samples from occupying token budget needed by high-priority ones. Since VenusRL only has two priority classes, batch construction requires only a linear-time partition rather than a full sort, adding negligible scheduling overhead. For decode batches, when the engine lacks sufficient token space to execute the next forward step, VenusRL retracts low-priority samples first. The framework-level slot preemption and engine-level priority-aware batching co-design strictly guarantees execu-tion order for high-priority samples.

This contention is further amplified by action-level scheduling due to increasing con-currency of active samples. Without proactive and priority-aware KV Cache management, high-priority samples may be delayed due to prefix caches reload or recomputation overhead, which compromises training throughput. To address this issue, VenusRL introduces trajectory-aware radix cache , as shown in Figure 7. VenusRL retains the prefix-tree architecture used by conventional radix caches, but addi-tionally augments cached token pages with trajectory-level reference counters. When each trajectory enters the rollout engine at first time, VenusRL records the priority of the tra-jectory and adds reference counters on the prefix pages it reuses. Unlike request-level protection, this reference is re-leased only when the entire multi-turn trajectory completes. This allows the cache manager to classify otherwise evictable pages into three tiers: unused , which is not referenced by any inflight trajectory; low_ref , which is referenced only by low-priority trajectories; and high_ref , which is referenced by at least one high-priority trajectory. Building on the trajectory-aware radix cache, VenusRL enforces priority-aware KV Cache residency by using the three-tiered model for both eviction and admission. When KV pages must be reclaimed for incoming prefill batch or running decode batch, VenusRL evicts pages in tier order: 

unused first, then low_ref , and high_ref last. Crucially, the eviction scope is further bounded by the priority of the run-ning requests. Low-priority requests only reclaim unused 

and low_ref tokens, whereas high_ref pages are consid-ered evictable merely for high-priority requests. Additionally, VenusRL applies the same tier model at admission time. For each candidate request, VenusRL computes its residual page budget using only the tiers that the request is permitted to evict. Crucially, admission and eviction must operate on the same reference snapshot since any skew between the counters used to compute the admission budget and the counters observed during eviction would introduce either memory leak or out-of-memory crashes. To enforce this consistency, VenusRL serializes all reference mutations at scheduler-iteration boundaries and applies them atomically before batch formation, preventing any reference update from being interleaved between a batch’s admission deci-sion and its KV allocation. This three-tiered eviction policy prevents low-priority trajectories from evicting KV pages that are still referenced by inflight high-priority trajectories, thereby reducing reload or prefill-recomputation overhead on the critical path of high-priority samples. However, tiered eviction alone is still insufficient. Although VenusRL prevents low-priority requests from evicting high_ref 

KV pages, severe KV pressure can still cause eviction among high-priority trajectories themselves. Thus, we propose resi dency-aware priority demotion . Instead of admitting every high-priority request into running batch as long as it can reclaim pages from evictable page pool, VenusRL explic-itly constrains the concurrency of high-priority trajecto-ries to retain KV residency. When the high-priority KV de-mand exceeds the available KV Cache capacity, VenusRL temporarily selects a group of high-priority inflight trajecto-ries and demotes them to low-priority tier, thereby reserving the KV residency for the remaining high-priority trajecto-ries. Notably, VenusRL adopts shortest-context-first policy to select demoted trajectories to preserve long trajectories’ generation progress. Once high-priority trajectories com-plete and release their references, the paused trajectories are promoted back and resumed. By converting reactive high-priority cache thrashing into controlled priority demotion, this mechanism eliminates repeated eviction among high-priority trajectories and reduces recomputation overhead on the critical path. 

Priority-aware Request Orchestration. As discussed above, high-priority requests may still be blocked under severe KV contention. Nevertheless, at the same time, some workers may have sufficient HBM space and execute low-priority samples due to imbalance distribution of high-priority sam-ples. This creates cross-worker priority inversion. To address this imbalance, VenusRL proposes runtime re-quest re-dispatching . VenusRL continuously monitors the high-priority KV Cache pressure of each rollout worker. Once a worker experiences KV contention among high-priority requests, the scheduler redistributes part of its pend-ing requests to workers with the lowest high-priority KV pressure. To avoid the recomputation overhead of long trajec-tory, VenusRL migrates the corresponding prefix KV Cache together. Following prior systems [59, 65], VenusRL uses Mooncake transfer engine [42] for zero-copy and resilient KV Cache movement. Notably, to avoid interference with three-tier allocation and admission consistency, VenusRL transfers KV Cache from host memory to host memory rather than directly manipulating on-device HBM cache. ## 5 Environment Resource Manager 

In this section, we target the low-density deployment prob-lem of agentic RL sandbox as discussed in §2. To solve this, we propose page-level sandbox placement design with dy-namic admission threshold in §5.1, and group-level page sharing design in §5.2. ## 5.1 Sandbox Placement 

VenusRL first introduces a cluster-level controller to allocate sandboxes from a shared environment pool, enabling high-density multiplexing across concurrent agentic tasks. Then, VenusRL performs fine-grained sandbox placement per node to further improve deployment density. As discussed in §2, coarse-grained admission based on declared sandbox memory leads to severe "memory strand-ing", where nearly 90% physical memory is left unused. After deep dive, we find that E2B [13] uses Linux userfaultfd to lazily populate snapshot-backed pages, so physical pages are allocated only when they are first touched. This mechanism explains the gap between actual physical memory usage and virtual upper memory bound, which allows physical node to maintain more sandboxes than permitted. However, this also creates an admission dilemma. If we admit too few sand-boxes, it falls back to conservative allocation and wastes physical memory. If we admit too many, later page faults, Copy-on-Write (CoW) modification, and runtime allocations may exhaust physical pages and trigger Out-Of-Memory (OOM) issues. Therefore, the key problem is to decide how far a node can safely overcommit memory at runtime. VenusRL addresses this with a dynamic admission thresh-old 𝑇 , where details can be seen in Algorithm 1. A node ad-mits a new sandbox only when its current physical memory usage is below this threshold. Dynamic Admission Threshold Adaption. The challenge here is how to estimate future memory growth for determin-ing the admission threshold 𝑇 . To answer this, we first deep dive into the memory allocation behavior of Firecracker [1]. We observe that the physical memory growth mainly comes from four sources: library loading, interpreter runtime caches, allocator operation, and CoW modifications to shared snap-shot pages. A deep investigation shows that sandbox memory growth has a structured lifecycle rather than an arbitrary pattern. Library loading and interpreter caches dominate the cold-start phase and are largely determined by the sand-box template. Allocator operations and CoW modifications continue during running phase, but both shows slower mar-ginal growth than the cold-start phase. The reason behind is that once allocators have expanded their arenas, subsequent allocations are often served from existing pages. Similarly, once shared pages have been privatized, later writes to the same pages no longer trigger additional CoW allocation. As a result, most sandboxes memory growth is typically bursty during cold start and much slower during running phase. Based on this observation, VenusRL uses two different predict heuristic for cold-start and running sandboxes. For cold-start phase sandboxes, VenusRL uses offline profiling of the same template to estimate memory growth, since sandboxes from the same template share the same runtime, dependencies, and initialization path. For running sandboxes, VenusRL esti-mates the recent growth rate from a short time window and uses this growth rate to estimate their short-term memory demand. At admission time, VenusRL accounts for both the re-cent growth of active sandboxes and the expected cold-start growth of the candidate one. It admits the candidate only af-ter reserving sufficient headroom for this near-term growth, with a safety factor 𝛼 to tolerate prediction bias. When sand-box memory usage is stable, VenusRL raises the threshold and admits more sandboxes; when memory is still grow-ing quickly, VenusRL lowers the threshold and delays fur-ther admission. This mechanism allows VenusRL to increase deployment density while avoiding the unsafe behavior of admitting sandboxes solely based on current memory usage. 

Sandbox Migration. Although dynamic admission thresh-old substantially reduces OOM risk, it may still underesti-mate sudden memory bursts when sandbox footprints grow faster than the predictor. To improve robustness, VenusRL uses sandbox migration as a fallback mechanism. When the controller fails to allocate new physical pages for allocation request, VenusRL intercepts the resulting OOM exception signal before the failure propagates to the task. It then temporarily pauses all running sandboxes while pre-serving their in-memory states. It then selects the sandbox with the largest resident memory footprint and migrates only this sandbox to another resource worker. After migration, VenusRL recomputes the node’s memory usage and admis-sion threshold. If the node still exceeds the safe threshold, VenusRL repeats the process. This incremental largest-first policy avoids unnecessary migration: VenusRL releases one sandbox’s memory at a time and stops as soon as the node re-turns to a safe state. To reduce migration overhead and limit its impact on the critical path, VenusRL uses the Mooncake transfer engine [42] to transfer sandbox memory state di-rectly between resource workers. This direct memory-state transfer avoids slow checkpoint-and-reload paths and al-lows VenusRL to quickly relieve memory pressure while pre-serving sandbox execution state. Upon migration, VenusRL resumes all paused running sandboxes.

As shown in Figure 9, VenusRL realizes intra-group sharing by decoupling three issues that the native fork/mmap path conflates, i.e., virtual-address ownership, physical-page residency, and write permission. Each sandbox keeps its own VMA (Virtual Memory Area) and page table, but read-only PTEs (Page Table Entry) from different sandboxes in a group may alias the same physical frame (shared residency), with write permission masked until a write actually occurs, i.e., lazy CoW. Two requirements must be satisfied. (i) Eager bind-ing: sharing should be installed at sandbox creation, before any page fault, so that no sandbox ever allocates a redundant frame for a snapshot page (Figure 9-left). (ii) Strict isolation: a write by one sandbox can’t be visible to its peers and com-promised sandboxes can’t corrupt the shared backing store. VenusRL realizes this across two layers: a user-space group page pool and a kernel-side write-protected mapping. 

Group Page Pool At User Space. VenusRL maintains a per-group page pool indexed by guest-memory offset, using the environment template as the sharing key. Pages are pop-ulated lazily: when any sandbox in the group first accesses a page, VenusRL loads that page into the shared pool (Fig-ure 9-middle). For each memory offset, VenusRL allows only one sandbox to perform this first load at a time, so concur-rent sandboxes always share the same populated page. Once populated, a pool entry is immutable: subsequent accesses from any group member find the frame already resident, with no per-sandbox allocation. Because the pool is keyed by template rather than by content hash, VenusRL never scans memory to discover sharing; every read-only snapshot page is shared by construction. 

Per-Sandbox Mapping And CoW At Kernel Level. Each sandbox maps its guest memory as a private, write-protected view over the page pool. Reads behave as ordinary guest-memory accesses, with the sandbox’s PTE silently aliasing a frame in the pool. A write is handled by allocating a private writable frame, copying the page contents, and rebinding only that sandbox’s PTE to the new frame. The pool entry and every other sandbox’s mapping are left untouched (Figure 9-right). Because aliasing is performed at PTE granularity rather than by sharing whole memory regions, divergence is local—isolation is preserved at the same granularity that the sandbox boundary already enforces, and a compromised sandbox can corrupt only its own private frames.

## 7 Related Work 

KV Cache Management. KV cache management has been extensively studied for efficient LLM serving, primarily focus-ing on allocation, reuse, and eviction. PagedAttention [29] manages KV caches with page-like blocks to reduce fragmen-tation, while vAttention [38] further decouples virtual and physical KV allocation. Other works like Mooncake [42] and HiCache [9] extend KV storage across GPU memory, host memory, SSDs, or remote storage. VenusRL’s priority-aware KV cache residency is complementary to these techniques and can be integrated with them. 

Resource Auto-scaling. Auto-scaling is widely used to improve deployment efficiency in cloud systems. For LLM in-ference, recent systems [51, 58, 61] optimize resource scaling for large-scale serving workloads. In RL systems, DistRS [67] uses history-based scaling and timeout-aware mechanisms to adjust resources at batch boundaries. Due to the strong isolation of MicroVM-based environments, VenusRL does not explore dynamic scaling, but we view it as a promising direction for future work. 

## 8 Conclusion 

This paper presents VenusRL, a fully disaggregated agentic RL system with priority scheduling and scalable interaction. VenusRL designs the priority-aware action scheduler for effective multi-turn rollout scheduling and environment re-source manager for scalable and cost-efficient environment interaction. Implementation and evaluations demonstrate that VenusRL accelerates training by up to 4.24 × and reduces environment costs by nearly 89%.

## C Effect of priority scheduling on model learning 

Though there are concerns that favoring easy-to-complete groups could persistently starve long trajectories, biasing the training data. Actually, this does not occur, due to following reasons. First, priority scheduling does not directly introduce ad-ditional off-policy behavior. In fully asynchronous RL, the off-policy degree of a sample is determined by the mismatch between the behavior policy 𝜋 𝑏 that generates it and the cur-rent policy 𝜋 𝜃 used for optimization, typically characterized by the importance ratio 𝑟 (𝑠, 𝑎 ) = 𝜋 𝜃 (𝑎 |𝑠 )    

> 𝜋 𝑏 (𝑎 |𝑠 )

. Scheduling only changes the execution and completion order of outstanding trajectories; it does not modify either policy itself. Its only possible effect on 𝑟 (𝑠, 𝑎 ) is indirect, through the number of weight updates that occur before a trajectory is consumed, i.e., its policy staleness. Therefore, priority scheduling may change the realized staleness of individual trajectories within this window, but does not introduce a new source of off-policy behavior beyond the staleness already present in fully asynchronous training. Second, long low-priority trajectories are not continuously blocked. Fully asynchronous RL does not allow rollout gener-ation to advance arbitrarily far ahead of training. With global batch size 𝐵 and maximum staleness 𝜂 , only 𝑂 (( 𝜂 + 1)𝐵 )

outstanding trajectories can remain within the admissible asynchronous window. Length-based priority therefore only reorders a finite competing set. Short trajectories may tem-porarily overtake a long trajectory, but they leave the active set once completed, while the staleness controller prevents an unbounded stream of new trajectories from continuously entering ahead of it. Additionally, the staleness ratio is delib-erately kept low to preserve algorithmic stability. Prior works like Asynchronous RLHF [35] and StreamRL [65] recommend that accumulated stale samples should not exceed one global batch size. As the step progresses, the higher-priority com-peting set shrinks, allowing long trajectories to continue generation rather than being repeatedly demoted. Thus, the stale long trajectory naturally becomes high-priority samples when step progresses. Third, VenusRL adds a hard safeguard for the tail case. Once a trajectory reaches its last admissible weight version, VenusRL immediately promotes it to the highest priority until completion. At this point, ordinary length-based pri-ority no longer applies, preventing newer short trajecto-ries from further delaying it. Thus, bounded staleness al-ready limits the opportunity for persistent deprioritization, while staleness-boundary promotion provides an explicit safety guard against priority-induced starvation. Moreover, VenusRL is fully compatible with off-policy correction meth-ods such as TIS [55] and IcePop [60], further preserving stability. 

## D Impact of OOM Recovered Behaviour 

VenusRL does not rely on a single mechanism to prevent OOM. Admission Threshold Adaption (Algorithm 1) serves as the primary safeguard: a sandbox creation request is ad-mitted only when the projected memory footprint of resident sandboxes remains below a workload-specific safety thresh-old. The reserved headroom is intended to absorb memory growth that occurs after admission and between consecutive control intervals. In practice, such growth is both sparse and small. In our production trace, fewer than 0 .5% of the sandboxes on a node exhibited concurrent memory bursts within a control interval, and each burst increased memory usage by at most roughly 100 MiB. These events were primarily caused by on-demand package installation triggered by agent tool calls and were therefore short-lived rather than sustained. Conse-quently, the aggregate unaccounted memory growth within a control interval is usually well within the headroom reserved by the safety threshold. With the threshold empirically tuned for each workload, the observed OOM frequency is therefore near zero. Additionally, rather than provisioning substantially more memory for these rare cases, VenusRL treats OOM as a re-coverable event. When it occurs, the affected sandbox is recovered on another node by transferring its full memory state over P2P RDMA and resuming execution there. This recovery takes approximately 2 .7 s (Figure 19), substantially less than the duration of a single RL step, making its over-head negligible at the RL timescale. We also further manu-ally inspected recovered sandboxes and verified that both filesystem contents and running process state were preserved across recovery. 

## E Robustness of the predictor 

VenusRL uses predicted remaining generation lengths to separate long and short samples and prioritize them accord-ingly. Thus, the scheduler primarily relies on the relative ordering of predictions rather than accurate absolute token counts. Prediction errors are therefore tolerable as long as they preserve the ordering relevant to scheduling. The pre-dictor conditions on both the problem specification and the accumulated trajectory history: the former provides signals about task difficulty, while the latter—including intermedi-ate reasoning, tool calls, and environment observations— reveals the sample’s current progress and remaining work. As execution proceeds, the predictor updates its estimate at turn boundaries, allowing scheduling decisions to track the evolving trajectory state. Policy updates during RL may shift absolute generation lengths, but they do not necessarily change the relative ordering across samples. Consequently, a lightweight predictor fine-tuned before training can remain effective as the rollout policy evolves. The Spearman correla-tion of 0 .98 reported in the main paper further confirms that the predictor accurately recovers the ordering required for priority scheduling. Additionally, the predictor can also be updated at training checkpoints using recent trajectories to mitigate policy drift, with fine-tuning typically converging within only minutes. We train the predictor using trajectories collected at the same sampling temperature as the target rollout workload. For each temperature, we collect a separate set of trajecto-ries and construct the corresponding (messages, length) 

pairs for fine-tuning. This matching avoids distribution shift between predictor training and rollout-time sampling be-havior. Supporting a different rollout temperature therefore only requires fine-tuning on trajectories collected under that temperature; the prediction mechanism itself is not tied to any particular setting. We report results at temperature 0 .7since this is the SWE-agent configuration used throughout our evaluation, rather than a limitation of the predictor.
