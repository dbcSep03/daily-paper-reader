Abstract 

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains agents on their own trajectories using supervision from a stronger teacher. We uncover a counterintu-itive phenomenon in multi-turn agentic tasks: a smaller, initially weaker student can sub-stantially outperform its teacher during train-ing, only to regress as distillation continues. We term this phenomenon distillation over-shoot . Through decision- and task-level anal-yses, we trace overshoot to two competing effects of OPD: repairing weak student deci-sions and eroding successful student choices that the teacher disfavors. Early in training, repair outpaces erosion, allowing the student to combine the teacher’s corrections with its own strengths and surpass the teacher. As training continues, erosion overtakes repair, causing these gains to vanish. Motivated by this finding, we propose Success-Preserving

On-Policy Distillation ( SPOD ), which selec-tively masks conflicting teacher supervision on successful student choices and introduces a soft probability floor to counteract their ero-sion, while retaining teacher guidance else-where. SPOD requires only binary episode success and model probabilities already avail-able during OPD. Across two model families and three agentic benchmarks, SPOD consis-tently outperforms ordinary OPD and evaluated variants, improving final success by up to 15.2 percentage points and surpassing the teacher by up to 20.0 points. Moreover, SPOD’s final checkpoints outperform even ordinary OPD’s best intermediate checkpoints in all six settings, demonstrating that beyond-teacher gains can be not only preserved but further extended. 

1 Introduction 

On-policy distillation (OPD) has become an effec-tive approach for training smaller language models under the guidance of larger teachers (Agarwal 1arXiv:2610.25034v1 [cs.LG] 18 Oct 2026 et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Qwen Team, 2025; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025), and is increas-ingly adopted for multi-turn agentic tasks (Wang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026a; Zhong et al., 2026). By matching the teacher’s ac-tion distribution at each decision state along the student’s own trajectories, OPD reduces the train-test mismatch associated with fixed demonstrations (Ross et al., 2011). Since the teacher serves as the target of distillation (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021), recent approaches have sought to surpass its performance through stronger supervision or addi-tional reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2026a; Tan et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026b). In this work, we uncover a distillation overshoot 

phenomenon in agentic OPD: a smaller, initially much weaker student can substantially outperform its larger teacher during training, only to lose much of this advantage as distillation continues. For in-stance, on ALFWorld, a Gemma E4B student im-proves from an initial 16.5% success, compared with 68.9% for its 31B teacher, to a peak of 82.8%, before declining to 73.7% by the end of training (Figure 1(a)). Similar overshoot-then-regression patterns emerge across multiple model–benchmark settings and OPD variants (Figure 3). These find-ings challenge the view of teacher performance as a natural ceiling for the distillation process. To understand this overshoot phenomenon, we examine how OPD affects individual student deci-sions and their impact on task success. Our analysis reveals two competing effects: repair of weak stu-dent decisions and erosion of successful student choices that the teacher disfavors (Figure 2). Cru-cially, these effects unfold at different rates. Early in training, repair outpaces erosion, allowing the student to combine teacher corrections with its own strengths and surpass the teacher. As distillation continues, however, erosion overtakes repair, driv-ing regression. We substantiate this mechanism at both the decision and task levels. Successful ac-tions strongly disfavored by the teacher lose about two-thirds of their probability within five updates and remain suppressed, while teacher-endorsed ac-tions continue to strengthen (Figure 1(b), bottom right). At the task level, gains dominate losses be-fore the peak, but this balance reverses afterward (Figure 1(b), bottom left). This diagnosis suggests that preserving beyond-teacher gains requires protecting successful stu-dent choices while retaining useful teacher guid-ance. Recent agentic OPD methods adjust rollout horizons, teacher interventions, supervision selec-tion, or loss weights (Wang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026a; Zhong et al., 2026), but do not explicitly protect successful student choices from conflicting teacher supervision. Motivated by this diagnosis, we propose Success-

Preserving On-Policy Distillation ( SPOD ), a sim-ple modification of OPD that preserves successful student choices while continuing to learn from the teacher elsewhere. Specifically, when an action belongs to a successful student trajectory and the teacher assigns it lower probability than the stu-dent, SPOD masks the corresponding distillation loss and applies a soft probability floor. These two components address complementary sources of erosion: masking removes the direct gradient that pulls the student toward the teacher, while the probability floor counteracts indirect suppression caused by parameter updates at other decisions. All remaining actions receive ordinary teacher supervi-sion (Figure 1(c)). SPOD uses only binary episode success and model probabilities already available in OPD, requiring no additional reward model, re-play buffer, or task-family-specific supervision. Experiments over two model families (Qwen (Qwen Team, 2026) and Gemma (Gemma Team, Google DeepMind, 2026)) and three multi-turn agentic benchmarks (ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), and Science-World (Wang et al., 2022)) demonstrate that SPOD outperforms ordinary OPD, the teacher, and evalu-ated OPD variants, improving average final success over ordinary OPD by up to 12.4 points. Gains reach 15.2 points over ordinary OPD and 20.0 points over the teacher. Notably, SPOD’s final 2checkpoints surpass even ordinary OPD’s best in-termediate checkpoints in every setting, demon-strating that it not only preserves but also extends the overshoot gains. Ablation studies further con-firm the complementary roles of masking conflict-ing supervision and preserving successful action probabilities. To summarize, our contributions are: • We reveal distillation overshoot phenomenon in agentic OPD: smaller, initially weaker stu-dents can substantially surpass their larger teachers mid-training before regressing. • We trace this phenomenon to the competing ef-fects of repairing weak student decisions and 

eroding successful choices that the teacher disfavors, explaining the beyond-teacher per-formance and its subsequent decline. • We propose SPOD, a distillation objective that selectively protects successful student choices from conflicting teacher supervision while re-taining useful guidance, surpassing the best intermediate checkpoints of ordinary OPD. 

2 Related Work 

On-policy distillation. OPD trains students on their own visited states, reducing the train– deployment mismatch of fixed demonstrations (Ross et al., 2011). GKD applies this principle to language models (Agarwal et al., 2024), MiniLLM uses reverse KL (Gu et al., 2024), and OPD is widely used in model post-training (Qwen Team, 2025; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). For multi-turn agents, TCOD schedules rollout hori-zons (Wang et al., 2026b), Guided-OPD introduces teacher turns (Li et al., 2026), SAGE-OPD selects interventions using teacher judgments (Zhou et al., 2026a), and SOD and ATOD reweight supervision using student–teacher disagreement (Zhong et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). Less is More truncates rollouts to avoid degraded teacher guidance at later token positions (Zhou et al., 2026b). These meth-ods adjust the collection or application of teacher guidance while SPOD selectively protects success-ful student choices that this guidance would erode. 

Students surpassing teachers. Students can ex-ceed their teachers through same-size distillation (Furlanello et al., 2018; Agarwal et al., 2024), weak-to-strong learning (Burns et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2026a), and multi-teacher supervision (Wang et al., 2026a). OPD studies likewise report students outperforming their teachers after training (Wang et al., 2026b; Tan et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026b; Nie, 2026). However, we show that final performance above the teacher can conceal a much stronger in-termediate policy, even for smaller, initially weaker students in multi-turn agentic OPD. We further ex-plain this rise and regression through competing repair and erosion, motivating SPOD.

3.2 Overshoot Emerges as Repair Initially Outpaces Erosion Repair initially outpaces erosion within trajecto-ries. To understand why the student surpasses its teacher, we first examine individual decisions. In a successfully learned Qwen episode (“heat some apple and put it in sidetable”), the probability of “go to microwave” rises from 9.3 × 10 −5 to 0.87 by update 15, completing the failure-to-success flip, even though the already-correct “heat apple” falls only from 0.82 to 0.58 (teacher: 0.076); a second example appears in Figure 1b (top). The pattern is general: 82.7% of strongly teacher-disfavored actions from successful episodes at update 11 halve by update 15, yet task success still rises from 52.1% to 86.1%. Thus early in training, repair often out-paces erosion. 

Decision-level imbalance yields beyond-teacher task-level performance. Consistent with this mechanism, task-level gains initially far exceed losses. Summing increases and decreases in per-task success between adjacent checkpoints, Qwen gains 188.7 task-equivalents from update 5 to its peak at update 15 but loses only 7.3 (Fig-ure 1b(bottom left)); gains exceed losses before the peak in all five ordinary-OPD runs across both model families. Moreover, at the peak both stu-dents solve 57.4% of the ALFWorld tasks the teacher usually fails (teachers: 16.0% and 4.7%), while still solving 93.2% and 94.5% of those it usually solves (Figure 4). Consequently, the peak student surpasses the teacher by retaining its reli-able behavior while adding successes where it is unreliable .

3.3 Regression Emerges as Erosion Overtakes Repair Continued imitation suppresses successful stu-dent choices. Despite repair’s initial lead, ero-sion still occurs, and its source is the distillation objective itself. Specifically, at a token position, forward KL has the exact logit derivative 

∂D KL (q∥pθ)

∂z v

= pθ(v) − q(v). (2) Whenever the student assigns a token higher prob-ability than the teacher, this gradient pushes its logit down, regardless of whether the episode suc-ceeded. Ordinary OPD thus treats a successful de-parture from the teacher the same as a mistake, and this pressure accumulates on the student’s useful choices as training continues. 

Successful choices erode as teacher-supported actions strengthen. We examine this pressure empirically by rescoring fixed state–action pairs from successful trajectories near the peak. In Qwen, teacher-supported actions rise in mean probabil-ity from 0.80 to 0.89, whereas strongly teacher-disfavored successful actions fall by 67%, from 0.36 to 0.12, within five updates and remain low (Figure 1b(bottom right)). Continued imitation thus strengthens teacher-supported behavior while erod-ing the student’s useful departures from it. 

Decision-level erosion translates into task-level regression. Does this erosion explain why tasks are lost? For the 25 Qwen tasks solved at the peak but lost by the end, we track the action on each successful peak trajectory whose probability falls most. We find that these actions are almost always teacher-disfavored (92%) and erode quickly: 22 of 25 halve within five updates, at or before the check-point where the task is first lost in 96% of tasks (76% at exactly that checkpoint; Figure 5). Gemma shows the same pattern across 37 lost tasks. Tasks are thus lost when continued imitation erodes a decisive choice the student had gotten right .In tasks that remain solved, erosion is milder (median 7-fold versus 41-fold) and rarely decisive, since an alternative route usually survives. Repair also continues after the peak (Qwen gains 85.7 task-equivalents) but is outpaced by erosion (100.7 lost), thus producing the regression.

3.5 Teacher Reliability Predicts Where Gains Are Fragile 

To locate post-peak losses, we group tasks by how often the teacher solves them. Losses concentrate where the teacher is unreliable: on tasks it never solves, success falls from the peak to the final checkpoint by 27.4 points for Qwen and 22.1 for Gemma, while tasks it always solves stay near their peak (Figure 4); this holds in all five ordinary-OPD runs. Task families also peak at different times: for Gemma, on ALFWorld, “Look” peaks at update 15 and falls from 74.2% to 28.0%, “Clean” peaks at update 25 and falls to 50.0%, while “Cool” and “Heat” peak at update 30 and hold. Thus no sin-gle stopping time, and no family-level rule, retains every gain, motivating decision-level protection. 

4 Success-Preserving On-Policy Distillation (SPOD) 

The preceding analysis motivates protecting suc-cessful student choices from conflicting supervi-sion while retaining useful teacher guidance. We therefore propose SPOD, a lightweight interven-tion which keeps ordinary OPD’s student rollouts and teacher scoring and modifies only the loss at each sampled decision.

4.2 Masking Makes the Floor Effective 

A natural alternative is to add the floor to ordinary OPD without masking. However, this would pit the teacher’s pull against the floor’s push, settling the protected action below the floor. SPOD avoids this by removing the teacher term on protected actions, leaving the floor unopposed. A one-decision model makes this precise. Let action 1 succeed and action 0 fail, with student and teacher probabilities p =

σ(z) and q for action 1; actions are sampled from the student, and the sampling distribution and gate are held fixed, as in SPOD. 

Proposition 4.1 (Masking enables preservation) .

Let λ = 1 , 0 < q < τ 20 and q < p < τ 0. Keeping the distillation term on gated actions and adding the floor gives E[∇z ℓadd ] = p2 − q, with a stable equilibrium at p = √q < τ 0. SPOD instead gives 

E[∇z ℓSPOD ] = −q(1 − p) < 0 below the floor and (1 − p)( p − q) > 0 just above it, so τ0 is an attracting boundary. 

For example, with q = 0 .16 and τ0 = 0 .5, the floor alone settles at a compromise with the teacher at p = 0 .4, while masking lets the protected action reach the floor. This matches the ablation: the floor alone or masking alone underperforms their combi-nation (subsection 5.4). Proofs and an analysis of shared-parameter effects are in Appendix D. 

Implementation and cost. SPOD adds negligi-ble cost: it reuses OPD’s student and teacher scor-ing passes, adding only sequence log-probability sums and a scalar gate. Action probabilities in-clude the end-of-action token and are computed before temperature or action constraints. Losses are averaged over episodes, states, then tokens, so long actions do not dominate. Beyond ordinary OPD, SPOD needs only binary episode success: no stored policy, per-family anchor, extra rollout, or teacher judgment are required.

We characterize distillation overshoot in multi-turn agentic OPD: smaller, initially weaker students can substantially surpass their larger teachers mid-training before regressing. The teacher initially repairs weak decisions faster than it erodes success-ful student choices; regression follows as erosion takes over. Motivated by this diagnosis, SPOD withholds conflicting teacher supervision and adds a soft probability floor on successful actions the teacher disfavors. Across six model–benchmark settings, SPOD outperforms the teacher, ordinary OPD, and its best intermediate checkpoints, pre-serving and extending the overshoot gains. Results with Guided-OPD further demonstrate SPOD’s po-tential to complement existing OPD methods. 9Limitations 

Statistical support. SPOD, ordinary OPD, al-ternatives, ablations, and sensitivity experiments use three training seeds; TCOD, Guided-OPD, and SAGE-OPD use one each. Every checkpoint is evaluated with three evaluation seeds. Additional training seeds would strengthen comparisons with the single-seed baselines. 

Experimental scope. We evaluate two teacher– student pairs and three text-based agentic bench-marks. Compute constraints limit our study to smaller students; larger-scale distillation remains a natural extension. Within each pair, student and teacher share a vocabulary, enabling full-vocabulary distillation. Extending SPOD across tokenizers and to real-world environments would broaden its applicability. 

Replication and coverage. SPOD, ordinary OPD, alternatives, ablations, and sensitivity ex-periments use three training seeds, while TCOD, Guided-OPD, and SAGE-OPD use one each. All checkpoints are evaluated with three evaluation seeds. Additional training seeds would strengthen comparisons with these methods. Hyperparam-eter sensitivity and the alternatives comparison focus on Qwen ALFWorld, while composition with Guided-OPD is evaluated on Gemma Science-World. The latter demonstrates a promising com-bination; broader evaluation would establish how widely this complementarity extends. 

Mechanism analysis. Our detailed decision-level analysis focuses on ALFWorld. The action-tracing evidence is retrospective, while the two-decision model isolates repair and erosion using independent decisions and illustrative update rates. Extending these probes to other environments and testing interventions on individual decisions would further characterize the mechanism. 

Outcome supervision. SPOD uses binary episode success to identify behavior worth preserv-ing, without requiring action-level annotations. This signal does not distinguish each action’s contribution to success. The current gate assumes reliable episode outcomes; incorporating noisy or graded feedback could extend preservation to tasks with less structured evaluation.
