# Tree-Aware Posterior Distillation for Long-Horizon Agentic Reinforcement Learning 

Heming Zou 1,2,* , Qi Wang 1, Yixiu Mao 1, Yun Qu 1, Yuyi Guo 1,2,* , Zeyuan Liu 1,2,* ,Chenglin Li 1, Weijie Liu 2, Saiyong Yang 2, Xiangyang Ji 1

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a scalable post-training paradigm for large language models. However, training agents over long-horizon interactions presents two challenges: using only terminal rewards makes turn-level credit assignment sparse and high-variance, while sequential mini-batch updates on a fixed rollout batch introduce policy staleness as the current policy drifts from the behavior policy. Existing approaches obtain denser credit via learned critics, process reward models, or on-policy distillation, but they rely on a value network, step-level annotations, or a stronger external teacher that increase training complexity. Meanwhile, importance sampling and clipping mitigate policy drift, but they do not specify a finite target that stops sequential updates from overshooting. In this work, we introduce TreeOPSD, a critic-free, tree-aware posterior self-distillation framework for long-horizon agentic reinforcement learning. Treating each interaction turn as a tree node, TreeOPSD branches alternative continuations from shared prefixes and recursively backs up terminal rewards into implicit turn-level credit. It then maps each credit to a closed-form, bounded teacher distribution parameterized by the likelihood ratio relative to the frozen behavior policy. Minimizing the divergence between this teacher and the student policy recovers the policy-gradient direction as the tangent at the behavior policy while bounding updates toward a finite target across sequential mini-batches. Empirically, TreeOPSD improves agentic capabilities across several competitive benchmarks and model scales, especially for long-horizon tasks, e.g., raising the average accuracy of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 by 4.97 points over GRPO on the Deep Research setting.

# 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025) has emerged as a central post-training paradigm for large language models (LLMs), using outcome rewards to incentivize reasoning or environment interactions on complex problem-solving tasks. While reasoning-oriented RLVR typically learns from Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rollouts, agentic RL (Yao et al., 2022) extends this paradigm to long-horizon, multi-turn trajectories in which reasoning is interleaved with actions and environment observations. As the interaction horizon grows, a single terminal reward provides increasingly sparse and high-variance supervision across intermediate turns: broadcasting one trajectory-level advantage over the entire rollout over-credits unhelpful actions in successful trajectories and penalizes useful steps in failed ones (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019; Li et al., 2026a). At the same time, updating the policy across sequential mini-batches within each rollout batch induces policy staleness as the current policy departs from the behavior policy (Zheng et al., 2025a). To address the credit assignment problem, actor–critic methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) estimate token-level advantages with a learned critic, which is difficult to train accurately and susceptible to value flattening (Li et al., 2026c). Process reward models (PRMs) (Lightman et al., 2024) provide step-level rewards but require process annotations, and summing them as discounted returns is vulnerable to reward hacking (Cheng et al., 2026). On-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024) provides dense token-level supervision on 

1arXiv:2610.23181v1 [cs.LG] 16 Oct 2026 student rollouts at the cost of querying a stronger external teacher. Extending OPD, on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026) constructs a self-teacher by conditioning the model on privileged context (e.g., reference solutions), which relies on task-specific heuristics and can yield unstable supervision. These limitations leave open how to derive dense intermediate credit directly from terminal verifier rewards. Sampling independent trajectories confounds all turns along each rollout, whereas multi-turn interaction exposes discrete turn-level prefixes as natural branching points. Branching alternative continuations from a shared prefix fixes the preceding history to isolate how subsequent decisions affect terminal outcomes, forming a rollout tree on which recursive backup converts terminal rewards into implicit turn-level credit without auxiliary value or reward models (Ji et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2026a).

Sequential mini-batch updates on a fixed rollout batch create a separate optimization challenge. While all rollouts in the batch are sampled from a frozen behavior policy 𝜋 𝜃 old , each update step moves the current policy 𝜋 𝜃 further away, increasing policy staleness for subsequent mini-batches. Policy-gradient methods compensate for this mismatch with importance-sampling ratios, and stabilize training through clipping (Schulman et al., 2017) or ratio regularizers (Zheng et al., 2025a) as those ratios depart from one. However, such controls only constrain the importance ratio while keeping the collection-time credit fixed, so the surrogate still specifies an ascent direction rather than a finite destination (Qu et al., 2026a). We therefore seek an objective that maps tree credit to a finite target and adapts its gradient as 𝜋 𝜃 evolves, halting at the target and reversing sign after overshoot. To this end, we introduce TreeOPSD, a critic-free posterior self-distillation framework for long-horizon agentic RL. TreeOPSD first maps backed-up turn credit into a frozen turn-level teacher distribution relative to the behavior policy, and then minimizes the divergence between this teacher and the current student policy across sequential mini-batches. Geometrically, the tangent of this distillation objective at the behavior policy recovers the exact tree policy-gradient direction, while later mini-batches scale each update by the current student–teacher gap rather than a fixed collection-time credit weight. Figure 1 illustrates this decoupling of target construction and distribution matching. In summary, our main contributions are two-fold: 

1. We derive closed-form, bounded turn-level target distributions via an affine map of backed-up credit relative to the frozen behavior policy. Because this affine map commutes with tree backup, matching the backed-up parent target is gradient-equivalent to aggregating descendant distillation losses. 

2. We formulate a Forward-KL posterior distillation objective whose tangent at the behavior policy recovers the tree policy gradient, while its student–teacher residual vanishes at the target and reverses sign upon overshoot to stabilize sequential mini-batch optimization. Empirically, TreeOPSD improves agentic capabilities across competitive benchmarks and model scales, and it elicits longer-horizon agent behavior with more interaction turns in Deep Research.

4.2 Main results 

Table 1 and Figure 4 show TreeOPSD with the highest Multi-Hop QA and Deep Research averages at both scales. TreePO is trained on the same initialize-then-expand trees, the same credits 𝑉 𝑖 , and the same optimizer blocks, so the two methods differ only in how that credit is used. TreePO keeps the collection-time coefficient 𝑉 𝑖 /𝐿 𝑥 on every later block. TreeOPSD matches a frozen turn teacher and recomputes the residual as 𝜋 𝜃 moves. TreeOPSD improves both Multi-Hop QA and Deep Research, with a larger gain on the longer-horizon Deep Research setting, where it raises the average over TreePO from 13.07 to 15.45 for Qwen3-4B and from 17.59 to 21.20 for Qwen3-30B-A3B. On that longer horizon, later blocks more often update a policy that has already left 𝜋 𝜃 old . A finite teacher can stop or reverse at that point, while a fixed credit keeps the original sign. The same policies remain ahead on BrowseComp, GAIA, and xbench, which use open-web search rather than the training corpus. Appendix D.3 also shows the performance gain under the same training time.

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented TreeOPSD, a critic-free posterior self-distillation framework for long-horizon agentic RLVR. It backs sparse terminal rewards into implicit turn-level credit on a shared-prefix tree and mitigates policy drift by matching a finite behavior-relative teacher with Forward KL. The affine target commutes with tree backup, and the distillation gradient recovers tree policy gradient at the behavior policy, then stops or reverses after overshoot, so a rollout batch supports sequential updates without a learned critic, process labels, or an external teacher. TreeOPSD improves over matched-budget baselines at both scales on Multi-Hop QA and Deep Research, and the gain is much larger on the longer-horizon setting.

# A Related Work  

> RLVR and agentic RLVR.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026) trains language models from automatically checked outcomes and has produced strong reasoning policies in mathematics, coding, and other rule-based tasks. PPO remains a general policy-gradient backbone (Schulman et al., 2017), while GRPO removes the learned value model by estimating advantages from response groups (Shao et al., 2024). Later variants alter the granularity of importance weighting (Zheng et al., 2025b) or organize comparisons within larger groups (Feng et al., 2025). RLVR has also moved from complete reasoning responses to agents that interleave language, tools, and environment observations (Yao et al., 2022). In this setting, a single terminal advantage supervises a sequence of search queries, tool calls, and answer decisions (Jin et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025d; Dong et al., 2026). TreeOPSD focuses on this turn-structured regime and derives intermediate targets directly from verifiable outcomes rather than introducing a learned critic or process labels.  

> Tree search and tree-based policy optimization.

Tree search lets language models compare continuations from a shared history instead of drawing only independent complete trajectories. At inference time, Tree-of-Thought (Yao et al., 2023), language-agent search (Zhou et al., 2023), and MCTS-style procedures (Feng et al., 2023) use branching to improve planning or answer selection. Offline methods turn reasoning trees into supervised or preference data (Lai et al., 2024), while online methods bring the tree into RL for exploration, value estimation, local comparisons, or rollout allocation (Hou et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025b; Zong et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2026a). These approaches differ in where branches are collected and how sibling outcomes become gradients. TreeOPSD takes a shared-prefix tree as input, backs terminal evidence into turn credit, and converts that credit into a finite target for the current policy. Its contribution lies in optimizing the acquired tree rather than choosing where the tree should branch.  

> Sample-efficient RLVR and rollout allocation.

A parallel line of work reduces rollout cost by deciding which examples receive sampling budget. Offline curation fixes the training set before optimization (Ye et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025). Online methods filter prompts whose rollouts are uninformative (Yu et al., 2026; Bae et al., 2026) or predict prompt difficulty before sampling (Zheng et al., 2025c; Qu et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2026a; Qu et al., 2026b), much as automated model evaluation predicts test accuracy from unlabeled data (Peng et al., 2024). POPO replays effective groups from earlier policies in place of zero-variance groups (Mao et al., 2026b), and budget allocation varies the number of rollouts per prompt (Li et al., 2025b; Yao et al., 2026). In supervised fine-tuning, online batch selection scores samples by utility and diversity (Zou et al., 2025a). TRACE extends allocation from prompt roots to turn-level prefixes with a shared success predictor (Zou et al., 2026a). These methods decide which rollouts to acquire, and TreeOPSD decides how an acquired tree updates the policy, so the two compose without changing the teacher in Section 3.2.  

> Credit assignment under sparse rewards.

Credit assignment under delayed rewards is a long-standing RL problem. Temporal-difference learning (Sutton et al., 1998), potential-based shaping (Ng et al., 1999), and return decomposition (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019) provide denser signals by estimating state values or redistributing terminal returns. For language-model reasoning, process supervision and process reward models score intermediate steps (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), and recent work derives progress signals from Monte Carlo continuations, answer likelihoods, or implicit process objectives (Setlur et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024). Long-horizon LLM methods further use trajectory graphs (Li et al., 2026a), vine-style continuations (Kazemnejad et al., 2025), or alternative reward aggregation (Cheng et al., 2026) to localize responsibility within a rollout. Rollout trees (Ji et al., 2026; Zong et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025b) provide a related source of counterfactual evidence because sibling suffixes keep the prefix fixed while varying the future. TreeOPSD backs this evidence through the sampled tree and preserves value shared by multiple useful continuations instead of removing it through sibling centering.  

> Behavior-relative targets and posterior distillation.

KL-regularized RL (Ouyang et al., 2022), maximum-a-posteriori policy optimization (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), and preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024) improve a policy while anchoring it to a reference distribution. Mirror-descent and mirror-ascent analyses 

> 17

make the corresponding target-and-projection structure explicit (Beck & Teboulle, 2003; Vaswani et al., 2022; Asad et al., 2025), and recent work applies this view to group-based language-model updates on finite response sets (Qu et al., 2026a). On-policy distillation instead trains on student-generated samples using a teacher distribution (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026) or privileged continuation information (Nguyen et al., 2026). TreeOPSD joins these perspectives at the turn level. It represents each observed turn by a behavior-relative Bernoulli, related to relative density-ratio estimation (Yamada et al., 2013; Cranmer et al., 2015) and contrastive source classification (Goodfellow et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2024), then obtains a closed-form teacher from backed-up credit and matches it with Forward KL. Independent turn targets avoid treating a sparse sibling sample as a closed continuation simplex, while the finite teacher supplies a destination that fixed-credit policy gradients do not specify.

# C Discussion 

C.1 Tree acquisition, credit assignment, and policy optimization 

Tree-based agent RL involves three decisions that are often coupled in one algorithm. A rollout procedure first decides which prompts or prefixes receive continuations. A credit-assignment rule then maps terminal outcomes on that tree to turn values. Policy optimization uses those values to update the current LLM. TreeOPSD handles the last step: it takes a sampled tree and normalized child weights, converts the backed-up values into behavior-relative targets, and matches the current policy to those targets. The same target construction can be paired with random branching, learned rollout allocation, or other tree samplers. 

C.2 Independent turn targets and pairwise preferences 

A categorical distribution over sampled siblings is invariant to a common shift of their logits or rewards. It can rank the observed continuations, but it removes value shared by all of them. This is appropriate when the sampled set is treated as a closed choice set. In an LLM, however, a few sampled siblings cover only a small part of the continuation space. If every observed sibling is useful relative to the behavior policy, a sibling-normalized update cannot raise them together. TreeOPSD instead assigns each observed turn its own behavior-relative Bernoulli target, allowing the shared policy to increase all useful turns while retaining their relative differences. Each target describes where one observed turn should move relative to its behavior likelihood. These targets do not form a normalized action distribution over the full vocabulary or continuation space. Step-level preference optimization is a boundary case. For two siblings with centered credits 𝑉 + = 𝑣 and 𝑉 − = −𝑣 , the TreeOPSD behavior-policy tangent is proportional to the usual pairwise direction ∇ log 𝜋 (𝑎 +) − ∇ log 𝜋 (𝑎 − ). The objectives differ after that initial point. A hard pairwise preference keeps enlarging the preferred margin, whereas TreeOPSD assigns both turns finite soft targets and stops when those targets are reached. When the pair has nonzero mean credit, TreeOPSD also retains the common update that pairwise centering removes. 

C.3 Finite-target feedback under sequential optimization 

Rollout batches are generated by a frozen behavior policy, but later optimizer blocks run after earlier blocks have already changed the shared LLM parameters. A fixed-credit surrogate continues to apply the collection-time coefficient 

𝑉 𝑖 even when the likelihood of turn 𝑖 has already moved. TreeOPSD keeps the teacher fixed and recomputes the student probability, replacing 𝑉 𝑖 by the residual Γ𝑖 = 𝜏 (𝑞 𝑖 − 𝑝 𝜃 ,𝑖 ). The first block therefore follows the tree-policy-gradient tangent. Later blocks reduce, stop, or reverse the update according to the current student–teacher gap. This feedback differs from importance-ratio clipping. Clipping limits how strongly a sampled action can move, but it does not specify a destination. The TreeOPSD teacher supplies that destination in a behavior-relative coordinate. The 

> 21

radius 𝜌 bounds the target probability and, with it, the geometric-mean likelihood ratio of the observed turn. It sets the finite target that later blocks pursue after updates on other prompts have already moved the shared policy. 

C.4 Limitations and future work  

> Allocation-aware teachers.

Theorems 1 and 2 condition on the sampled tree, so TreeOPSD can train on trees from learned allocators such as DPS (Mao et al., 2026a), GPS (Qu et al., 2026b), and TRACE (Zou et al., 2026a), while its teachers still come only from verifier outcomes and behavior likelihoods. The allocator also sets the scale of those teachers. Under the Samuelson calibration, the root-mean-square of 𝑞 𝑖 𝑘 − 1/2 over the 𝐾 leaves equals 𝜌 /√𝐾 − 1whenever the rewards are not all equal, about 0 .075 at the default 𝜌 = 0.25 and 𝐾 = 12, and only a tree with a single dissenting reward reaches the full radius. Allocators that favor mixed outcomes move trees toward balanced rewards, and budget allocators vary 𝐾 across prompts (Li et al., 2025b; Yao et al., 2026). A radius set from the realized credits or from 𝐾 would let allocation and teacher scale be designed together. It keeps both theorems, since the first-block tangent does not depend on 𝜏 and Theorem 1 only needs every 𝑞 𝑖 in (0, 1). 

> Tree replay.

Each tree currently serves 𝐸 = 4 optimizer blocks before it is discarded. POPO saves rollouts by replaying effective groups from earlier policies in place of zero-variance groups (Mao et al., 2026b), and the same replay fits TreeOPSD. A target ratio 𝑟 ★𝑖 is defined relative to the policy that collected its tree, so a replayed teacher still satisfies Eq. 12, and the residual 𝜏 (𝑞 𝑖 − 𝑝 𝜃 ,𝑖 ) shrinks or reverses on turns that the current policy has already moved toward or past their targets. The residual also scores stored turns with one forward pass, which could drive utility- and diversity-aware batch selection over a replay buffer (Zou et al., 2025a). Since each 𝑉 𝑖 averages continuations from the collecting policy, replayed credit ages as the policy improves, and weighting stored trees by their off-policiness, as POPO does for groups, would account for this aging.  

> Continual agentic post-training.

A tree whose rewards all coincide has 𝑞 𝑖 = 1/2 at every turn, so in later blocks it pulls its own turns back toward 𝜋 𝜃 old , much as Learning without Forgetting distills the previous model’s outputs (Li & Hoiem, 2017). On-policy RL forgets less than SFT, and its forgetting tracks the KL shift on the new task (Shenfeld et al., 2026). TreeOPSD shares the on-policy sampling behind this result, and its teachers also stay within 𝜌 of 1 /2. RL post-training still forgets across task sequences, however, because KL measured on current-task data does not control drift on prior-task distributions (Luo et al., 2026). Mixing a few stored trees from earlier environments, together with their logged behavior probabilities, into later batches would extend the 𝑞 𝑖 = 1/2 anchor to prior tasks without new rollouts. On the parameterization side, continual-learning methods mitigate the stability–plasticity dilemma through parameter regularization (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), fly-olfactory-circuit designs (Zou et al., 2025b, 2026b), and guided random projections (Li et al., 2026b). FlyLoRA limits inter-task interference in LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) by using a frozen sparse random projection as an implicit router over rank-wise experts (Zou et al., 2025c), and TreeOPSD applies to such parameterizations unchanged because it only reweights token log-likelihood gradients. The residual already accounts for drift among prompts within a batch (Appendix C.3), and a decorrelated parameterization would limit interference across environments that never share a batch.

# E Experimental and implementation details 

We follow Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2026a) for Multi-Hop QA and OpenResearcher (Li et al., 2026d; Tao et al., 2026) for Deep Research. 

E.1 Datasets, environments, and rewards 

E.1.1 Multi-Hop QA  

> Training data and environment.

We use all 90,447 questions from the HotpotQA distractor training split (Yang et al., 2018). The agent searches a local E5 retrieval server (Wang et al., 2022) built over a Wikipedia dump (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Each tool call accepts up to four queries and returns the top three passages for each query. We cap a rollout at five assistant turns, including the final answer, which leaves at most four tool-calling turns.  

> Evaluation benchmarks.

We evaluate on the 7,405-question HotpotQA validation split, the 12,576-question 2WikiMultiHopQA development split (Ho et al., 2020), the 2,417-question MuSiQue development split (Trivedi et al., 2022), and all 125 Bamboogle test questions (Press et al., 2023).  

> Reward function.

Training uses thresholded token F1. A parsed answer receives its token-level F1 when that score is at least 0 .3 and receives zero otherwise. Validation records both F1 and exact match. Table 1 and the training curves use exact match (EM). 

E.1.2 Deep Research  

> Training data and environment.

OpenResearcher provides 16 synthesized trajectories for each of 6,102 MiroVerse questions (Li et al., 2026d). We keep one prompt per unique question and do not place the released teacher responses in the policy input. Training and BrowseComp-Plus (Chen et al., 2025b) evaluation use closed-corpus retrieval. BrowseComp (Wei et al., 2025), GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), and xbench-DeepSearch (Chen et al., 2025a) use the same search, open, and find tools with a Serper backend.  

> Evaluation benchmarks.

Final evaluation covers all 830 BrowseComp-Plus test questions, 1,266 BrowseComp test questions, 103 text-only questions from the GAIA development split, and 100 xbench-DeepSearch test questions. To limit temporal variation in live search, we interleave the open-web methods within the same evaluation window and archive the raw search responses with each generation.  

> Reward function.

The training reward is exact match, with a 0 .1 bonus for a well-formed final-answer span. Table 1 reports exact match without that bonus. No evaluation question from either setting is used for policy optimization. 

E.2 Models 

The main experiments use Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 (Yang et al., 2025a). Appendix D.1 also trains Qwen3-1.7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) evaluates the corresponding initialization in the same agent interface. The model repositories are:

E.3 Training and evaluation settings  

> Optimization and systems.

All runs use bfloat16 and SGLang on one eight-GPU node. Qwen3-4B uses FSDP. Qwen3-30B-A3B uses FSDP for Multi-Hop QA and FSDP2 for Deep Research, with rollout tensor parallelism of four on 96GB H20 GPUs. We train for 100 policy updates with AdamW, a constant learning rate of 10 −6, and weight decay 0 .01. The Adam coefficients are (0.9, 0.999 ) for Multi-Hop QA and (0.9, 0.98 ) for Deep Research. We use no auxiliary KL penalty or entropy regularization, and sample training rollouts with temperature 1 .0 and top-𝑝 1.0. Appendix D.4 repeats the Qwen3-4B comparison with five training seeds. TreeOPSD uses 𝑀 = 4, 

𝑁 = 2, likelihood-weighted backup, target radius 𝜌 = 0.25, and 𝐸 = 4 sequential Forward-KL optimizer blocks, with unique-turn reconstruction.  

> Task-specific settings.

For Multi-Hop QA, collection uses 256 prompts split into four optimizer blocks of 64. Prompt and response lengths are capped at 2,048 and 6,144 tokens, retrieved observations at 16,384 characters, and each rollout at five assistant turns including the answer. Evaluation decodes one answer per question every five updates with temperature 0 .6 and top-𝑝 0.95. For Deep Research, collection uses 128 prompts split into four blocks of 32, with prompt, response, and tool-observation limits of 4,096, 16,384, and 2,048 tokens. Training allows 60 assistant turns. BrowseComp-Plus evaluation allows 80 and greedily decodes one trajectory per question every five updates.  

> Evaluation and checkpoint selection.

We evaluate every five policy updates through step 80 on Multi-Hop QA and step 70 on BrowseComp-Plus, and report one checkpoint per run in that window. In Figure 8, the serialized trajectory stores two chat entries per assistant decision, so we divide both evaluation and training turn counts by two. The training-turn curve averages the rollouts collected at each checkpoint. Final Deep Research evaluation uses that checkpoint with one rollout per question, exact match against the short reference, at most 200 assistant turns, and a 131,072-token response budget. The reported average weights the four datasets equally. 

E.4 Baselines and rollout-budget control  

> Controlled methods.

ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) evaluates the shared initialization without parameter updates. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) standardizes terminal rewards over complete rollouts from each prompt. GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025b) replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio while retaining group-relative terminal advantages. TreePO uses the same initialize-then-expand trees, likelihood-weighted credits 𝑉 𝑖 , unique-turn reconstruction, prompt normalization, and optimizer blocks as TreeOPSD, but applies the fixed ascent coefficient 𝑉 𝑖 /𝐿 𝑥 throughout optimization instead of matching a finite posterior target. Every trainable method applies four actor optimizer blocks per collected prompt batch.  

> Rollout budget.

GRPO and GSPO use 𝐺 = 8 rollouts per prompt. TreePO and TreeOPSD begin with 𝑀 = 4complete trajectories, select 𝑁 = 2 branch-anchor occurrences from each, and sample one fresh terminal suffix per occurrence. Keeping the roots gives 𝑀 (𝑁 + 1) = 12 terminal leaves. A continuation randomly starts from an intermediate prefix, so its expected generation length is roughly half that of a root rollout, and the expected cost 

𝑀 (1 + 𝑁 /2) = 8 matches 𝐺 . Appendix D.3 compares the methods at equal training time.
