LLM agents for coding, search, and workplace tasks increasingly rely on long-context capabilities to effectively aggregate and reason over extended interaction histories. Recent work has incorporated agent trajectories into mid-training stage, drawing on their naturally long and interaction-rich structure. Yet how to organize the information within these trajectories into effective mid-training supervision remains underexplored. In this work, we investigate the relationship between long-context and agent atomic capabilities and introduce TRAJLONG, a novel framework that compiles trajectories into long-context training tasks with dense supervision, targeting three representative atomic capabilities: evidence grounding, cross-evidence aggregation, and temporal state mainte-nance. We mid-train Qwen3-14B-Base and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base with data compiled by TRAJLONG, followed by supervised fine-tuning. Experiments on six long-context and twelve agent benchmarks demonstrate broad performance gains, with controlled ablations showing improvements over raw and masked trajectory baselines. Capability-level analyses further reveal task-dependent associa-tions between long-context and agent atomic capabilities. These findings suggest that the shared capability demands of long-context reasoning and agent execution provide a principled basis for designing mid-training data to develop downstream agent capabilities. 

1. Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly expected to serve as agents in a wide range of real-world applications including coding, search, and workplace tasks (Liu et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2024). Completing these long-horizon user tasks requires LLMs to integrate informa-tion across many interaction turns, making effective use of long context essential to agent execu-tion (Jimenez et al., 2024; Su et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Agent trajectories capture these extended interaction histories and provide a natural source of agent-oriented supervision. Recent work incorporates such supervision into mid-training, exposing base models to tool-use patterns and evidence-guided navigation before subsequent adaptation (Jiang et al., 2026; Su et al., 2025). Be-yond teaching interaction patterns, these trajectories also offer rich long-context training material for developing the foundational capabilities required for agent execution. The challenge is how to turn this rich but heterogeneous trajectory information into effective mid-training supervision. Applying next-token prediction to complete trajectories computes loss on both agent outputs and tool observations, introducing noisy tool responses into the training targets (Chen et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025b; Zeng et al., 2026). This further motivates moving beyond aggregate long-context scores to examine which long-context capabilities are relevant to different agent atomic abilities and how they can guide the organization of trajectory data. We therefore ask: How can the information dependencies within agent trajectories be organized into effective long-context supervision for agentic mid-training? 

We introduce TRAJLONG , a capability-targeted framework for agentic mid-training (Figure 1). It reorganizes agent trajectory context and constructs questions and target responses around the underlying information dependencies, targeting evidence grounding (Retrieval), cross-evidence ag-gregation (MultidocQA), and temporal state maintenance (State Tracking). We apply next-token prediction across all tokens in the compiled context, question, and target response. We perform capability-targeted mid-training on Qwen3-14B-Base and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, fol-lowed by SFT, achieving gains over SFT-only baselines across diverse long-context benchmarks and search, tool-use, and coding agent benchmarks. Figure 1 (right) summarizes post-SFT performance of the Qwen3-14B variants on seven selected benchmarks. Matched-budget Qwen3-14B ablations further show that the combined variant outperforms both unmasked and masked raw-trajectory mix-tures. Our analyses during mid-training and after SFT reveal overlapping long-context and agent gains with task-dependent learning dynamics. Evidence-based QA and dependency reasoning are associated with code understanding and planning, while state-oriented supervision may also sup-port aggregation and structured reasoning beyond temporal tasks, suggesting shared information-processing demands between long-context tasks and agent execution. Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We introduce TRAJLONG, a trajectory com-pilation framework targeting evidence grounding, cross-evidence aggregation, and temporal state maintenance for long-context agentic mid-training. (2) We demonstrate broad downstream improve-ments on both 14B and 30B models across long-context reasoning, search, tool use, and coding agent tasks. (3) We characterize task-dependent correlations between long-context performance and agent atomic capabilities, and examine the utility of compiled supervision across mid-training and SFT. 

2. Related Work 

Long-context data construction. Natural long-form corpora are widely used for long-context train-ing, but long documents are unevenly distributed across data sources (Fu et al., 2024). Existing work constructs long contexts through query-based grouping (Gao et al., 2025a), reference-linked document packing (Tao et al., 2026; Zhuang et al., 2025), and hard-negative insertion (Gao et al., 2025b). A complementary line develops synthetic QA supervision for retrieval and multi-evidence reasoning (Li et al., 2024a,b; Peng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Xu et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025). These approaches draw primarily on documents and webpages. ACC (Su et al., 2026) compiles evidence from search, software-engineering, and SQL agent trajecto-ries into shuffled long contexts for question answering, demonstrating the value of agent trajectories as a training source. We further investigate capability-targeted trajectory compilation and the links between long-context capabilities and agent atomic capabilities. 

Long-context mid-training. Mid-training, commonly implemented as continued pretraining (CPT), is widely used to develop long-context capabilities before downstream adaptation (Mo et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2025). Alongside positional adaptation for context-window extension (Ding et al., 2024; Lian et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2025), data-centric studies examine domain mixtures and sequence-length sampling (Fu et al., 2024; Runwal et al., 2026). 3.2. Trajectory Preprocessing 

We collect agent trajectories from real-world software-development and workplace tasks and stan-dardize them into a unified interaction format. We replace sensitive identifiers with consistent, type-appropriate surrogates to preserve within-trajectory references and exclude harmful tasks and tra-jectories with unresolved privacy or confidentiality risks. We combine rule-based checks with LLM assessment to screen for format validity, task complete-ness, and interaction quality. We reject traces with excessive repetition, errors, or low-value actions, while treating error recovery and progressive exploration as high-value patterns. We also remove formatting noise introduced by agent harnesses, clarify user instructions, and refine tool-response content and formatting. Retained trajectories are assigned to code or search domains according to task intent and tool-use patterns. Detailed criteria are provided in Appendix A.1.

3.4. Long-Context Mid-training 

For mid-training, we combine a fixed 30B-token background corpus with 10B tokens of compiled trajectory data, yielding approximately 40B training tokens in total. The background corpus covers code and repositories, books and papers, web data, math and STEM, and instructions and logs. Compiled variants use Retrieval, MultidocQA, or State Tracking individually, or mix them at a 3:4:3 ratio by input-token count (TRAJLONG-All), with matched token budgets and optimization settings. We group compiled examples sharing a context into training records, placing the context once before 𝑀 QA pairs: 𝑥 = serialize (𝑐, 𝑞 1, 𝑦 1, . . . , 𝑞 𝑀 , 𝑦 𝑀 ). We optimize Eq. (1) with 𝑚 𝑡 = 1 at all valid prediction positions, supervising the context and all questions and target responses. Mid-training uses 256K contexts and is followed by the same downstream SFT recipe across variants. Training details and corpus statistics are provided in Appendices D.1 and A.2. 

4. Experimental Setup 

Models and Mid-training. We conduct controlled experiments with Qwen3-14B-Base and addition-ally train TRAJLONG-All on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. Following Section 3.4, each mixture contains 30B background tokens and 10B trajectory-derived tokens. We train with ProLong (Gao et al., 2025c) at 256K sequence length, using a RoPE base of 1.6×10 7 without additional scaling. Mid-training uses AdamW, a peak learning rate of 4×10 −5, cosine decay, 10% warmup, and a global batch size of 64 packed sequences. Further training details are provided in Appendix D.1. 

Downstream SFT. We construct our SFT data mixture from Nemotron-SFT-Agentic-v2, Nemotron-SFT-SWE-v3.5, and UltraChat-200K, with supervision restricted to assistant reasoning, responses, and tool calls. SFT uses 128K sequences while retaining the 256K positional configuration used for mid-training, with AdamW, a peak learning rate of 2×10 −5, cosine decay, 3% warmup, and a global batch size of 32 packed sequences. Main 14B comparisons use two effective epochs; the direct-SFT baseline starts from Qwen3-14B-Base with the same positional configuration and SFT recipe. Data mixture and training details appear in Appendices A.3 and D.1. 

Evaluation. To probe capabilities learned during mid-training before SFT, we adapt long-context benchmarks into ICL tasks ( Long ICL ) targeting long-context atomic capabilities. Drawing on APT-Bench (Qin et al., 2025), we also implement Agent ICL to assess base models’ agent atomic capabil-ities. These evaluations, together with general ICL, use greedy completion or candidate likelihood scoring, with a default generation limit of 1,024 tokens. After SFT, we evaluate long-context, search, tool-use, and coding agent benchmarks. Our downstream evaluation runs use task-level defaults of temperature 0.7, top-𝑝 1.0, and a 65,536-token output cap per model call, subject to benchmark-specific settings. We report average and best-of-three metrics (Avg@3 and BoN@3), with score transformations and repeated-trial aggregation defined in Appendix C. More details about baselines and benchmarks are presented in Appendices B and E.3.

5. Experimental Results 

5.1. Main Results 

Following mid-training and SFT, we evaluate TRAJLONG on Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-30B-A3B against SFT-only counterparts and frontier LLMs. We assess long-context performance and downstream search, tool-use, and coding tasks, comparing the three specialized 14B variants with their mixture to examine task-specific strengths and broader gains. Additional results are provided in Appendix E. 

Capability-Targeted Compilation Improves Long-Context Performance. Table 1 shows distinct long-context strengths across the three compilation schemes. Among the Qwen3-14B variants, Re-trieval leads on MRCR, improving Avg@3 by 4.99 percentage points over SFT without mid-training. 

MultidocQA performs best on LongBench V2 and GraphWalks, while State Tracking achieves the strongest GSM-Infinite results, with a 4.78-point Avg@3 gain over the same baseline. These task-dependent differences are consistent with the distinct information dependencies emphasized during compilation. Combining the three capabilities in TRAJLONG-All improves both Avg@3 and BoN@3 over SFT-only on nearly all benchmarks for Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-30B-A3B. These findings sug-gest that compiling agent trajectories into tasks targeting atomic capabilities can improve general long-context performance, with each scheme exhibiting distinct task-specific strengths. 

Long-Context Gains Transfer to Downstream Agentic Tasks. Table 2 shows improvements across search, tool use, and coding under the same downstream SFT recipe. For Qwen3-14B, each spe-cialized compilation scheme outperforms SFT without mid-training on all 12 agent benchmarks. Combining the three schemes in TRAJLONG-14B-All yields the strongest SWE-Verified and SWE-Pro results among the 14B and 30B variants. 5.3.1. Capability-Aligned ICL Learning Dynamics 

Figure 4 shows that long-context ICL gains generally rise before leveling off. Retrieval reaches its highest observed MRCR gain at the first evaluated checkpoint (approximately 5B tokens), whereas

Retrieval improves MRCR sharply early in training, whereas MultidocQA on BrowseCompLong and State Tracking on GraphWalks + LongBench V2 achieve their largest observed gains later, despite intermediate fluctu-ations. 6. Conclusion 

We presented TRAJLONG, a framework that compiles agent trajectories into long-context supervision targeting evidence grounding, cross-evidence aggregation, and temporal state maintenance. Exper-iments on Qwen3-14B and 30B show gains across long-context and agent benchmarks, comparing with both raw and masked trajectories. Our analyses reveal task-dependent learning dynamics and correlations between long-context performance and agent atomic capabilities, with evidence-based QA and dependency reasoning associated with code understanding and planning. State-oriented su-pervision may also support aggregation and structured reasoning beyond temporal tasks. Together, these findings support organizing trajectory information around long-context capabilities as a basis for effective agentic mid-training rather than being left entirely to post-training.

A. Dataset Details 

A.1. Trajectory Sources and Cleaning Sources and Permissions. Our source corpus consists of agent trajectories generated while carrying out real-world user tasks in software development and workplace settings. Each trajectory records user requests, assistant responses, and tool interactions. All data used in this study were collected with users’ knowledge and authorization, with the necessary permissions and licenses obtained for their use in this research. 

Privacy Protection. We apply three complementary forms of privacy protection: user-level de-identification, organization-level exclusion, and task-level semantic privacy screening. 

User-level de-identification. The replacement pipeline targets three groups of sensitive informa-tion: 

• Personal identifiers: user identifiers, names, email addresses, phone numbers, identity numbers, and payment-card numbers. 

• Authentication material: API keys, access tokens, embedded passwords, private keys, and database connection strings. 

• Environment-specific identifiers: personal filesystem paths, version-control identity fields, IP addresses, and internal domains. Detection combines pattern matching with contextual checks, allowlists, and format or checksum validation where applicable. It operates on message text, including tool observations, as well as reasoning fields and string values in structured tool-call arguments. Detected values are replaced with randomly generated, type-appropriate synthetic values. Repeated values of the same category within a trajectory receive consistent replacements, preserving references across messages and tool interactions. 

Organization-level exclusion. We discard entire trajectories containing prominent company iden-tifiers or involving internal documents. The unit of removal is the complete trajectory, including its user requests, assistant responses, and tool observations, so that associated organizational content is excluded together with the identifying material. 

Task-level semantic privacy screening. Sensitive information can also be conveyed through task content after explicit identifiers are removed (Mireshghallah et al., 2024). We therefore use an LLM to assess whether a task involves sensitive personal information or confidential company projects. Trajectories flagged by this semantic screening are discarded in full. This criterion addresses privacy and confidentiality in the task’s meaning and context, complementing explicit identifier replacement and organization-level filtering. 

Task Safety. Separately, an LLM assesses task objectives for harmful intent, including malicious cyberattacks, phishing, fraud, and dangerous weapon-related instructions. It distinguishes such ob-jectives from routine software development and data processing. Trajectories labeled unsafe are excluded in full. Task safety and privacy are separate exclusion criteria: a task with a benign objec-tive may still contain sensitive personal or organizational information.

A.4. Benchmark Decontamination 

We screen the raw agent trajectories, generated question–answer (QA) examples, approximately 30B-token background corpus, and downstream SFT data against benchmark test data. Screening combines 𝑛 -gram matching with semantic matching to identify lexical overlap and semantically equiv-alent content, including rephrased test material. Both source trajectories and compiled QA examples are checked, covering the original interactions and their derived supervision. For trajectory-derived data, exclusion operates at the source-trajectory level. If a trajectory or any of its generated QA examples is flagged, we discard the entire trajectory together with all QA examples derived from it, rather than removing only the matched passage or individual QA pair. Matched records in the background corpus and SFT data are likewise excluded from their respective training mixtures. 

B. Benchmarks and Baselines 

This section describes the baseline models, followed by the long-context and agent benchmarks used for downstream SFT and mid-training ICL evaluation. 

B.1. Baseline Models GPT-OSS-120B. OpenAI’s gpt-oss-120b (Agarwal et al., 2025) is an open-weight mixture-of-experts reasoning model with approximately 117B total and 5.1B active parameters per token. It supports configurable reasoning effort and native tool calling. 

Gemini 2.5 Flash. Gemini 2.5 Flash (Comanici et al., 2025) is Google’s multimodal model designed to balance reasoning quality, latency, and computational cost. It supports a controllable thinking budget, long-context input, and tool use; our tables report the thinking configuration. 

QwenLong-L1.5-30B-A3B. QwenLong-L1.5 (Shen et al., 2025) builds on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, combining synthesized long-context reasoning tasks with specialized reinforcement learning. Its reported 256K context window is distinct from the memory-management framework used for longer inputs.

Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-30B-A3B. Both models belong to the Qwen3 family (Yang et al., 2025a) and support thinking and non-thinking modes. Qwen3-14B is a dense model with 14.8B parameters, whereas Qwen3-30B-A3B uses a mixture-of-experts architecture with 30.5B total and 3.3B active parameters. B.2. Downstream Long-context SFT Benchmarks 

For downstream SFT evaluation, we strictly follow the official evaluation protocols of the following six public long-context benchmarks. 

LongBench v2. LongBench v2 (Bai et al., 2025) evaluates long-context understanding and reason-ing through multiple-choice questions covering single- and multi-document QA, in-context learning, dialogue history, code repositories, and structured data. 

MRCR. Multi-round Co-reference Resolution (MRCR), described in Michelangelo (Vodrahalli et al., 2024), requires locating and reproducing a specified response among repeated, similar requests in a long conversation. The OpenAI release (OpenAI, 2025b) includes two-, four-, and eight-needle settings, testing retrieval with order-sensitive disambiguation. We compute scores separately for the 2-, 4-, and 8-needle settings and report their unweighted mean. 

GraphWalks. GraphWalks (OpenAI, 2025a) presents a directed graph as an edge list and asks for nodes satisfying a breadth-first-search or parent-retrieval query, testing dependency tracing over long structured inputs. We compute scores separately for the 0–128K and 128–256K context-length intervals and report their unweighted mean. 

Oolong-Synth. The synthetic component of Oolong (Bertsch et al., 2025) requires interpreting in-dividual text records and aggregating their properties to answer counting, comparison, and distribu-tional questions across the context. Results are reported in Table 15. 

GSM-Infinite (GSM-Inf ). GSM-Infinite (Zhou et al., 2025b) generates grade-school arithmetic prob-lems from computational graphs, allowing reasoning complexity and distractor-driven context length to be controlled separately. 

CorpusQA. CorpusQA (Lu et al., 2026b) evaluates corpus-level analysis that integrates evidence across many documents, including comparison and statistical aggregation, using synthesized ques-tions with programmatically verifiable answers.

D.2. Retrieval Data Construction 

Retrieval converts recorded search passages and file contents into exact-reproduction questions over the complete preprocessed trajectory. We extract candidate items, construct questions that distin-guish similar observations or repeated file interactions, align reference answers with the rendered context, and obtain and filter teacher-generated solutions. 

Evidence Extraction and Processing. We preserve the original interaction order and associate each candidate with its trajectory position and source metadata. Candidate extraction differs between search and code trajectories: 

• Search passages. We unpack structured search responses into individual passages, accommodat-ing passage, snippet, and content fields across tool formats. Results from the same search response form a candidate group in their original order. We retain the associated query, result titles, and passage positions. These groups provide topically related candidates without inserting synthetic distractors. 

• File contents. Read targets are file contents returned in tool observations, whereas write targets are content payloads supplied in tool-call arguments, rather than write acknowledgments. We nor-malize tool names, recover readable text from structured payloads, and remove system reminders. We match calls and responses only when their correspondence is unambiguous, recording the file path, operation type, and occurrence for each candidate. 

Question Construction. For search trajectories, an LLM summarizes each group’s query and result titles into a short topic phrase, falling back to the original query when needed. Eligible groups contain at least five passages; target passages must contain at least 100 characters and occur only once among the trajectory’s extracted search passages. We deduplicate candidate groups by topic and sample one eligible target per selected group. When more than 12 groups remain, we retain the 12 with the largest total eligible passage lengths. Questions specify the topic and the target’s original one-indexed position within its group. Seventy percent of search questions additionally require a 

> 31 TRAJLONG: Co-Designing Agentic and Long-Context Supervision for Mid-Training

random 10-character alphanumeric prefix before the reproduced passage, while the remaining 30% request the passage alone. We do not renumber passages after target filtering or add extra questions from an already selected group to fill the question budget. For code trajectories, questions specify the file path, read/write operation, and occurrence, dis-tinguishing repeated interactions with the same file. We sample read and write targets at a target ratio of 7:3, filling shortages from the other type. Requests may additionally require a random prefix before the reproduced content; 30% of code questions use this constraint. Both prefixed and plain requests ask for the recorded content, not a summary or a reconstructed file state. 

Context Rendering and Reference Alignment. We render the complete preprocessed trajectory without further truncating tool observations. Each search passage receives its group’s topic header, while file blocks retain explicit read/write cues. Reference answers are recovered from the rendered target, rather than copied from an earlier raw representation, so that formatting cleanup and con-sistent de-identification do not create answer–context mismatches. We require the rendered target to occur exactly once in the context and discard questions with missing or ambiguous alignments. Other trajectory content remains available as context and distractors. 

Solution Generation and Filtering. We use DeepSeek-v4-pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) as the teacher model to solve retrieval questions given the rendered trajectory. We compare the final answer ex-tracted from each solution with the reference answer, checking required prefixes and applying the same fidelity criteria to search and code questions. Exact matches and matches under Unicode and whitespace normalization are accepted directly. Otherwise, solutions are retained only when their final answers achieve a SequenceMatcher similarity of at least 0.95 with the reference. Lower-scoring solutions and failed generation requests are discarded. We append accepted question– solution pairs after the shared trajectory context and remove records with no surviving questions. D.3. MultidocQA Data Construction 

MultidocQA converts the preprocessed trajectories into questions that require combining evidence across sources. We first extract and organize the evidence, then construct and refine questions, and finally obtain teacher-generated solutions using the complete evidence collection. 

Evidence Extraction and Processing. We split each trajectory into interaction turns initiated by user messages. GPT-OSS-120B groups these turns according to continuity of the task, the objects being manipulated, and the resulting artifacts. Turns that develop the same feature or research deliverable, including follow-up requests and related debugging or verification, belong to the same group. A group may contain nonconsecutive turns when the user returns to an earlier task. We check that the groups cover all turns without duplication. Within each group, we extract file, document, and execution evidence from tool interactions. We check content validity and exclude empty or uninformative responses, inaccessible or removed content, binary data, visibly corrupted text, and fragments below minimum length thresholds. We then process each evidence type as follows: 

• File evidence. We extract code and configuration contents from file reads and write/edit opera-tions. We remove system reminders, terminal escape sequences, and non-printing control charac-ters, retaining the recorded code/configuration text and edit fragments. 

• Document evidence. We collect retrieved passages, fetched webpage text, and textual documents

read or produced during the task. We unpack structured responses to recover titles and text, removing tool wrappers, image markup, and URL or metadata clutter. 

• Execution evidence. We extract standard output and error text from command results, remove runtime-injected noise, and filter failure outputs without useful content. Previously observed contents of referenced scripts or files supplement the results when available. We retain groups with at least four distinct source keys, based on file paths, commands, or doc-ument content; versions of the same file count as one source. D.4. State Tracking Data Construction 

State Tracking converts coding-agent trajectories into questions about evolving file states and the origins of their contents. We reconstruct per-file histories, derive reference answers programmatically, and obtain teacher-generated solutions for two question families: Snapshot and Provenance .

Evidence Extraction and Processing. We deduplicate trajectories within each agent harness using normalized user requests and tool/file-event sequences, retaining more complete representatives when one trajectory is a prefix of another. Tool calls are aligned with their responses using call identifiers. We normalize file paths and operation names and retain message positions, operation arguments, and execution outcomes. Events are then organized into per-file timelines. Only ex-plicitly successful operations contribute to state updates; failed calls are not counted as successful modifications, and uncertain execution outcomes are not treated as confirmed updates. 

> 34 TRAJLONG: Co-Designing Agentic and Long-Context Supervision for Mid-Training

State Reconstruction and Verification. A reconstruction segment begins from a complete Write payload or a Read whose completeness can be established from its arguments and returned contents. Partial or truncated reads are not treated as complete initial states. We replay successful edits in order, checking that each replacement can be applied unambiguously and preserving untouched content. A Write replaces the complete file state. When available, subsequent complete reads are compared with the reconstructed state. We distinguish read-verified histories from deterministic histories supported only by complete Write or strict Read anchors, rather than requiring a later read for every sample. Unreplayable edits, uncertain execution outcomes, ambiguous same-file ordering, or inconsistent observations break the segment. Reconstruction can resume from a new complete anchor. Potential unobserved changes from shell commands or sub-agents are recorded as separate risk indicators. 

Question Construction. The two families share the reconstructed histories but query different as-pects of state evolution. Table 14 summarizes their seven question subtypes and corresponding answer formats: 

• Snapshot. Questions request the complete file immediately before or after a specified successful Edit, at the end of the retained history, or after rolling back a suffix of consecutive edits. Reference answers are the corresponding reconstructed snapshots. Rollback is restricted to the supported edit suffix rather than arbitrary deletion of an earlier operation. These questions require com-posing partial updates while preserving the rest of the file, not reproducing an individual edit payload. 

• Provenance. Questions identify the last operation that wrote a surviving fragment, the operation after which it first appeared, or the operation that removed it. During replay, content spans retain their source labels unless overwritten, while a full Write resets their provenance. Candidate frag-ments must have an unambiguous answer. Removal questions additionally require the fragment to exist immediately before the selected operation, disappear immediately afterward, and remain absent from all subsequent known states. Answers specify an operation type and its successful-operation index within the target file. Edit, Write, and Delete are counted separately from one, while an initial anchor uses index zero. We diversify question wording using Chinese and English template pools and LLM-based paraphras-ing, while keeping the target file, temporal boundary, operation-counting convention, and output requirements explicit and unchanged.
