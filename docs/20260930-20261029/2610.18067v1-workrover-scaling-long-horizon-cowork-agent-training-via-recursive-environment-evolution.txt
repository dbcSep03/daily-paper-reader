Abstract
Scaling interactive environments has become increasingly important for training long-horizon agents
through reinforcement learning. These environments often require further testing and refinement after
initial generation, and correcting defects in a task may also require updating its work context and ver-
ifier. However, existing construction pipelines offer limited support for such iterative refinement while
keeping these components aligned. In this paper, we introduce WorkRover, a framework for training
long-horizon cowork agents through recursive environment evolution. Its harness-driven synthesis en-
ables construction agents to jointly build and validate semantically aligned work contexts, executable
tasks, and evidence-grounded verifiers, adapting their actions to workspace state and execution feedback
rather than following a fixed construction workflow. To support environment evolution, we introduce
Factorial Compatibility Replay (FCR), which uses rollout feedback to evaluate combinations of component
revisions through dependency-closed replay and selects the smallest validated repair among tested can-
didates while preserving task requirements and evaluation constraints. To provide finer-grained training
signals, we propose Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment (VTCA), which augments trajectory-level GRPO
advantages with signals from deterministic workspace-progress checks and assigns a bounded share of
positive-progress credit to prerequisite turns using recorded tool-output and artifact dependencies. Us-
ing WorkRover, we post-train Qwen3.5-35B-A3B to obtain WorkRover-35B-A3B and evaluate it
on several cowork-agent benchmarks. Its reported scores exceed those of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B by 26.27
percentage points on JobBench, 29.32 percentage points on OfficeQA Pro, and 413.25 Elo points on
GDPval v2. These results suggest that WorkRover offers a promising foundation for scalable training
of long-horizon cowork agents.

Introduction
Cowork agents powered by large language models (LLMs) perform professional tasks across files, tools, and
applications [15]. These tasks unfold over long sequences of dependent actions, requiring agents to locate
relevant materials [17, 31, 37], track workspace changes, and maintain consistency across related artifacts
[3, 20, 32, 33]. Training these agents through reinforcement learning (RL) depends on environments that
support diverse, executable workflows and provide reliable feedback on task outcomes [5, 10, 11, 26, 41, 45, 49].
Recent work has explored automated environment and task synthesis to expand the range of workspaces and
tasks available for agent training [2, 18, 30].
However, expanding the number of synthesized environments does not by itself ensure reliable training feed-
back. In a cowork environment, the work context must support the task’s requirements, and the verifier must
assess the requested outcomes while accepting valid alternative solutions. Agent rollouts can expose missing
dependencies, inconsistent task specifications, or verification errors that remain after initial construction [47].
Such defects can make valid behavior appear unsuccessful or allow incorrect results to be rewarded. Repair-
ing these defects often requires coordinated revisions within a task environment: a correction to the work
context or task specification may also require updating the verifier to keep execution and evaluation aligned.
Existing construction pipelines offer limited support for maintaining this alignment throughout repeated
revisions. This motivates recursive environment evolution: repeatedly using execution feedback to identify
defects, coordinate component revisions, and revalidate the revised environment while preserving the original
task requirements and evaluation constraints. Reliable terminal evaluation also leaves a credit-assignment
challenge: useful steps can occur in failed trajectories and redundant actions in successful ones, while earlier
information-gathering turns may only contribute through later workspace changes [6, 34].
To this end, we introduce WorkRover, a framework for long-horizon cowork agent training that integrates
harness-driven environment synthesis, recursive environment evolution, and verifier-grounded policy learning
(Figure 2(b)). Its harness tracks component dependencies and enables construction agents to jointly build and
validate semantically aligned work contexts, executable tasks, and evidence-grounded verifiers. These agents
adapt their construction actions to workspace state and execution feedback rather than following a fixed
workflow. Independent reference executions from a clean initial workspace establish task feasibility, while
verifier validation tests incorrect outcomes and valid alternatives against instruction-grounded requirements
rather than a prescribed solution path. For environment evolution, we introduce Factorial Compatibility
Replay (FCR), which evaluates combinations of fixed repair proposals across the work context, task, harness,
and verifier. Each combination is rebuilt with its dependency-induced updates and tested under matched
rollout conditions.
FCR selects the smallest validated repair among the tested combinations, subject to
preserving task requirements and evaluation constraints and passing regression checks on affected tasks.
Fresh rollouts confirm selected repairs and provide evidence for subsequent evolution. For policy learning,
we introduce Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment (VTCA) to supplement trajectory-level GRPO advantages
with finer-grained feedback. Frozen deterministic checks measure progress and setbacks in recorded workspace
states, while tool-output and artifact dependencies identify prerequisite turns eligible for a bounded share of
positive-progress credit. This provides turn-level learning signals without intermediate judge calls.
We post-train Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with WorkRover and evaluate the resulting model on five external bench-
marks spanning occupational tasks, professional deliverables, cross-application workflows, and document-
grounded reasoning. WorkRover-35B-A3B exceeds the reported backbone scores on all five benchmarks,
with gains of 26.27 percentage points on JobBench, 29.32 percentage points on OfficeQA Pro, and 413.25 Elo
points on GDPval v2. Controlled experiments further examine the contributions of environment evolution
and verifier-grounded learning. Under matched repair budget caps, FCR improves task-package synthesis
pass rate by 6.0 percentage points over full regeneration. A separate maintenance evaluation shows that
FCR also resolves naturally occurring defects more reliably while reducing unintended changes to task intent.
Construction ablations also show that following file dependencies and matching files with compatible skills
improve task yield and executability, beyond assembling workspaces through topical similarity alone. With
the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) initialization, task and seed schedules, and rollout budget fixed, VTCA out-
performs episode-only GRPO across all five benchmarks. These findings support improving both environment
reliability and turn-level learning signals for long-horizon cowork agent training.

Our main contributions are:
1. Harness-driven construction of semantically aligned environments. We introduce a Harness-driven syn-
thesis pipeline that jointly constructs semantically aligned work contexts, executable tasks, and evidence-
grounded verifiers at scale. The Harness explicitly tracks their dependencies and validates task feasibility
and verifier consistency.
2. Recursive environment evolution. We introduce Factorial Compatibility Replay (FCR) to support iter-
ative environment repair under explicit component dependencies. FCR evaluates coordinated revisions,
identifies minimal validated repairs, and propagates changes across affected components while preserving
task intent and evaluation constraints.
3. Training from verifier-grounded progress. We introduce Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment (VTCA)
to augment terminal-reward GRPO with workspace-grounded advantage shaping. Deterministic checks
provide progress proxies, with bounded positive allocations to prerequisite turns through recorded tool
and artifact dependencies, without intermediate Judge calls.

Method
In this section, we present WorkRover (Figure 3), a framework for training long-horizon cowork agents
through recursive environment evolution. We first introduce harness-driven synthesis of semantically aligned
work contexts, tasks, and verifiers (Sec. 3.1).
We then present Factorial Compatibility Replay (FCR) for
rollout-driven recursive evolution (Sec. 3.2) and Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment (VTCA) (Sec. 3.3).

3.1.2
Long-Horizon Task Synthesis
Phase II synthesizes task Ti from the three Contexts in Ci (Figure 3).
A construction profile sets the
target edit scope (files), interaction horizon (turns), skill complexity (number and types of combined skills),
distractor ratio, and cross-environment dependencies (Table 4; Appendix B.4). We profile selected file and app
instances for task-relevant semantics, relations, and executability. The Harness H then assembles compatible
files, apps, and capabilities into a WorkEnv. Starting from a job skill, the Skill Matcher fits the task scope to
the WorkEnv’s available resources and combines job, file, and app skills into an internal workflow whose stages
depend on earlier outputs or state changes. Its dependency graph guides preparation of the Starter WorkEnv
xvi, including all required resources and marking editable and protected state. Task instructions specify
goals, materials, deliverables, and constraints. An independent Demonstrator Agent executes the task from
xvi; success confirms feasibility and yields a Reference State x⋆
i . The Task Instruction, Starter WorkEnv, and
Reference State form the candidate, with state differences providing evidence of completion. The workflow
and reference execution must not add requirements or exclude other valid solutions.

to test dependency necessity; independently confirmed compatibility problems trigger FCR (Section 3.2).
Candidates passing construction checks proceed to Phase III for Verifier synthesis and validation.
3.1.3
Verifier Synthesis and Validation
Phase III synthesizes and validates the Verifier Vi (Figure 3). We derive an Evaluation Contract from the task
instructions and agent-visible workspace conditions, fixing its criteria before inspecting the Reference State.
The Contract specifies expected outcomes, allowed edits, protected state, critical criteria, and admissible
evidence.
We use it to generate a rubric and verification rules, binding each criterion to evidence from
files, application states, or state differences.
Infrastructure Validity returns invalid when infrastructure
faults prevent evaluation; Canonical State Diff checks state changes against the Contract; Functional Checks
test artifact content and consistency; and Semantic Judge assesses criteria unresolved by executable checks.
Valid evaluations pass only if the weighted outcome score meets its threshold and all mandatory checks
pass; otherwise they fail. We calibrate Vi to reject the Starter State and negative cases that violate the
Contract, while accepting the Reference State and independently obtained valid alternatives. Independently
confirmed compatibility problems may trigger FCR, followed by recalibration under the same fixed Contract.
We then freeze Vi for a separate, one-time admission test; candidates that fail are rejected without feedback
for further repair. Passing candidates become admitted packages Pi containing the Task Instruction, Starter
WorkEnv, Reference State, rubric, and evidence, with accepted execution traces τi retained for training. See
Appendix C.1 for scoring and validation details.
3.2
Recursive Environment Evolution
The Work Context, Task, Harness, and Verifier must work together for a task to run and be evaluated
correctly. Factorial Compatibility Replay (FCR) repairs mismatches revealed during construction or later
5


agent rollouts.
Since changing one component can affect the others, FCR tests proposed changes both
individually and jointly, then selects the smallest validated repair that preserves the task’s original intent.
Diagnosis and proposals.
Independent diagnostics first distinguish environment problems from mistakes in
the agent’s decisions. Agent mistakes alone do not trigger environment changes. For an environment problem,
the Harness records the affected versions and dependencies, keeps task requirements and validation checks
fixed, and freezes one proposed repair per eligible component: ∆C, ∆T, ∆H, and ∆V as applicable. The
Evaluation Contract remains fixed once grounded in the task requirements. Edits visible to the agent must
preserve information boundaries, including keeping hidden evaluation evidence hidden. These edits must also
pass checks by a fixed panel of solvers for unintended task simplification.
Harness screening and replay.
For d ≤4 eligible components, each combination χ ∈{0, 1}d specifies which
proposals to apply: 1 applies a proposal and 0 omits it. This gives 2d combinations, at most 16. Before rebuild-
ing packages or running rollouts, the Harness removes combinations with confirmed conflicts in dependencies,
resource bindings, or requirements; uncertain cases proceed to validation. Each remaining combination is
expanded into a dependency-closed update set Γ(χ) = Close(χ). This set includes the selected repairs and
all additional updates needed to keep dependent components compatible, even if those components were not
directly selected for repair. The rebuilt packages are then replayed with the same agent policies, execution
budgets, and random seeds. Comparing individual and joint repairs reveals whether some changes work only
when applied together. If only the Verifier changes, all alternatives evaluate the same recorded evidence.
Repair selection.
Independent checks must confirm that the original problem is resolved, required outcomes
and information boundaries are preserved, and the repair introduces no new problems in affected task pack-
ages. These checks do not use agent success or scores from the proposed Verifier as evidence of resolution.
Once a Verifier exists, the repair must also pass the fixed Contract checks and tests confirming that the
Verifier rejects known failures and accepts valid alternative solutions. Among the tested repair combinations
that pass these checks, FCR selects χ⋆whose full update set Γ(χ⋆) changes the fewest component versions,
counting updates required by dependencies. Ties favor fewer rebuilt dependencies, then lower cost.
Confirmation and update.
Fresh rollouts with new random seeds confirm the selected repair. A candidate
that has not yet been admitted to the training collection repeats validation from the earliest affected construc-
tion step and must pass initial admission. For an already admitted package, all validated changes take effect
together after confirmation. If repair selection or confirmation fails, FCR discards an unadmitted candidate
or keeps the previous admitted version. Later rollouts can reveal new mismatches and initiate another repair
cycle. See Appendices C.2 and C.3 for details.
3.3
Verifier-to-Turn Credit Assignment
Terminal rewards score an entire rollout but provide little guidance about which turns made progress. VTCA
adds feedback from verified workspace changes and recorded dependencies (Figure 5).
Trajectory supervision.
We initialize the policy with verified demonstrations using SFT, then sample RL
rollouts for the same task and initial state. Among rollouts with valid evaluations, terminal rewards combine
Verifier outcome scores and trace scores. Rewards are zero if any critical criterion or integrity, protected-state,
or safety check fails. We standardize rewards within each group to obtain the trajectory advantage Atraj

Measuring progress.
Frozen deterministic checks [34] score supported criteria before and after each turn.
We subtract the earlier score from the later one, so increases indicate progress and decreases regression. If
either score is unavailable, we emit no local signal. Missing observations are never treated as zero completion.
Checks run on recorded states without intermediate Judge calls.

Assigning turn credit.
At least half of each positive increment stays with the turn that produced it. The
rest is divided equally among earlier turns whose tool outputs or exact artifact versions were directly used to
produce that progress, as confirmed by recorded dependencies. For example, a turn that reads a table may
share credit when a later turn uses that returned table to compute a checked result. Merely opening a file
does not qualify. If no earlier turn qualifies, all credit stays local. Negative increments remain entirely with
the turn that produced them.

4.1
Experimental Setup
Model and training data.
We post-train Qwen3.5-35B-A3B [25] to obtain WorkRover-35B-A3B. Super-
vised fine-tuning (SFT) uses 30,115 trajectories generated by Qwen3.5-397B-A17B in synthesized workspaces;
all pass task verification and quality checks. For reinforcement learning (RL), we independently synthesize
20,000 task–Verifier pairs and screen each pair with four Qwen3.5-35B-A3B pilot rollouts. Consistency filter-
ing retains 3,200 pairs for training. Starting from the SFT checkpoint, the model collects online rollouts on
these tasks and learns with VTCA-GRPO (Sec. 3.3). Subset-selection details are in Appendix D.1.

Main Results
WorkRover-35B-A3B improves on its Qwen3.5-35B-A3B backbone across all five external benchmarks (Ta-
ble 1). Among the percentage-based scores, the largest gains are on OfficeQA Pro (+29.32 percentage points)
and JobBench (+26.27 points), followed by AutomationBench (+22.67 points) and WorkBuddy Bench (Of-

span document reasoning, occupational tasks, professional deliverables, and application workflows, supporting
transfer beyond the synthesized training workspaces.
Among the listed 27–35B open-weight models, WorkRover has the highest reported score on every bench-
mark. Occamy-1.0 has the second-highest reported score in this group on each benchmark. The largest
percentage-point gap between the two models is on WorkBuddy Bench (Office): 74.13% versus 66.65%, a
margin of 7.48 points.
The JobBench margin is 2.62 points (36.89% versus 34.27%), while the leads on
AutomationBench and OfficeQA Pro are smaller, at 1.07 and 0.77 points, respectively.
Compared with larger and proprietary models, WorkRover’s relative performance varies across benchmarks.
It exceeds GLM-5.2 and MiniMax-M3 on both AutomationBench and OfficeQA Pro, and Kimi K3 on Work-
Buddy Bench (Office) (74.13% versus 72.18%). Gaps remain on occupational work and deliverable quality:
its JobBench score of 36.89% is below GPT-5.5 (38.30%) and Kimi K3 (54.27%), and its GDPval score of
1218.00 Elo is below all listed open-weight models above 400B (1303.96–1572.42 Elo). WorkBuddy perfor-
mance also remains below GPT-5.5 (86.05%) and Claude Opus 4.7 (92.51%). Comparisons with reference
models use their reported scores under source-specific evaluation settings.

4.3.2
Recursive Environment Evolution
We compare repair methods using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B under matched proposal and replay budget caps. Safe
Resolution Rate (SRR) measures how often a defect is fixed without causing new failures in related tasks;
repairs must also pass fresh-seed confirmation and a held-out audit. FCR raises SRR on naturally occurring
defects from 60.7% with full regeneration to 70.3% (Figure 7(b)). It also reduces changes to task intent from
14.3% to 1.3% while using fewer model tokens (relative cost: 1.00 versus 1.34).
Choosing compatible edits.
With repair proposals fixed, Table 2 tests selection separately from proposal
generation.
Compared with applying all edits (Apply-all), FCR raises SRR by 9.0 percentage points on
natural defects and 10.4 points on injected defects, and reduces cross-task regression from 7.0% to 0.7%.
Testing edit combinations thus improves repair reliability, at a higher model-token cost (1.00 versus 0.59).
Joint repair and repeated evolution.
With proposals fixed, 48–74% of safely resolved cases require joint
edits, depending on the component pair: no smaller subset of the selected edits passes the same repair checks
(Figure 7(a)). Across three generations, SRR stays at 70.3–72.4% and audit pass rate at 93.8–94.6% (panel c),
showing little change in repair quality across these rounds. FCR’s task success rate also increases over five
repair iterations (panel d).
Repairing initial candidates.
FCR also improves initial construction. With the candidate set and repair
budget caps fixed, synthesis pass rate reaches 74.8%, compared with 49.8% before repair and 68.8% with full
regeneration. Repair therefore recovers more tasks that pass validation from the same initial pool.

5
Conclusion
WorkRover uses execution feedback to improve both long-horizon cowork agents and the environments
used to train them. The Harness keeps work contexts, tasks, and verifiers aligned during construction and
repair. FCR revises inconsistent components while preserving task requirements, and VTCA uses verified
workspace progress to guide learning at individual turns. Experiments show that FCR increases the share
of tasks that pass validation and repairs defects with fewer unintended changes to task intent and fewer
regressions on related tasks. Under matched rollout budgets, VTCA outperforms episode-only GRPO on
all five benchmarks. These findings support recursive environment evolution as a practical foundation for
reliable long-horizon cowork agent training.

Extended Related Work
Executable environments and task synthesis.
Professional agent training requires executable tasks, recov-
erable state, and observable outcomes across related artifacts. AppWorld provides controllable application
environments; EnvScaler supports programmatic environment construction, while ClawGym materializes
workspaces around task and capability requirements [2, 30, 36]. Agent-World uses capability diagnostics
to expand tool/database environments [7]. ToolForge constructs virtual-tool tasks, and MetaTask and CLI-
Universe emphasize executable validation [4, 12, 18, 22]. These approaches organize synthesis around task
bundles, environment expansion, or capability requirements. WorkRover grounds task construction in file
relations and professional skills, then assembles Tasks and Verifiers within recoverable Work Contexts under
a versioned Harness. Shared contexts also make dependency tracking and revalidation necessary when one
component changes.
Grounded acceptance and verifier reliability.
Acceptance checks must agree with the instruction and the ev-
idence available in the workspace. ANCHOR constructs instructions, environments, solutions, and verifiers
from a shared workflow specification [13]. FACET grounds artifacts in shared state and repairs inconsistencies
while preserving source intent [29]. Hacker–Fixer audits tests and environments with adversarial cases while
checking legitimate solutions [47]. Stronger rejection checks therefore need to be considered alongside false re-
jection of valid alternatives. WorkRover freezes instruction-grounded requirements and criterion–evidence
bindings, then evaluates implementations using violating mutants and independently valid solutions. Refer-
ence artifacts supply recomputable anchors without adding requirements. Infrastructure-invalid episodes are
distinguished from policy failures. Validation remains limited to the declared Contract and the coverage of
the controls.
Environment evolution and repair selection.
Envs-FORGE studies joint revision of instructions, fixtures, so-
lutions, tests, and environments, while EchoVerse studies executable environment evolution [23, 39]. FACET
routes failed validation to implicated artifacts and repeats validation from a clean state [29]. Program repair
likewise studies patch minimization: GenProg reduces test-passing patches through structural differencing
and delta debugging [38]. FCR freezes component-level proposals over Context, Task, Harness, and Verifier
before replaying their combinations. Materialization includes dependency-induced changes, and promotion
checks affected retained tasks for regressions under fixed requirements. The selected repair is the smallest
admissible closed edit set among the tested combinations; bounded enumeration does not establish a global
minimum over all possible repairs. This separates proposal generation, compatibility selection, and validation
of a shared environment revision.
Local progress in long-horizon learning.
Methods differ in the evidence used to assign local feedback. GiGPO
uses state comparisons, while HiPER uses a planning hierarchy [8, 24]. TRACE forms temporal differences
in frozen-model reference-answer likelihoods, with bounded look-ahead for delayed tool effects [34]. TRCA
combines deterministic transition-rubric scores with increments in cumulative condition coverage [46]. VTCA
instead evaluates successive observable workspace states with frozen checkers. Signed progress can register
both completion and setbacks in persistent, mutable artifacts. Unsupported or unavailable evidence supplies
no local signal, while terminal evaluation remains applicable to supported task outcomes. These differences
concern the feedback source and its coverage; they do not by themselves establish the comparative effectiveness
of individual components.
Evidence attribution and shaping limits.
STAMP maps verified citations to first-exposure actions; DRACO
uses judge-attributed steps with sign-preserving GRPO advantage redistribution [9, 43].
VTCA follows
recorded direct use of exact tool outputs or artifact versions, assigning at most half of positive progress to
eligible prerequisite turns and leaving negative increments local. Access alone creates no dependency edge.
Incorrect provenance can misallocate credit, and missed links leave prerequisites unrepresented. Recorded
use therefore does not establish causal necessity. Potential-based reward shaping preserves optimal policies
under stated assumptions [19]; VTCA is formulated as workspace-grounded advantage shaping. Conservation
14


of checker increments before gating, costs, and clipping establishes neither policy invariance nor an unbiased
policy-gradient estimate.

B.1
Work Context Construction
For each source workspace w, the index I(w) stores a typed graph Gw = (Vw, Ew) with files or versions
as nodes and semantic, structural, temporal, provenance, and workflow edges. Directory co-location is only
a weak structural signal. A job skill specifies its professional setting, workflow, materials, and deliverables.
The Skill Matcher retrieves related file regions, then expands them through file and application skills with
compatible inputs, outputs, and interfaces. The Harness validates dependencies, permissions, bindings, reset,
recovery, and evidence capture. Recorded lineage and derived task branches stay in one data split.
Construction complexity.
Let J be the number of job skills, W the number of source workspaces, k the
number of shortlisted regions per job skill, and M ≤Jk the number of candidates retained for environment
assembly. Let Cindex and Cretrieve denote per-workspace indexing and per-job-skill retrieval costs; Cagent,
Cmatch, and Cenv denote per-candidate agent inspection, skill matching, and environment-assembly costs.
Excluding source collection and one-time pool construction, the total cost of indexed Work Context construc-
tion is
O(WCindex) + O(JCretrieve) + O(Jk(Cagent + Cmatch)) + O(MCenv).

C.2
FCR Mechanics and Promotion
Freeze one direct proposal per available Context/Task/Harness/Verifier factor before replay: at most 16
cells, or eight before compilation.
Each cell χ applies the dependency-closed update set Γ(χ), including
dependency-induced updates; changed tasks require fresh execution/compilation, and shared Harness edits
require affected-task checks. Established conflicts make cells unavailable; uncertain cells proceed to material-
ization. Candidate repair returns to the earliest affected gate; maintenance freezes the admitted parent. Both
preserve intent, outcomes, transitions, and applicable Contract weights, thresholds, flags, and gates. Hid-
den evidence cannot leak into visible edits; new tools require separate releases and semantic changes create
new candidates. Blinded equivalence review and a frozen solver panel apply development-fixed simplification
margins.
Matched Context–Task–Harness seeds produce evidence reused by Verifier variants. Health uses all seeds;
outcome contrasts use common healthy seeds on complete compatible subcubes, without imputation. Verifier
contrasts use sealed label agreement.
Selection requires a proposal-independent defect/health predicate,
proposal-disjoint replay, equivalence, no affected-task regression, and compiled-Verifier calibration plus a one-
use binary promotion shard. Reporting audits never select repairs. The smallest validated closed edit set
Γ(χ⋆) among tested cells receives fresh-seed confirmation (Section 3.2); failure discards candidates or retains
maintenance parents and quarantines unresolved triggers.
C.3
FCR Evaluation Protocol
Frozen stratified screening of the 10K pool targets 300 natural mismatches, classified by two blinded reviewers
with third-reviewer adjudication. The separate controlled cohort injects 260 concealed Contract-preserving
defects: 80 single-, 120 pair-, 40 triple-, and 20 four-factor cases. Candidate-controlled comparisons share
proposals; end-to-end comparisons allow independent proposals under matched caps. Each distinct Context–
Task–Harness configuration uses five matched seeds; compatible Verifiers reuse evidence. Confirmation seeds
and generation-specific promotion shards are disjoint. Joint necessity excludes passing strict proposal subsets.
Triggers are statistical units; seeds/cells are nested measurements.
