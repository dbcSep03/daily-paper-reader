ABSTRACT 

Large language models are increasingly trained as multi-turn agents that interact with external tools. However, outcome-only reinforcement learning can produce a form of misguided reinforcement : even on successful trajectories, undesirable local behaviors may receive positive token-level advantages and be favored by the policy update. To address this issue, we propose Confidence-Guided Credit Correction (C 3), a plug-in method for local credit correction. C 3 identifies spans corresponding to undesirable behaviors and selectively corrects positive credit af-ter advantage estimation, with the correction strength adaptively controlled by the behavior policy’s confidence in the corresponding behavior, while leaving the task reward and original credit estimation unchanged. Across multiple multi-turn benchmarks, C 3 improves performance under both critic-based and critic-free re-inforcement learning methods. Injecting the same behavioral signals as turn-level or outcome rewards does not consistently improve performance. Our results show that local credit correction provides an effective form of process supervision for outcome-driven RL. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-turn agentic reinforcement learning has emerged as an effective paradigm for training lan-guage agents on complex tasks that require long-horizon reasoning and iterative tool interac-tion (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Jian et al., 2026). A common approach optimizes agents with outcome-level rewards that evaluate the success of an entire interaction trajectory (Jin et al., 2025). However, outcome-level supervision provides only sparse and coarse-grained feedback for intermediate behaviors, making it difficult to assign appropriate credit across long-horizon interac-tions (Wang et al., 2025b;a; Lu et al., 2026). To provide denser supervision for intermediate be-haviors, recent work introduces process-level rewards and other process-level signals (Wang et al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). 

Our Key Observation. We inspect representative training trajectories under outcome-only training and find that undesirable local behaviors can still receive positive token-level advantages. We iden-tify this phenomenon as a form of misguided reinforcement . More precisely, it is a form of local credit misalignment, where the token-level advantage assigned to an undesirable behavior is incon-sistent with its process-level quality. This creates a fundamental mismatch between what behavior should be discouraged and what the policy update favors . Figure 1(a) shows two representative undesirable behaviors observed. Why Existing Credit Construction Falls Short. Figure 1(b) illustrates how token-level credit is constructed under two common optimization paradigms. In critic-based reinforcement learning, token-level advantages are estimated through returns and value functions, so a process-level penalty can propagate to tokens beyond the behavior that triggered it. In critic-free methods, relative advan-tages are derived directly from trajectory-level outcomes, causing local behaviors to inherit credit from the overall trajectory. Thus, the two paradigms differ in how credit is constructed, but share a more fundamental challenge: 

How can process-level supervision correct a localized undesirable behavior without altering the credit assigned to unrelated behaviors, thereby avoiding credit misalignment? 

To address this challenge, we propose C3 (Confidence-Guided Credit Correction) , a lightweight process-level credit correction module that directly operates on token-level advantages. Given un-desirable behaviors localized by external process-level supervision, C 3 selectively corrects positive advantages within the corresponding spans, with the correction magnitude adapting to the model’s confidence in the corresponding actions. C 3 introduces no additional process rewards and does not modify the underlying policy optimization objective, making it a plug-in module applicable to both critic-based and critic-free reinforcement learning paradigms. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify and characterize misguided reinforcement, where undesirable local behaviors can receive positive policy advantages under outcome-level training, creating a mismatch between behavioral quality and the direction of policy updates. • We propose C 3, a plug-and-play confidence-guided credit correction framework that recti-fies misaligned positive credit within detector-localized undesirable behavior spans without altering task rewards or the underlying RL objective. • We evaluate C 3 across different policy optimization paradigms and multi-turn agent bench-marks. Results show that C 3 reduces positive advantages on detected undesirable-behavior spans, decreases their occurrence, and improves overall agent performance. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents. Language agents are commonly optimized with critic-based methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) or group-relative objectives such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). They support retrieval (Jin et al., 2025) and long-horizon research or collaboration 2Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025), but typically learn from trajectory-level outcomes. Con-sequently, an otherwise successful trajectory may still reinforce undesirable intermediate behaviors. Unlike approaches that modify the overall policy optimization process, C 3preserves the underlying objective and instead performs localized credit correction on undesirable behavior spans. 

Credit Assignment for Multi-Turn Agents. Credit assignment asks how trajectory-level outcome credit should be attributed to intermediate actions. Hierarchical methods allocate credit across turns (Zhou et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2026), while turn- or step-level estimators align advantages with action boundaries (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a). Action-to-token allocation further refines the mapping from outcomes to local advantages (Ma et al., 2026), and retrospective meth-ods estimate each turn’s contribution (Ekbote et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b). C 3 leaves this outcome-to-advantage mapping intact and operates on the resulting token-level advantages. While these approaches refine how outcome credit is distributed, C 3 instead focuses on whether the resulting local credit is consistent with the underlying behavior. 

Process Supervision for LLM Agents. Process supervision instead asks whether an intermediate behavior is desirable. Process reward models score individual steps (Lightman et al., 2023); search agents use step-wise rewards (Wang et al., 2025b), information gain (Wang et al., 2025a), or hind-sight signals (Lu et al., 2026). Judges and self-distillation provide local assessments (Wang et al., 2026c; Li et al., 2026), while counterfactual attribution derives feedback from alternative rollouts (Zhu et al., 2026). These signals are typically introduced as rewards or advantage shaping. Rather than introducing additional process rewards, C 3 uses localized process feedback to correct positive advantages assigned to undesirable-behavior spans within the existing token-level advantage.

Figure 3(c) shows that, among 63 ,511 valid tokens within detector-identified undesirable-behavior spans in an in-strumented PPO run, 18 ,460 receive positive base advantage before correction, corresponding to 29.07%. Together, these observations expose a failure mode in multi-turn RL: undesirable behaviors can re-main positively reinforced at the token level even as they are suppressed at the trajectory level. The contrasting dynamics under PPO and GDPO further suggest that this issue is not specific to a par-ticular credit construction mechanism, but can arise whenever trajectory-level signals are translated into local credit. This motivates a targeted intervention: 

Instead of redesigning rewards or advantage estimation, can correcting positive credit for a small number of localized undesirable behaviors be sufficient to improve RL?

7 CONCLUSION 

Trajectory-level supervision can assign positive advantages to spans corresponding to undesirable local behaviors, creating a mismatch between behavioral quality and policy credit. C 3 addresses 9Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 this through confidence-guided local credit correction, reversing positive advantages on detected undesirable-behavior spans while leaving the task reward, returns, and critic targets unchanged. Across experiments, C 3 improves average exact match and reduces detected undesirable behaviors, highlighting that effective process supervision is not only about providing denser behavioral feed-back, but also about translating that feedback into appropriate local policy credit. B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

B.1 EVENT DETECTION 

Action Parsing. The search agent emits one action per turn. A valid turn contains either a search action, 

<think>...</think> <search>...</search> ,

or a final answer action, 

<think>...</think> <answer>...</answer> .

No text is allowed outside the corresponding tags. The parser performs strict validation together with a single-action recovery pass, so that recoverable formatting errors remain executable actions. Errors that affect only strict-format evaluation are retained in the evaluation logs but are not treated as undesirable-behavior events. 

Behavior Categories. We consider two types of undesirable behaviors: invalid actions and query loops . Invalid actions include malformed or impermissible tool interactions, such as unclosed tool-call tags, unavailable tools, invalid tool arguments, or actions issued after a completed answer. Query loops refer to redundant tool interactions in which the same or nearly identical query is repeated across turns. The first occurrence is retained, while each subsequent occurrence is treated as an undesirable-behavior event. 

Query-Loop Rules. We distinguish exact and near query loops. An exact query loop occurs when the normalized query exactly matches an earlier query in the same trajectory. A near query loop occurs when a non-identical query has normalized character-trigram Jaccard similarity of at least 

0.9 and length of at least eight characters. Exact matches take precedence over near matches. Invalid actions and exact query loops are assigned severity 1.0, while near query loops are assigned severity

Credit Construction. The five training arms differ in both the signals used to construct credit and the point at which detector information enters the optimization. Terminal PPO adds the pro-cess penalty directly to the terminal outcome, after which PPO computes GAE. Process-reward PPO instead assigns the penalty to the turn where the undesirable behavior occurs and includes it in the return before GAE. PPO+C 3 uses the outcome reward and leaves the base advantage con-struction unchanged, applying a localized actor-side correction only after the base advantages have been computed. GDPO constructs group-relative credit from outcome, format, and process signals; GDPO+C 3 applies the same localized post-estimation correction to that group-relative credit. 

Process-Reward PPO. For the process-reward PPO baseline, detected events are converted into turn-level penalties. Invalid actions and exact query loops receive the full penalty, while near query loops receive half strength. These penalties are accumulated at the corresponding turn boundaries and incorporated into the return before advantage estimation. In contrast, C 3 uses the same behav-ioral localization but does not modify the task reward or return.

Rather, after correcting credit on detected undesirable behaviors, the resulting policy can allocate retrieval differently depending on the information requirements of the task. In particular, reducing unnecessary interactions on simpler tasks does not preclude retaining additional retrieval on tasks that require multi-hop information gathering. The behavior under GDPO is different. Compared with outcome-only GDPO, GDPO+C 3 generally increases the number of searches across the benchmarks. This difference from PPO highlights that the behavioral effect of the same local credit correction is not invariant to the underlying optimization paradigm. PPO and GDPO construct token-level credit differently, so correcting the same type of local positive credit can lead to different changes in the resulting interaction policy. Importantly, the increase in search frequency under GDPO should not be interpreted as C 3 directly encouraging more retrieval, since retrieval frequency is not an explicit optimization target of the method. Taken together, these results show that C3 does not impose a fixed preference for shorter or longer interaction trajectories . Instead, its behavioral effect emerges indirectly through local credit cor-rection and varies with both the optimization paradigm and the task. This distinction is important because it suggests that the performance gains from C 3 should not be understood simply in terms of performing more or fewer tool interactions. C 3 changes which local behaviors receive policy credit, while the resulting interaction allocation is determined by the broader optimization process and task requirements.
