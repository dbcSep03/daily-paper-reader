Abstract 

Joint reinforcement learning and self-distillation combines task rewards with dense teacher guidance for agent training, but their gradients can partly cancel. A mixing coefficient controls relative scale without selectively removing opposing components. We present CORD , a general framework for conflict-aware optimization of two objectives. CORD separates the choice of protected objective from the condition that triggers projection, and combines the gradients once per optimizer mini-batch. A nearest-point formulation yields a closed-form update and characterizes its first-order effect on the protected objective before optimizer transformations. Gradient diagnostics connect conflict in agent training to lost local optimization progress and motivate selective correction. As a gradient-combination mechanism, CORD can be added to different joint training recipes while retaining their objectives. Across three such recipes, it improves ALFWorld success by 1.5–15.9 percentage points. On WebShop, it improves two of the three pairs, with SDAR rising from 60.7% to 64.6% success. Our analyses explain why protection direction and projection gate must be chosen together, and why the distillation objective and its mixing weight remain consequential after projection.

# 1 Introduction 

Language-model agents learn to complete tasks through sequences of actions, but outcome rewards provide limited credit for individual decisions (Feng et al., 2025). Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) compares returns across sampled attempts (Shao et al., 2024), yet supplies little learning signal when rewards tie. On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level feedback on student trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024), but its usefulness depends on the teacher’s guidance. Joint training couples this guidance with reward-based optimization, allowing agents to learn from both task outcomes and informed action preferences (Lu et al., 2026). Existing approaches refine how teacher guidance enters learning. Skill-conditioned self-distillation supplies an informed contextual view of the policy (Wang et al., 2026), while SDAR weights token supervision using detached teacher–student confidence gaps (Lu et al., 2026). RLSD incorporates detached log-probability differences into token advantages, preserving their signs without a separate distillation loss (Yang et al., 2026a). Confidence gates reweight token signals, whereas a gradient inner-product gate tests their accumulated effect. The two operations can be composed for separate RL and OPD losses.

Aligned pairs remain unchanged. We introduce CORD , a general framework for conflict-aware optimization of two objectives. CORD accumulates both gradients under the specified loss weights for each optimizer mini-batch. It retains the selected protected gradient and minimally modifies the other before combining them. Conflict-only projection removes opposing components, while an always-projection variant also removes aligned components. Either objective can be protected. Our analysis relates the resulting first-order contribution on fixed sampled losses to finite step size and optimizer transformations. # 3 Method 

CORD combines two objective gradients by making the protected direction and projection gate explicit. It keeps one weighted gradient intact and makes the smallest change to the other that satisfies the chosen geometric constraint. The same construction accommodates either protection direction. The loss functions and their relative weight specify the signals supplied to it. Figure 2 illustrates the combination for GRPO and OPD, with an analytic loss surface showing example update paths.

# 4 Experiments 

We test whether changing the gradient-combination rule adds value across joint training methods, then use the analyses to guide its configuration. The experiments answer three research questions: 

(RQ1) Effectiveness: How much does CORD improve existing joint training recipes? (RQ2) Projec-tion choices: How do the protected objective, projection gate, and loss construction guide practical configuration? (RQ3) Agent behavior: How do update choices appear in agent interactions? 

4.1 Experimental setup 

Tasks and training. We use the SDAR agent suite (Lu et al., 2026) with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024). ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) evaluates household tasks over at most 50 actions. We report success across six categories and the pooled average. WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) evaluates instruction-following shopping. We cap episodes at 15 actions and report graded task score and strict success rate on a 0–100 scale. Our shared trainer uses 150 training iterations, eight rollouts per prompt, AdamW at 10 −6, reference-KL coefficient 0 .01, and responses capped at 512 tokens on eight NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs. Full settings are given in Appendix B. 

Methods. Table 1 includes Vanilla, Skill-Prompt, and OPSD values reported by Lu et al. (2026). GRPO and RLSD results are our own runs. RLSD incorporates the self-distillation signal into the policy optimization objective (Yang et al., 2026a). Following SDAR, the joint recipes share a predefined skill bank whose contents remain fixed throughout training. Each Base–CORD pair uses the same bank and skill-selection rule to isolate gradient combination. Household tasks select task-specific manipulation skills, while shopping instructions select relevant skills by keyword alongside general strategies. This context guides teacher scoring of student-generated actions and is absent from the student’s rollout and evaluation inputs. We apply CORD to three joint training recipes in this shared interface: Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026) ( 𝑘 3, 𝜆 = 0.001), GRPO+OPSD (host 𝑘 1,

𝜆 = 0.005), and SDAR (Lu et al., 2026) (confidence-weighted host 𝑘 1, 𝜆 = 0.01). Each recipe without CORD uses naive addition. + CORD retains its coefficient, protects the RL gradient, and projects only on conflict. RQ2 then varies the protected objective and projection gate. 

Evaluation. We fix validation tasks to track progress on the same problems during training and hold the final evaluation tasks constant across paired methods. For the main comparison and full-training analyses in RQ2, including PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020), we evaluate the final policy four times after 150 updates at temperature 0 .4. Each run uses one training seed, and the displayed ±

is the population standard deviation across these four passes. Our WebShop evaluation uses 500 tasks, whereas the published baseline values in Table 1 use the 128-task protocol of Lu et al. (2026). Appendix B.3 details the protocol.

Direction and gating therefore interact with the coefficient: removing the aligned component has a different effect depending on which objective is protected and the relative gradient scales. CORD keeps one gradient intact, whereas the two-objective PCGrad adaptation (Yu et al., 2020) modifies both when they conflict (Appendix B.3). Protecting RL changes the weighted distillation gradient, so the correction scales with 𝜆 .Protecting distillation changes the RL gradient, whose correction is unchanged when only 𝜆 varies. 

A small auxiliary coefficient therefore does not make distillation protection a small intervention. Because conflict-only projection retains aligned contributions, the effect of always projection depends on the selected protection direction.

# 5 Conclusion 

Joint RL and distillation gives agents complementary feedback, but opposing gradient components can reduce its local optimization benefit. CORD applies a simple projection rule to this interaction, allowing either objective to be protected and making the trigger explicit. The first-order analysis explains the correction on fixed surrogates before optimizer transformations, and the agent experiments establish incremental gains across three host recipes on ALFWorld and two of three on WebShop. The analyses also yield concrete configuration guidance: choose protection and triggering together, tune the mixing weight for the selected distillation loss, and specify aggregation before comparing gradient directions. CORD thus makes gradient combination a reusable design choice.

# A Additional Related Work 

A.1 On-policy and skill-conditioned distillation 

On-policy distillation trains a student on its own generated sequences while a teacher supplies token-level targets, reducing the distribution mismatch between training and inference. Agarwal et al. (2024) develop generalized knowledge distillation with a separate teacher. On-policy self-distillation instead uses different contextual views of a model: Zhao et al. (2026) condition the teacher on privileged information, such as verified reasoning traces, while the student sees the original question. For multi-turn agents, Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026) derives dynamic skills from completed trajectories and uses an importance-weighted reverse-KL objective with a periodically synchronized teacher. These methods supply dense supervision on student-generated trajectories. SDAR (Lu et al., 2026) combines GRPO with skill-conditioned distillation and modulates token-level supervision using a detached sigmoid gate. Its experimental protocol draws on the SkillBank of SkillRL (Xia et al., 2026), which learns a hierarchical skill library and evolves it with the policy. SDAR also evaluates retrieved-skill adaptations of Skill-SD and OPSD, together with prompt-based skill injection. Our three host recipes—Skill-SD, GRPO+OPSD, and SDAR—follow the agent-training implementations described in Section 3 and Appendix B. In particular, the retrieved-skill 𝑘 3 host follows the adaptation in Lu et al. (2026), which differs from the original Skill-SD dynamic skill-generation protocol. A token-level distillation gate changes the input gradient, whereas CORD’s projection gate decides when to modify the relationship between the two accumulated gradients. The two operations can therefore be composed. Another approach incorporates teacher information directly into the advantage or policy-loss signal. RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a) uses detached teacher–student log-probability differences to reweight token advantages while preserving their signs. This route modifies the policy update internally, without a separate distillation loss to combine. 

A.2 Evolving skills and reflection memory 

The guidance available to an agent can evolve with its experience. SkillRL expands and refines a hierarchical skill library using failure analysis (Xia et al., 2026). RetroAgent stores retrospective lessons from successful and failed attempts in a growing reflection memory, and combines retrieved lessons with progress-based intrinsic rewards (Zhang et al., 2026). Its RL-trained variant also jointly optimizes decision making and self-reflection. These approaches improve what the agent retains from its interactions and retrieves for later tasks. Evolving guidance need not reside in a persistent library. SEED trains the policy to generate hindsight skills from its current trajectories and internalizes their guidance through joint RL and on-policy distillation (Wu et al., 2026). OPID constructs episode- and step-level skills whose induced log-probability shifts enter the policy update (Yang et al., 2026b). GRSD uses reflections from successful and failed rollouts to form group-level guidance and modulate advantages (Zheng et al., 2026). CORD operates at the subsequent combination of objective gradients, so its use is orthogonal to whether guidance comes from a fixed bank or evolves with the policy. Potential applications include combining reward and distillation gradients in SEED, or decision-making and reflection gradients in the RL-trained RetroAgent variant, while preserving their respective guidance mechanisms.

A.3 Conflicting gradients and projection 

Multi-task loss balancing adjusts objective scales using gradient magnitudes or learned uncertainty (Chen et al., 2018; Kendall et al., 2018). Gradient projection instead changes directions and has a long history in multi-task and continual learning. For two conflicting task gradients, PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) removes each gradient’s component along the other before combining them. A-GEM (Chaudhry et al., 2019) uses an asymmetric projection: it modifies the current-task gradient to satisfy a single half-space constraint defined by the gradient estimated from a sampled episodic-memory batch. This constraint prevents an increase in that sampled memory loss under a first-order approximation. Orthogonal gradient descent (Farajtabar et al., 2020) projects updates away from a stored subspace of gradients of previous network outputs. CAGrad (Liu et al., 2021) balances progress on the mean task loss with the worst local improvement among tasks. CORD reuses the single-half-space projection underlying A-GEM (Lemma 1). The protected reference in CORD comes from one of the two jointly trained objectives, and the projected candidate is added to that reference. Either objective can be protected. Conflict-only projection uses a half-space constraint, while the always-project variant imposes an orthogonality constraint even when the gradients agree. The agent experiments study how protection, triggering, loss construction, and aggregation affect learning. The main host comparisons protect GRPO with conflict-only projection, while the direction and gate analyses test both choices. 

A.4 Agent RL and GRPO-style policy optimization 

GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) estimates advantages relative to groups of sampled responses, avoiding a separate learned critic. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) supplies the clipped policy surrogate used by these host implementations. The truncated importance weighting in our 𝑘 3 and 𝜌 d distillation objectives is related to the correction used in V-trace/IMPALA (Espeholt et al., 2018), although the distillation weight does not implement the full V-trace return estimator. GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) improves multi-turn credit assignment by combining episode-level advantages with step-level comparisons among actions grouped by shared anchor states. These choices determine the reward-derived gradient. CORD operates after the two objective gradients have been accumulated for a mini-batch, so credit assignment and gradient combination address different parts of the update.

C.6 Computational cost 

Let 𝑑 be the number of trainable parameters and let a training iteration contain 𝐾 PPO mini-batches, each partitioned into 𝑀 micro-batches. CORD accumulates the two objective gradients over each complete PPO mini-batch before applying the projection. Computing their inner products, norms, and combined update requires 𝑂 (𝑑 ) arithmetic per mini-batch, or 𝑂 (𝐾𝑑 ) per iteration. The additional gradient buffers require 𝑂 (𝑑 ) storage, which is distributed across workers when gradients are sharded. The scalar inner products and norms can be communicated in a packed reduction whose message size does not grow with 𝑑 .For gradient evaluation, write 𝐹 for the cost of a student forward computation and 𝐵 1, 𝐵 2 for the reverse-mode computations of the two objectives on a fixed micro-batch. If both objectives use the same student outputs, a shared-forward schedule has actor-update cost 

𝑇 actor CORD = 𝐾𝑀 (𝐹 + 𝐵 1 + 𝐵 2) + 𝑂 (𝐾𝑑 ) + 𝑇 opt ,

where 𝑇 opt includes gradient clipping and optimizer updates. A fused-loss Base instead has cost 

𝑇 actor Base = 𝐾𝑀 (𝐹 + 𝐵 sum ) + 𝑇 opt ,

with 𝐵 sum denoting the reverse-mode computation of the weighted loss sum. These expressions use equal-sized micro-batches to simplify notation. For variable workloads, the corresponding costs are summed over micro-batches. Parameter and gradient communication belong to the gradient evaluation costs, separately from the packed reduction for projection. Sharing the forward computation avoids evaluating identical student outputs twice. It does not make two objective-gradient evaluations equivalent to one backward pass on their summed loss: the latter supplies only the combined gradient, which generally does not determine the inner products needed by CORD. Both schedules have the same asymptotic order when the reverse-mode costs are comparable, while their constant factors depend on graph reuse, gradient-buffer handling, sharding, and hardware. Forward sharing, fused vector operations, and selective diagnostic collection are implementation opportunities rather than a fixed wall-clock guarantee. Teacher scores already computed for the auxiliary objective are reused. 

Cost of a complete training iteration. Let 𝐴 denote the actor-update time of an ordinary fused-loss Base, and let 𝑆 include rollout and environment interaction, teacher, reference, and old-policy scoring, reward and advantage computation, and the remaining work within a training iteration. 𝑟 actor , then
