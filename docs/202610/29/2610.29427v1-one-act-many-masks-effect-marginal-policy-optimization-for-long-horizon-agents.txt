ABSTRACT 

Recent advances in multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) have substantially im-proved the performance of LLM agents on complex, long-horizon interactive tasks. However, despite progress in fine-grained credit assignment, policy opti-mization still applies these learning signals through response-specific probability ratios. Different textual responses can induce the same executable effect, yet credit differences arising from subsequent outcomes remain tied to their individual tex-tual realizations. We identify this mismatch as credit–update misalignment . Our analysis reveals that credit–text pairing directly shapes policy-update directions, even when the policy context and executable effect are held fixed. Motivated by this finding, we propose Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO), which aligns policy optimization with execution-level equivalence through an effect-level surrogate that couples updates across alternative textual realizations. EMPO preserves the original credit signals, integrates with both trajectory- level and step-level credit estimators, and requires no additional critics or rollouts. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban show that EMPO outperforms strong base-lines in both task performance and learning efficiency. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as interactive agents for embodied deci-sion making, computer use, tool use, and coding. Unlike single-turn reasoning, these tasks require agents to repeatedly perceive, reason, and act over extended interaction horizons, often under partial observability, sparse feedback, and delayed rewards. Reinforcement learning (RL), which directly improves policies through environment feedback, has therefore become an important paradigm for post-training long-horizon LLM agents. Credit assignment remains a central challenge in long-horizon agent RL, where delayed outcomes must supervise many intermediate decisions. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) typically relies on a learned value function for advantage estimation, while group-based methods such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) replace the critic with relative comparisons among sampled trajectories. Recent work further improves the granularity and reliability of credit through local state comparisons, hierarchical or graph-structured attribution, and action-level calibration (Feng et al., 2025; He et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026). Despite their different constructions, these methods share the goal of providing more informative credit for individual decisions along a trajectory. Yet credit estimation is only one part of policy learning, as the resulting signal must still be trans-lated into a policy update. An LLM agent generates a textual response containing reasoning and an eventual action, while an execution interface maps that response to the command that actually affects the environment. The mapping from textual responses to executable effects is generally many-to-one. Responses that differ in reasoning, wording, or length may still induce the same immediate executable effect. We find that conventional response-wise optimization contains a within-effect covariance term coupling supplied credit with realization-specific response scores. Based on this view, we propose Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO) , which reorganizes policy updates around executable effects rather than individual textual realizations. Controlled analyses further connect the optimization objective to its intended mechanism. Effect-ratio sharing changes within-cell gradient coupling and leads to mea-surable differences in response-probability updates after a controlled policy step, while introducing only small additional actor-side computation. (1) We identify credit–update misalignment , a policy-optimization problem arising when execution-equivalent textual responses retain realization-specific credit–gradient pairing, and characterize it through an exact decomposi-tion of the response-wise update. (2) We propose Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO) ,which shares policy ratios across locally comparable, execution-equivalent responses while preserv-ing the original advantages and requiring no additional rollouts, critics, or auxiliary models. 2 RELATED WORK 

Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents. Reinforcement learning has become a central post-training paradigm for improving the reasoning and decision-making capabilities of large language models. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) remains a foundation, while critic-free and group-relative methods such as RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), REINFORCE++ (Hu, 2026), and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) improve the scalability and stability of LLM reinforcement learning. More recently, GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) moves importance weighting and clipping from the token level to the sequence level. Building on these advances, RL has increasingly been extended from single-turn reasoning to agents that interact repeatedly with external environments. RAGEN (Wang et al., 2025) studies multi-turn RL for reasoning agents in interactive environments, while Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025), WebAgent-R1 (Wei et al., 2025b), and ToolRL (Qian et al., 2025) apply RL to search, web interaction, and tool use, respectively. Related efforts extend RL to software-engineering tasks (Wei et al., 2025a) and develop general interfaces for training hetero-geneous agent workflows (Luo et al., 2025). As agentic training moves toward longer interaction horizons, recent methods also address challenges specific to multi-turn optimization, including adap-tive exploration (Wang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026) and training stability (Li et al., 2026b). These studies establish RL as a practical framework for improving LLM agents through interaction tasks. 

Credit Assignment in Long-Horizon Decision Making. Credit assignment is a longstanding challenge in reinforcement learning, where delayed outcomes must be attributed to the interme-diate decisions that produced them. Classical approaches reduce this difficulty through temporal value estimation, such as generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2018), reward shaping, or explicit redistribution of delayed returns (Arjona-Medina et al., 2019). For LLM agents, one line of work provides denser intermediate supervision through process-level reward or verification (Choudhury, 2025; Ma et al., 2026). Another line extracts finer-grained credit directly from sam-pled interaction trajectories without requiring an additional critic. Turn-level advantage estimation assigns localized credit across multi-turn tool interactions (Wei et al., 2026), while GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) constructs step-level comparison groups from recurring environment states. HGPO (He et al., 2026) further accounts for historical-context consistency when constructing these compar-isons. Other approaches exploit richer rollout structures. SALT (Li et al., 2026a) derives step-level advantages from trajectory graphs, Tree-GRPO (Ding & Ye, 2026) obtains local supervision from tree-structured rollouts, and GraphGPO (Cheng et al., 2026) estimates progress using a shared state-transition graph. Recent work further studies the reliability and granularity of these estimates. ECPO (Li et al., 2026c) aggregates returns over canonical actions and calibrates low-support step-level credit, while GACA (Liang et al., 2026) adaptively balances trajectory- and step-level advan-tages according to policy uncertainty. These methods primarily improve what credit is assigned, or how it is estimated, densified, and calibrated. Our work studies a complementary problem at the policy-update stage. EMPO preserves the underlying credit signal and instead changes how it is translated into policy updates when multiple textual responses share the same executable effect.

5 EXPERIMENTS 

We first evaluate the task performance and learning efficiency of EMPO on long-horizon agent benchmarks (Section 5.2). We then ablate the three operations underlying the effect-marginal update (Section 5.3). Finally, we examine the resulting policy-update structure (Section 5.4) and character-ize the effect cells available for ratio sharing throughout training (Section 5.5). 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Tasks. We evaluate EMPO on three long-horizon agent benchmarks: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), and Sokoban (Schrader, 2018). ALFWorld evaluates multi-step decision making in household environments and contains 3,827 task instances across six categories: Pick & Place, Examine in Light, Clean & Place, Heat & Place, Cool & Place, and Pick Two & Place. WebShop requires agents to search, navigate, and purchase products in a simulated shopping website containing over 1.1 million products and 12,000 user instructions. Sokoban evaluates sequential visual planning through box-pushing puzzles. We report success rate (SR) on all three benchmarks and additionally report the average task score on WebShop to capture partial task completion. 

Baselines. We compare against three groups of baselines: (1) closed-source LLMs, including GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (Comanici et al., 2025); (2) prompting-based agents, including direct prompting with Qwen2.5-Instruct, ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), and Reflex-ion (Shinn et al., 2023); and (3) reinforcement learning methods, including PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), and GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025). We instantiate EMPO on GRPO and GiGPO, two representative RL algorithms with trajectory-level and step-level advantage estimation, yielding EMPO-G and EMPO-GiG. 

Training details. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025) for ALFWorld and WebShop, and Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) for Sokoban. For each base algorithm and its EMPO counterpart, we use the same model initialization, rollout con-figuration, training budget, and hyperparameter settings. The rollout group size is set to 8 for all group-based RL experiments. We train and evaluate each main setting with three random seeds and report mean ± standard deviation. We ablate the three operations underlying EMPO, including length normalization, effect-based grouping, and ratio sharing. As shown in Table 4, all three components contribute to the effec-tiveness of the effect-marginal update. Removing length normalization makes response-level ratios increasingly sensitive to generation length, allowing accumulated token-level probability changes in longer responses to exert a larger influence on the shared ratio. Disabling ratio sharing preserves the normalized response ratio but restores realization-specific policy directions, weakening coor-dinated updates among execution-equivalent responses. Replacing effect-based cells with arbitrary groups couples responses without preserving executable-effect consistency, introducing interference between policy updates associated with different effects. 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO) for long-horizon LLM agent rein-forcement learning. We identified credit–update misalignment, where execution-equivalent textual responses remain associated with realization-specific policy updates, and characterized its effect through an exact decomposition of the response-wise update. EMPO addresses this mismatch by sharing length-normalized policy ratios among locally comparable responses with the same exe-cutable effect while preserving the advantages provided by the base algorithm. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban show that EMPO consistently improves both task performance and learning efficiency across different model scales and advantage estimators. Further analysis shows that effect-level ratio sharing induces more structured within-cell gradient coupling and more coordinated policy updates among execution-equivalent responses. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of effect-marginal policy optimization for long-horizon LLM agent training. 9Preprint

B LIMITATIONS 

EMPO relies on identifying comparable interaction contexts and canonical executable effects. These are readily available in the structured-action environments considered here, but may require more careful construction for less structured tool interfaces. Because effect cells are formed from sampled responses, their coverage also varies across tasks and training stages, responses outside eligible cells retain the base objective. Extending effect-cell construction and evaluation to a wider range of agent interfaces is a natural direction for future work.

Policy-gradient loss. We report the clipped surrogate loss used to optimize the actor. Its value is determined jointly by the estimated advantages, policy ratios, and clipping behavior, and therefore reflects the optimization signal encountered by the policy during training. 

KL divergence. We track the token-level KL divergence between the current policy and the fixed reference policy. This quantity measures the accumulated deviation of the learned policy from its initialization over the course of training. 

Policy-ratio clip fraction. The clip fraction measures the proportion of policy-update terms for which the likelihood ratio falls outside the PPO clipping interval. It characterizes how frequently proposed probability changes are constrained by the clipped surrogate objective. For EMPO, this statistic is computed using the effective ratios defined in Eq. 14. 

Training reward and success rate. We report the mean episodic reward and the fraction of suc-cessful episodes in each rollout batch to track on-policy task progress during training. The main experimental results are evaluated separately on the corresponding validation sets. 

Token entropy. Token entropy is computed from the next-token distribution along sampled re-sponse prefixes and averaged over valid response tokens. It measures the diversity of the model’s token-level policy throughout training. 

Gradient norm. We record the norm of the actor gradient before gradient clipping. This statis-tic characterizes the scale of the proposed parameter update and is used to monitor optimization dynamics throughout training. 16 Preprint Table 5: Environment-specific training configurations.

We further examine how effect-marginal optimization shapes policy adaptation throughout training. Figures 6 and 7 report token entropy, KL divergence to the reference policy, and gradient norm for GRPO, GiGPO, and their EMPO variants. Across both ALFWorld and WebShop, EMPO maintains higher token entropy during the later stages of training while exhibiting continued policy movement, as reflected by the KL divergence and gra-dient norm. The three quantities together reveal a characteristic optimization pattern: the policy continues to adapt while retaining a less concentrated token distribution. This pattern appears con-sistently for both trajectory-level GRPO and step-level GiGPO, indicating that it is preserved across different advantage-estimation granularities. This training behavior connects naturally to the effect-marginal update in Section 4. In response-wise optimization, each credit signal is paired with its realization-specific policy direction, allowing within-cell credit variation to directly shape the probability of individual textual realizations. EMPO couples execution-equivalent responses through a shared effect-level ratio, so their policy changes 19 Preprint are organized around a common effect-conditioned direction. The resulting entropy dynamics pro-vide a distribution-level view of the coordinated updates observed in the policy-update analysis in Section 5.4.
