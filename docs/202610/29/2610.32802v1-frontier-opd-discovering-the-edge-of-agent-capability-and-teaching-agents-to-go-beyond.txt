# Frontier-OPD: Discovering the Edge of Agent Capability and Teaching Agents to Go Beyond 

Zheng Jiang , Yiming Chen , Houde Qian , Ling Li , Chaoyang Li , Lifeng Sun †

Tsinghua University | †Corresponding author 

On-policy distillation aligns teacher feedback with a student’s current behavior, yet acquiring useful supervision for multi-turn agents requires identifying both where guidance is needed and whether the student can sustain the interaction afterward. We introduce Frontier-OPD , which addresses this problem through the student’s learning frontier : decision situations that remain challenging but admit useful autonomous continuation after brief teacher guidance. To estimate this frontier, Frontier-OPD organizes on-policy experience into a shared-state trajectory graph, where trajectory-balanced aggregation of teacher–student disagreement identifies distinct intervention candidates while preserving the histories needed for replay. Short teacher bridges are then assessed by comparing agreement over the bridge and ensuing student continuation with an equally long segment of the original trajectory, providing a behavioral proxy for guidance that the current student can build on. Accepted teacher responses augment ordinary distillation data, and fresh rollouts renew the frontier estimate as the policy changes. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and ScienceWorld report higher mean autonomous performance than the compared distillation baselines across the evaluated student scales and teacher configurations, with ablations indicating complementary effects of candidate selection and guidance filtering. In one ALFWorld timing setting, the additional collection cost is offset by a shorter time to a target success rate.

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-turn agents interact with their environments through sequences of decisions whose consequences determine the observations and choices that follow (Yao et al., 2023), making autonomous task completion dependent on coordinated behavior across the entire interaction. Distilling this ability therefore requires supervision that remains relevant to the histories induced by the student’s own policy, a concern closely related to distribution shift in interactive imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011). On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this concern by querying a teacher on student-generated responses (Agarwal et al., 2024); however, aligning feedback with current behavior leaves open the question of which additional teacher guidance is most appropriate for the student to learn from next. Teacher–student disagreement alone provides an incomplete basis for answering this question, because the need to correct a decision and the student’s ability to use that correction are distinct considerations. Where the student already behaves reliably, additional guidance may offer limited benefit; where its behavior differs substantially from the teacher’s, an intervention may still leave it facing subsequent decisions that exceed its current capabilities. Consider a student that can complete a placement task once shown a particular object manipulation: a short demonstration at that decision may provide usable guidance, whereas an intervention leading to an unfamiliar procedure may leave the student unable to sustain the interaction. Effective supervision acquisition thus requires identifying learning needs that can be addressed through guidance compatible with the student’s subsequent autonomous behavior. We characterize these opportunities as the student’s learning frontier : decision situations that it has not reliably mastered, but from which brief teacher guidance can enable useful autonomous continuation. This frontier is relative to the current policy and concerns the suitability of a learning opportunity, rather than simply whether an environment state has been visited. Estimating this frontier entails two coupled challenges, illustrated in Figure 1. Identifying learning needs 

requires relating individual responses to the decision situations in which they arise: an isolated large teacher–student gap may not represent a persistent weakness, and repeated visits within one attempt can disproportionately influence the evidence. The representation must consequently consolidate behavior across shared situations while preserving the histories that condition individual actions. Assessing the usability of guidance , in turn, requires evidence from the student’s behavior after control is returned, since the quality of the teacher’s action does not by itself establish whether the student can continue productively from the resulting history. Recent agentic distillation methods address complementary aspects of supervision acquisition: TCOD varies student execution depth through temporal curricula (Wang et al., 2026), Guided-OPD schedules teacher participation across turns (Li et al., 2026), and FutureBridge-OPD validates localized guidance through subsequent student continuations (Chen et al., 2026). Building on continuation-based validation, we investigate how decision situations should be selected for intervention and how that selection can be coupled to an assessment of the resulting guidance. The learning-frontier perspective connects these two decisions through the evolving needs and capabilities of the student. We introduce Frontier-OPD , which operationalizes and tracks a proxy for the learning frontier by combining shared-state evidence aggregation with history-specific intervention and validation. Student trajectories are organized into a graph whose shared nodes align observed decision situations, allowing trajectory-balanced aggregation of disagreement to rank distinct frontier candidates without allocating multiple candidate slots to repeated visits at the same node. Each candidate is replayed from a concrete history, after which a short teacher bridge is assessed by comparing agreement over the bridge and ensuing student continuation with an equally long original segment. # 2 Related Work 

On-Policy Distillation for Multi-Turn Agents. Knowledge distillation transfers teacher predictions to a student (Hinton et al., 2015), while on-policy approaches align supervision with student-generated responses (Agarwal et al., 2024) and support reverse-KL optimization for generative models (Gu et al., 2024). In multi-turn environments, the student’s actions also determine the histories on which later feedback is obtained, making experience construction an important part of the training procedure. Drawing on curriculum principles that adapt learning opportunities to progress (Graves et al., 2017; Florensa et al., 2017), TCOD regulates student execution depth (Wang et al., 2026), whereas Guided-OPD schedules teacher participation within rollouts (Li et al., 2026). Frontier-OPD retains student-driven collection while organizing observed decision situations as a shared reference for acquiring additional supervision. 

Selective Teacher Guidance and Validation. Beyond constructing training trajectories, interactive learning must determine where expert guidance is warranted and how its usefulness should be assessed. DAgger collects expert labels under learner-induced state distributions (Ross et al., 2011), while ThriftyDAgger uses novelty and risk to allocate interventions under a budget (Hoque et al., 2022); in agentic distillation, FutureBridge-OPD assesses short teacher bridges through subsequent student continuations (Chen et al., 2026). These approaches motivate complementary considerations of intervention need and downstream behavior. Our learning-frontier perspective connects them through trajectory-balanced aggregation of disagreement at shared states and matched-window validation of history-specific guidance, adapting the supervision selected for training to both the student’s observed behavior and its continuation after teacher intervention. 

# 3 Method 

Frontier-OPD estimates the learning frontier through two complementary operations: aggregating teacher– student disagreement to identify candidate learning needs, and validating short teacher interventions through the student’s subsequent behavior (Figure 2). A shared-state trajectory graph provides the reference for organizing behavioral evidence, while the original decision histories are retained so that each candidate can be replayed and evaluated in its own context. This distinction allows supervision to be allocated at the level of a shared decision situation without replacing the history on which the student and teacher condition their responses. Let πθk and πT denote the student at collection iteration k and the teacher, respectively. At turn t, the agent conditions on interaction history ht and generates response yt specifying environment action at; shared state identities are used for evidence aggregation and ranking, whereas the full histories determine intervention replay and validation.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate whether supervision selected through the learning-frontier procedure improves autonomous task performance, how candidate selection and guidance filtering contribute, and how the collection budgets affect performance and training time. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks and metrics. We evaluate Frontier-OPD on ALFWorld for household interaction (Shridhar et al., 2021), WebShop for grounded web interaction (Yao et al., 2022), and ScienceWorld for interactive scientific tasks (Wang et al., 2022), reporting success rate (SR) and, where available, task score. All evaluations use held-out task instances on which the student executes independently from the environment’s initial state, following the protocol in Appendix A. 

Models and comparisons. The main comparison uses Qwen3-32B as teacher with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B students (Yang et al., 2025), supplemented by an RL-trained Qwen3-8B teacher (Qwen3-8B-RL) with the same student scales to examine transfer across teacher configurations (Section 4.3). We compare against OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024), TCOD-F2B and TCOD-B2F (Wang et al., 2026), Guided-OPD (Li et al., 2026), and FutureBridge-OPD (Chen et al., 2026), with zero-shot student and teacher policies providing reference performance. Within each controlled comparison, we fix initialization, training tasks, reference-prefix 7curriculum, prompts, decoding, and optimization settings; the reference Frontier-OPD configuration uses 

M = 4 , B = 4 , K = 1 , H = 3 , and η = 0 .

Reporting protocol. For trained policies, Tables 1, 2, and 3 report mean ± sample standard deviation over three seeds, while the sensitivity figures show the reported success rates. All metrics use a 0–100 scale with higher values indicating better performance, and success-rate differences are expressed in percentage points. Comparisons refer to the reported means, without inferring statistical significance from the three-seed summaries alone. 

4.2 Overall Task Performance 

With a Qwen3-32B teacher, Frontier-OPD has the highest reported mean among trained students on every reported metric at both student scales (Table 1). This pattern is consistent with the supervision-acquisition procedure being useful across the evaluated domains, although the three-seed summaries do not establish statistical significance. The larger gaps relative to the compared baselines on ALFWorld and ScienceWorld for the smaller student are consistent with targeted guidance being useful when autonomous execution remains difficult, while the persistent but varying gains for the larger student are consistent with dependence on the environment and teacher–student configuration. The comparison with FutureBridge-OPD is more modest on WebShop, where the small differences in task score and success rate are consistent with gains in both partial progress and task completion but warrant a more cautious interpretation than the ScienceWorld results. Some trained students also outperform the zero-shot teacher, consistent with the value of environment-specific adaptation; because the teacher is unadapted in this comparison, however, these results do not establish superiority under equal adaptation budgets.

4.5 Sensitivity to Intervention and Trajectory Budgets 

Both sweeps use Qwen3-8B-RL → Qwen3-4B on ALFWorld with K = 1 , H = 3 , and η = 0 , keeping initialization, training tasks, and optimization settings fixed within each sweep. When the candidate-attempt budget increases at a fixed trajectory count (Figure 3(a)), performance initially increases and then enters a flatter region. This pattern is consistent with diminishing returns to additional intervention attempts over the tested range, without establishing that all useful learning opportunities have been exhausted. Increasing the sampled trajectory count at a fixed intervention budget instead produces an upward trend over the evaluated range (Figure 3(b)). Since additional trajectories enlarge both the candidate pool and the ordinary distillation dataset, this trend cannot be attributed to more accurate frontier estimation alone; it nevertheless suggests that expanding student experience can remain useful after additional intervention attempts yield limited gains. # 5 Conclusion 

We presented Frontier-OPD, a framework that organizes supervision acquisition around the student’s learning frontier by jointly considering where guidance is needed and whether the current policy can build on it. Trajectory-balanced aggregation identifies candidate decision situations from shared-state experience, while matched-window validation screens short teacher bridges before their responses enter on-policy distillation. By renewing this selection process with fresh student rollouts, the method adapts supervision to the evolving policy and yields higher reported mean autonomous performance across the evaluated comparisons on three interactive benchmarks. These results motivate further investigation of learning-frontier dynamics, particularly through direct measurements of whether situations that initially require teacher guidance become independently solvable after training. 

# Limitations 

Our learning-frontier estimate relies on behavioral proxies whose relation to acquired capability remains imperfect: high disagreement need not indicate a missing skill, and non-decreasing agreement over a joint bridge-and-continuation window guarantees neither better continuation alone nor subsequent mastery. Although the experiments measure autonomous task performance, they do not directly trace skill acquisition at selected nodes or establish monotonic expansion of the learning frontier. The estimate is also conditioned on sampled experience and an observation-based state abstraction, which can merge histories with different learning needs and admits nodes with only one eligible visit. Shared state identity influences candidate ranking, but graph adjacency does not enter the score; moreover, because the random-selection ablation changes both ranking and the allocation unit, it cannot isolate the benefit of state aggregation. Practical applicability depends on replayable environments and access to teacher token probabilities, while the measured timing advantage is limited to one target success rate on ALFWorld and may vary with the performance threshold and hardware scale. The reported trained-policy comparisons use three 10 seeds and sample standard deviations; they do not establish statistical significance or robustness to additional seeds. The Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-8B-RL experiments also change teacher size and training history, so their results should not be read as an isolated teacher-scaling or RL-training comparison. Finally, all evidence comes from three benchmark environments and does not establish generalization to unrestricted real-world agents.

14 C Implementation Details 

State abstraction and decision identity. State keys combine normalized observations with available-action information, using deterministic normalization and ordering to avoid creating distinct keys from incidental formatting differences. Action information consists of admissible commands in ALFWorld, search availability and clickable elements in WebShop, and valid action–object combinations in ScienceWorld. Although the key determines node-score aggregation and graph membership, each occurrence retains its original context, response, and replay prefix, allowing disagreements from distinct histories to contribute to a shared-node estimate without replacing the model input. This observational abstraction need not distinguish every latent environment state. 

Candidate eligibility and ranking. An eligible occurrence must have valid actions through the candidate, a replayable prefix, a state key, aligned nonempty token-score arrays, finite disagreement, and enough original responses for the K + H reference horizon. Equation equation 5 is implemented by first averaging eligible disagreements within each trajectory at a node and then averaging over contributing trajectories, so neither visit count nor token count determines a trajectory’s total weight. For example, three visits with score 10 from one trajectory and one visit with score 0 from another yield a node score of 5 rather than 7.5. Negative scores remain valid, and the optional minimum-disagreement threshold is applied only after aggregation, before the selector takes at most B distinct nodes and assigns each its highest-disagreement eligible replay representative. There is no per-trajectory candidate quota; occurrence ties follow trajectory/turn order, while node-score ties follow the first eligible encounter. 

Replay and handoff. Before each intervention, the environment is restored and the recorded student actions are replayed, preceded by the reference prefix when a curriculum is active, after which teacher generation uses the saved decision context. A bridge lasts at most K response turns and may stop earlier at graph exit, but terminal handoffs are excluded because validation requires subsequent student behavior. At the default K = 1 ,both graph-internal and graph-external handoffs remain eligible, meaning that shared-node aggregation still affects intervention selection even though graph novelty is not an acceptance condition. 

Agreement computation. Both models score the same tokens under each response’s own context, including teacher-generated bridge responses, with agreement computed as the negative mean absolute log-probability gap within a response and then averaged uniformly across turns. This normalization prevents longer responses from receiving greater weight solely because of their length, while matching Lc + H turns on each branch uses the realized bridge length Lc rather than its upper bound K. Reference scores are reused from the collected trajectory, and acceptance requires all validity conditions in equation 10 together with a gain of at least η,including equality when η = 0 .

Training data and renewal. Ordinary student responses are retained regardless of bridge acceptance, and all responses in accepted teacher bridges enter the same distillation pool, with repeated occurrences preserved rather than collapsed by graph identity. Excluding the validation continuations separates the behavioral evidence used to select supervision from the additional supervision itself. Subsequent rollouts reconstruct the graph and candidate scores, adapting the supervision distribution to the updated policy without presuming that every accepted candidate has become mastered. 

Default settings. The default configuration uses M = 4 , B = 4 distinct nodes, K = 1 , H = 3 , and η = 0 ,with frontier_node_weighting=trajectory_balanced and no per-trajectory candidate quota. 

Collection cost. For N collected occurrences and U eligible distinct nodes, graph assembly and trajectory-balanced aggregation require O(N ) time apart from state-key normalization, followed by O(U log U ) sorting to select the first B nodes and O(N + U ) storage in the selector. Beyond ordinary rollouts and prefix replay, validation requires at most B(K + H) additional generation turns per task and the corresponding probability evaluations, whereas at most BK teacher responses enter training. Since rejected candidates consume collection resources without contributing bridge data, collection cost and accepted supervision volume must be considered separately. 15 D Extended Related Work 

Distillation and the supervision distribution. Knowledge distillation transfers predictive information through teacher outputs (Hinton et al., 2015), while sequence-level distillation extends the target to teacher-generated sequences for autoregressive students (Kim and Rush, 2016). These formulations distinguish the supervision target from the distribution of sequences on which it is applied, a distinction central to on-policy distillation’s use of student-generated responses (Agarwal et al., 2024) and MiniLLM’s study of on-policy reverse-KL optimization (Gu et al., 2024). Frontier-OPD addresses the acquisition of additional supervision within interactive trajectories, retaining ordinary student experience while selectively adding short teacher responses. 

Interactive imitation and curricula. Because learner actions determine the states encountered during execu-tion, dataset aggregation collects expert supervision under learner-induced state distributions (Ross et al., 2011), while scheduled sampling addresses a related training–inference mismatch by gradually replacing ground-truth prefix tokens with model-generated ones (Bengio et al., 2015). Agentic OPD extends the setting to language-conditioned environment histories, with TCOD adjusting student execution depth (Wang et al., 2026) and Guided-OPD scheduling teacher participation at individual turns (Li et al., 2026). Frontier-OPD’s aggregation of decision-level disagreement and history-specific validation operate at the level of supervision allocation, allowing both to coexist with curricula that determine the starting histories of student rollouts. 

Feedback in multi-turn agents. Environment observations and linguistic feedback support sequential behavior through approaches such as ReAct’s interleaving of reasoning and actions (Yao et al., 2023) and Reflexion’s use of episodic memory to improve later trials without parameter updates (Shinn et al., 2023). Within distillation, FutureBridge-OPD evaluates guidance through its induced student continuation (Chen et al., 2026), providing the rationale for assessing interventions beyond the teacher’s immediate response. Frontier-OPD combines this perspective with trajectory-balanced candidate ranking: shared observations organize evidence of learning needs, and matched-window agreement determines which teacher responses contribute to the subsequent parameter update. 

Interactive evaluation. AgentBench’s evaluation across diverse interactive environments (Liu et al., 2024) and WebArena’s assessment of functional task completion on reproducible websites (Zhou et al., 2024) emphasize capabilities that extend beyond isolated response quality. ALFWorld, WebShop, and ScienceWorld provide complementary settings for evaluating such grounded sequential behavior (Shridhar et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). Our evaluation measures autonomous execution after training, keeping teacher interventions confined to supervision acquisition and distinguishing task-level performance from the agreement scores used to select that supervision. 

# E Benchmark Descriptions 

ALFWorld. ALFWorld aligns text-based interaction with embodied household tasks (Shridhar et al., 2021); we use its text interface, in which agents receive instructions and observations and act through admissible commands. Coordinating navigation, object interaction, and task-specific conditions makes this setting suitable for examining whether localized guidance improves decisions under changing object and location contexts, with success rate measuring task completion rather than reproduction of a particular demonstration. 

WebShop. WebShop combines real-world product information with natural-language shopping requests (Yao et al., 2022), requiring agents to search, navigate product pages, select options, and complete purchases that satisfy the requested attributes. Task score captures graded fulfillment and success rate captures completion under the evaluation criterion, while page observations and available clickable elements provide the observational identity used to organize revisited states. 

ScienceWorld. ScienceWorld evaluates scientific reasoning through interaction in a text-based simulated world (Wang et al., 2022), where agents must translate goals into executable sequences involving objects, tools, and environmental changes. This setting tests whether local guidance can be followed by further 16 student-controlled procedural reasoning, with task score capturing partial progress and success rate measuring completion of the objective. 

Benchmark selection. The suite spans household manipulation, web information seeking, and scientific procedures, providing domains that share policy-dependent observations but differ in action interfaces, task structure, and feedback. The main comparison covers all three benchmarks, while the RL-teacher comparison and core ablations use ALFWorld and WebShop, and the sensitivity and efficiency studies use ALFWorld. This scope permits evaluation of a common supervision-acquisition method across distinct environments, without establishing generalization to unrestricted real-world settings.
