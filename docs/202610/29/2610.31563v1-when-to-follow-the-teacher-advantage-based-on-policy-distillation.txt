# When to Follow the Teacher: Advantage-Based On-Policy Distillation 

## Mahdi Sabbaghi *1 , George Pappas 1, Hamed Hassani 1, and Adel Javanmard 2

> 1

University of Pennsylvania 2University of Southern California 

Abstract 

Online Policy Distillation (OPD) often outperforms offline methods such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) by training on trajectories sampled directly from the student. However, standard OPD trains the student to follow the teacher at every state of the trajectory, assuming that teacher guidance is always beneficial. We instead distinguish between what the teacher prefers and what actually helps the current student succeed. We introduce Advantage-Based On-Policy Distillation (AOPD), which weights teacher actions by their counterfactual advantage under the student. Teacher actions that improve the student’s expected outcome are reinforced, while those that do not are downweighted. Directly computing these advantages requires trying alternative teacher actions at each step and running new rollouts from each. Instead, we show that the AOPD objective can be estimated using only standard student rollouts and their terminal rewards. Our estimator uses teacher importance weights on the student-sampled actions to provide unbiased advantage-weighted supervision, without any additional rollouts or reward evaluations. This allows AOPD to focus teacher supervision on actions that help the current student while preserving the rollout efficiency of standard OPD. Experiments on math, coding, and Medqa show gains over distillation and RL baselines in both external-teacher and self-distillation settings.

# 1 Introduction 

Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a stronger teacher to a smaller student model by training the student to imitate the teacher’s outputs. Offline distillation is a common approach that uses fixed teacher-generated trajectories. But this approach can lead to a mismatch between the training trajectories and the states the student generates itself. On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this mismatch by querying the teacher along student-sampled trajectories, providing dense token-level supervision at the states the student actually visits (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Lab, 2025; Song and Zheng, 2026; Ross et al., 2011; Li et al., 2026a). Although the trajectories now come from the student, the objective remains to match the teacher, without distinguishing which teacher actions improve the student’s chance of success (Liu et al., 2026). This limitation also applies to on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), which replaces the external teacher with a privileged version of the student itself, such as conditioned on ground-truth solutions (Zhao et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026). Whether external or privileged, the teacher may choose an action from which it can solve the problem, while the student cannot complete the remaining steps correctly. Conversely, distillation discourages a valid student solution when it differs from the teacher’s preferred approach. Thus the question is whether the teacher action at every state increases the student’s chance of success. An action should be credited according to its impact on the student’s task success, not simply the teacher’s preference (Chen et al., 2026; Saadi and Wang, 2026). Prior attempts to mitigate this optimization misalignment rely on making the teacher supervision more selective. A common approach combines outcome-based reinforcement learning with distillation, but adding a

Other methods downweight distillation when the teacher is uncertain, using entropy as a proxy, or when teacher tokens are implausible under the student (Jin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Alternative approaches validate teacher actions using auxiliary reward models (Saadi and Wang, 2026; Gu et al., 2026) or explicit intervention rollouts (Chen et al., 2026). These methods provide ways to identify useful teacher guidance, but rely on auxiliary signals or additional counterfactual trajectories. More importantly, they do not formulate the objective directly around the impact of the teacher action on the student’s expected task reward. Therefore, we ask: 

How should a distillation objective weight teacher supervision to improve the student’s eventual task success? 

We propose Advantage-based On-Policy Distillation (AOPD), the first formulation of on-policy distillation that directly weights teacher actions by their advantage under the current student. Intuitively, the advantage measures whether taking a teacher action and then letting the student finish the response improves expected final reward over following the student’s own policy. Since this advantage is defined with respect to the current student, the same teacher action can be useful for one student but unhelpful for another. AOPD therefore reinforces teacher actions with positive advantage and suppresses those with negative advantage. This is illustrated in Figure 1 (left). A direct estimate of these advantages would require generating new student continuations after alternative teacher actions. We instead derive an unbiased importance-sampling estimator of the AOPD objective using only the student’s on-policy trajectories and their terminal rewards. This allows AOPD to incorporate outcome-level rewards into token-level teacher supervision without learning a value function, generating counterfactual continuations or teacher rollouts, or relying on an external process reward model. 

Our contributions are summarized as follows: • AOPD algorithm. In Section 3, we provide an advantage-weighted formulation of on-policy distillation and derive an unbiased estimator of its objective, without additional rollouts or auxiliary models. We also discuss clipping the importance sampling weights. • Experimental results. In Section 4, we evaluate multiple model sizes on tasks such as math and coding under both external-teacher and self-distillation settings, showing improvements over OPD, OPSD, and GRPO. We further examine whether teacher actions are uniformly beneficial in an ablation study. • Reward improvement beyond imitation. In Section 5, we establish a reward-improvement guarantee for AOPD under an exact KL-proximal analysis and show that forward-KL OPD can reduce reward even with a stronger teacher. We further construct a two-state counterexample showing that simply adding an RL objective to OPD does not guarantee reward improvement. 22 Related Work 

Offline supervision. Supervised fine-tuning and offline knowledge distillation learn from fixed demonstra-tions or teacher-generated trajectories (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016; Wen et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). As established in imitation learning, offline supervision suffers from covariate shift and compounding errors when the learned policy visits states absent from the training distribution (Ross et al., 2011; Bengio et al., 2015; Agarwal et al., 2024). This mismatch is particularly relevant for autoregressive reasoning, where early deviations change all subsequent steps drastically (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). 

On-policy reinforcement learning. On-policy RL instead learns from trajectories generated by the current policy and has become central to LLM alignment and reasoning through methods such as PPO, RLOO, GRPO, and related policy-gradient algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Ahmadian et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Sabbaghi et al., 2026b). RLVR directly optimizes task reward but typically provides sparse outcome-level supervision and can struggle when useful trajectories are rarely sampled (Casper et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025). 

On-policy distillation. On-policy distillation combines on-policy sampling with dense teacher supervision by matching teacher and student distributions along student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Lu and Lab, 2025; Li et al., 2026a; Song and Zheng, 2026). OPSD further uses a privileged version of the student itself as the teacher, often conditioning it on ground-truth solutions or other privileged information (Zhao et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Penaloza et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). These approaches correct where supervision is provided, but generally still apply teacher supervision uniformly and throughout the student’s trajectory. 

Selective teacher supervision. Recent methods make this supervision selective using teacher uncertainty, teacher–student compatibility, local validity, verifier signals, or additional intervention rollouts (Jin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Saadi and Wang, 2026; Gu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). These criteria identify when teacher guidance may be useful, but do not directly define usefulness by its effect on the student’s eventual task reward. AOPD instead introduces a new formulation for on-policy distillation that weights each teacher action by its advantage under the current student and estimates this signal from the existing on-policy rollouts and terminal rewards.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate AOPD in two settings: (i) distillation from a stronger external teacher and (ii) self-distillation from a version of the student with privileged information. We compare AOPD against existing distillation and RL methods across math, coding, biology, and medical question answering. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Tasks. We conduct our experiments across four tasks: 

Math: We train on Open-Thoughts (Guha et al., 2025) and evaluate on held-out Open-Thoughts problems, AIME24, AIMO-Validation-AMC (AMC), and HMMT (AI-MO, 2024a,b; Dekoninck et al., 2026). 5Coding: We train on verified TACO problems (Li et al., 2023; Li, 2024) and evaluate on HumanEval, HumanEval+, and MultiPL-E (Chen et al., 2021; Cassano et al., 2022). 

Biology: We train on SciKnowEval (Feng et al., 2024) and evaluate on 50 held-out biology questions and the biology subset of MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024). 

Medical QA: We train on MedMCQA (Pal et al., 2022) and evaluate on held-out MedMCQA and the separate MedQA benchmark (Jin et al., 2021). 

Models and configurations. Following previous work (Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026), we use Qwen2.5 and Qwen3 models of different sizes (Yang et al., 2025). For external-teacher distillation, we use Qwen3-1.7B as the student, with Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the teacher for math training and Qwen3-8B as the teacher for coding. For self-distillation, we use Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B for math, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct for biology, medical question answering, and coding. All Qwen3 experiments use non-thinking mode for both the student and teacher. 

Baselines. For external-teacher distillation, we compare AOPD with OPD using forward-KL (FKL) and reverse-KL (RKL) objectives (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Lab, 2025), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), SFT, and KDRL (Xu et al., 2025), which combines OPD and GRPO. For self-distillation, we compare with OPSD and GRPO, using the forward-KL objective for OPSD following previous work (Zhao et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). Within each comparison, we use the same number of training iterations, a batch size of 32, and the same LoRA adapters with (r = 64, α = 128 ), while selecting method-specific hyperparameters. 

Hyperparameters. To preserve the support required by the importance-sampling estimator in Equation (3.6), we disable top-p and top-k pruning for AOPD. We use a sampling temperature of T = 0.8 , which we found effective for generating coherent Qwen responses. For OPD and OPSD, we use T = 1.1 , top-p = 0.95 , and top-k = 20 , following Zhao et al. (2026) and the settings that reproduce their best performance. For the main Qwen3 math and coding experiments, we use a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5 for AOPD. 

## 4.2 External-Teacher Distillation 

We first evaluate AOPD with stronger external teachers on math and coding. For math, we use Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the teacher because it performs better on Open-Thoughts than Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode. All methods train for 450 iterations on Open-Thoughts and 350 iterations on TACO. Math training uses a binary correctness reward. For coding, we use partial rewards based on five test cases, which we found more effective for training. We use G = 8 for GRPO and KDRL, following Xu et al. (2025), and G = 4 for OPD and AOPD. This is because our ablations show that larger groups provide little accuracy benefit for OPD and AOPD (Appendix A). For math, we use a maximum completion length of 8,192 tokens during training and 16,192 tokens at evaluation. 

Results. Figure 2 compares OPD (FKL), GRPO, and AOPD during training. On math, OPD improves quickly and then plateaus, consistent with observations in previous work (Yang et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). On the other hand, GRPO improves more slowly and requires more iterations to converge. AOPD also improves rapidly at the beginning but also continues to improve later in training. This is because unlike standard OPD, which enforces uniform supervision, our objective dynamically adapts which teacher signals to follow as the student improves. Although OPD and GRPO achieve comparable Open-Thoughts evaluation rewards, OPD performs better on AIME24, as shown in Figure 2 (middle). On TACO, OPD makes little progress in evaluation reward, whereas AOPD improves and remains above GRPO (Figure 2, right). Nevertheless, OPD (FKL) improves HumanEval and MultiPL-E performance over the base model (Table 1). Overall, our finding across the experiments is that the all distillation methods achieve better performance on benchmarks regardless of the training reward. It improves AIME24 by 2.4 percentage points and AMC by 3.3 points over the baselines. For coding, AOPD achieves the best scores on HumanEval. ## 4.3 Self-Distillation 

We next replace the external teacher with a version of the student conditioned on privileged information. For math, we use Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B, training for 400 iterations on Open-Thoughts with G = 4 for OPSD and AOPD. The maximum training completion length is 8,192 tokens. For the remaining tasks, we use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct for biology, medical question answering, and coding. We train models for 300 iterations with a maximum completion length of 2,048 tokens on medical QA, and for 400 iterations with a maximum completion length of 4,096 tokens on biology and coding.

## 4.4 Ablation: Is the teacher always useful? 

To test whether teacher-preferred actions necessarily benefit the student, we conduct a single-token intervention study with Qwen3-1.7B as the student and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as the teacher. We also consider a self-distillation setting, where the teacher is Qwen3-1.7B with access to privileged information. For a set of 512 student response on Open-Thoughts, we select a position either (i) uniformly at random or (ii) by maximizing the KL divergence KL (T(·| h)∥S(·| h)) between the teacher and student distributions. We replace the student’s token with the teacher’s highest-probability token, then let the student complete the response greedily under the same 8192-token total budget. We compare the final answer of this intervention with the original response. The results are shown in Table 4. Teacher interventions cause both improvements and failures: with choosing the highest-disagreement position, teacher actions help (i.e., changing the reward to 1) in 9.6% of cases but harm (i.e., changing the reward to 0) in 9.8%. The high unchanged rate is expected as single-token interventions on the entire trajectory can rarely flip the final outcomes. Interestingly, the privileged self-teacher provides more helpful interventions than the external teacher at uniformly random positions (8.6% vs. 6.9%). This suggests that self-distillation can provide more beneficial guidance in some settings, despite using a smaller model. This further highlights the importance of selecting teacher guidance based on its contribution to the student’s reward rather than the teacher’s preferences alone. 80 150 300 450

# 5 Theory: Reward Improvement with Advantage-based Distillation 

Although a teacher policy may achieve a higher reward than the student when evaluated as a complete policy, this does not imply that every partial update toward the teacher improves the student. In practice, training does not replace the student policy with the teacher policy directly; rather, it proceeds through a finite sequence of updates, often with a student whose capacity is limited relative to that of the teacher. Consequently, the teacher’s eventual advantage does not establish that intermediate training updates improve task performance. To characterize the effect of training on the student, we analyze a single update under different objectives and examine whether it increases the student’s expected final reward.

We introduced AOPD, an on-policy distillation objective that asks not only what the teacher prefers, but whether that preference actually helps the current student succeed. By weighting teacher actions with their advantage under the student, AOPD reinforces useful guidance and suppresses harmful imitation, while requiring no additional counterfactual rollouts or auxiliary value models. Across external-teacher and self-distillation settings, AOPD consistently improves over typical distillation and RL baselines, showing that effective distillation should optimize for student success rather than teacher agreement.
