ABSTRACT 

Training language agents is bottlenecked by the high cost of long-horizon rollouts, yet standard agentic reinforcement learning (RL) learns only from a terminal scalar reward, leaving the supervision in intermediate steps largely unused. Prior work exploits this supervision through value targets that estimate per-step success probabilities or observation targets that fit environment response. Both are hard to construct well: (1) value targets come from the same single scalar signal for each step in the trajectory, and (2) observation targets, such as web pages and terminal logs, are long and noisy and reach only one step ahead. We propose C OPE, which adds a third kind of multi-step action targets. On successful on-policy trajectories, C OPE trains the policy backbone to generate the action sequences that follows each history. Each action is also supervised when forecast from the preceding histories, forming a temporal ensemble that the shared backbone absorbs during training, and this enables acting and forecasting to co-evolve. The policy still acts one step at a time at inference, with no overhead. Across five benchmarks (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, τ 2-bench, and AppWorld), COPE (1) achieves stronger aggregate performance than GRPO and RL baselines with predictive auxiliary targets across simulated environments, while improving overall performance on both realistic applications; (2) delivers up to 1.9× and 2.0×

higher sample efficiency than ECHO and GRPO, respectively, on ScienceWorld; and (3) achieves these gains with a single policy backbone, without additional models, separate pretraining, or inference overhead. The codebase is available at 

https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/CoPE .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) enable autonomous agents to tackle complex, long-horizon tasks such as web navigation (Zheng et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), agentic coding (Yang et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2023; Sapkota et al., 2025), and scientific discovery (Zheng et al., 2025; Buehler, 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2025). However, training these agents remains computationally expensive. Because each rollout involves numerous interaction steps and tool calls, extracting the maximum learning signal from a limited number of interactions is crucial (Liu & van der Schaar, 2025). Yet, standard agentic reinforcement learning (RL) often learns only from a single scalar reward at the end of a trajectory, leaving stepwise supervision in the process, such as intermediate actions and environment observations, largely unused. To exploit more intermediate supervision from the process, prior work constructs two kinds of additional targets. Value targets are estimates of how likely each intermediate step is to lead to success, obtained from process reward models (Choudhury, 2025; Xi et al., 2026) or step-level credit assignment (Feng et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2026), and serve as dense rewards or advantages. 

Observation targets are the environment’s next observation or state. Some methods fit a separate LLM world model to them and use it to plan or to synthesize additional experience (Chen et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2026); others fit them as an auxiliary objective of the policy backbone, before or alongside policy optimization (Shrivastava et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026).

Key challenges . Both existing prediction targets are difficult to construct effectively in complex agentic environments. (1) Value targets are derived from only the shared outcome. A trajectory records only its final outcome, not the success probability of each step, so every step is labeled with this same outcome, whose randomness comes mostly from later actions and observations rather than from the step itself. Distinguishing between steps requires step-level annotations, additional rollouts from intermediate states, or exact recurrence of states across rollouts. (2) Observation targets are noisy and reach only one step ahead. In web navigation or agentic coding, most tokens of the next observation (e.g., HTML DOM trees or verbose terminal logs) are irrelevant to the decision (Fig. 1a), so the prediction loss is dominated by reproducing them. The target also covers only single-step transition; reaching later steps requires chaining predictions, where errors compound. 

Our solution . We propose C OPE, which constructs a third kind of target: multi-step action targets .Alongside GRPO, C OPE employs multi-step action forecasting on successful on-policy trajectories, predicting actions at:t+K directly from the current history while skipping the intervening observations. We show that the population optimum of this objective is Pr( at:t+K | ht, success ), a hindsight distribution (Harutyunyan et al., 2019; Russo, 2026) that conditions on both the history and the episode’s eventual success. Success thus enters as a condition that selects trajectories, not as a label assigned to each step. Therefore, C OPE overcomes previous limitations in three key ways. (1) Action targets are specific to each step. A successful trajectory labels each step with its own continuation, the actions that actually followed it. (2) Action targets are decision-relevant. Actions are concise (Fig. 1a), so the loss is not spent on irrelevant observation content. (3) Multi-step action targets span the horizon and form an implicit temporal ensemble. Each action is supervised both where it is taken and when it is forecast from the K preceding histories, and the shared backbone absorbs this ensemble into the policy through training alone. This agrees with the delay view of action chunking in robot learning, where predicting chunks trains each action from the current and several earlier observations; the resulting ensemble of delayed predictors, which ACT averages explicitly at test time (Zhao et al., 2023), accounts for much of chunking’s benefit (Lazzati et al., 2026). C OPE differs by still acting one step at a time at inference, with no overhead .

Main findings . We evaluate C OPE on five benchmarks spanning three simulated environments (ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2020), ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022), and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022)) and two realistic applications ( τ 2-bench (Barres et al., 2025) and AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024)). Our experiments yield three main findings. (1) Broad improvements: C OPE outperforms GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and observation-prediction baselines (ECHO (Shrivastava et al., 2026) and RWML (Yu et al., 2026)) on aggregate metrics in the three simulated environments, and improves overall metrics on both realistic applications (despite a negative result on the Airline domain of τ 2-bench). (2) Sample efficiency: on ScienceWorld, C OPE is up to 1.9× and 2.0× more sample-efficient than ECHO and GRPO, respectively. (3) Low overhead: on ScienceWorld, the action forecasting pass adds just 11% time per training step on average, while shorter rollouts reduce the measured end-to-end step time by 9% ; C OPE requires neither a separate model nor a pretraining stage. 2Preprint 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Sample-efficient agentic RL . Methods for sample-efficient agentic RL follow two main directions. The first makes better use of outcome rewards, either by refining credit assignment within trajecto-ries (Feng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Kong et al., 2026) or by converting completed interactions into extra policy supervision via hindsight rewriting or on-policy distillation (Hu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). These methods change how sparse outcomes are assigned or reused, without necessarily introducing a predictive objective. The second learns from the environment feedback available at every step, typically by adding auxiliary prediction of future states or observations to policy training (Zhang et al., 2026; Shrivastava et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). Such objectives provide dense token-level supervision but generally focus on a single environment transition. C OPE combines elements of both directions: success filtering reuses outcome rewards, while multi-horizon prediction learns from how trajectories unfold in action space rather than reconstructing observations. 

Multi-horizon prediction . C OPE is closely related to several forms of multi-horizon and outcome-conditioned prediction. Multi-token prediction trains language models to predict several future tokens (Gloeckle et al., 2024), while robotic policies such as ACT and Diffusion Policy predict sequences of future actions (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2023). Multi-token prediction is closest architecturally because multiple offsets are learned by one backbone, whereas action chunking is closest in the structure of its targets. Outcome-conditioned sequence modeling conditions policies on desired returns, while self-imitation emphasizes high-return trajectories (Chen et al., 2021; Oh et al., 2018); both connect policy learning to trajectory-level outcomes. Accordingly, the k = 0 term in COPE is success-filtered imitation, while the k ≥ 1 terms extend this supervision to multiple future action offsets. C OPE uses these predictions as an auxiliary objective during on-policy GRPO: targets are regenerated from successful rollouts, and the predicted future actions are not executed as a chunk but affect acting through the shared backbone.

We instantiate the acting policy πθ and the forecaster pθ with a single LLM backbone θ: πθ (at | ht)

is the model’s next-action distribution given ht, while pθ (at:t+K | ht) is the distribution of the chunk it generates after a forecasting instruction is appended to ht. The two are distinct distributions: even the first forecast position pθ (at | ht) is in general not equal to πθ (at | ht), since it is produced under the forecasting instruction. Sharing the backbone brings two benefits. (1) No additional model. Forecasting reuses the policy’s parameters, so there is no separate model to train, store, or serve. 

(2) Shared representation. This allows the forecasting objective to write what structure it captures directly into the representation the policy acts from, allowing the two to co-evolve (Sec. 5.3). 5.2 MULTI -STEP ACTION FORECASTING LOSS

6 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

Tasks . We evaluate on five long-horizon agent benchmarks with sparse rewards that differ in observation complexity, action space, and horizon. Three are simulated environments: (1) ALF-World (Shridhar et al., 2020) for household tasks, (2) ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022) for elementary science experiments, and (3) WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) for online shopping over long web-page observations. Two are realistic applications: (1) τ 2-bench (Barres et al., 2025) for policy-constrained customer service with a user simulator, and (2) AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024) for composing API calls across consumer apps with code as actions. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2024) for the three simulated environments and τ 2-bench, and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct for the more difficult AppWorld tasks. Details are available in Appendix A. Unless otherwise stated, all analysis experiments are carried out on ScienceWorld. 

Baselines . On the simulated environments (Table 3), we compare against three groups. ( 1) Prompting :Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite (Comanici et al., 2025), GPT-5-mini and GPT-5.5 (Ope-nAI, 2025; 2026), and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), which adds verbal self-reflection on failed trials. (2) RL w/o auxiliary loss : RFT (Yuan et al., 2023), rejection sampling fine-tuning; GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), critic-free RL with group-normalized advantages; and EMPG (Wang et al., 2025), which rescales GRPO’s step-level gradients by policy entropy. ( 3) RL w/ auxiliary loss : RWML (Yu et al., 2026), which first trains next-state prediction on environment transitions and then runs GRPO; and ECHO (Shrivastava et al., 2026), which adds a next-observation prediction loss to GRPO. Both supervise only single-step transitions, testing whether forecasting actions over multiple steps is more effective than predicting the next observation. On τ 2-bench and AppWorld (Tables 1 and 2), we include published baseline numbers reported by G2PO (Wang et al., 2026a) and InfoPO (Kong et al., 2026), together with GRPO and C OPE runs from our implementation. Because the published AppWorld results use a different task subset and protocol (Appendix B.1), our direct comparison is between GRPO (ours) and C OPE under our shared setup.

Q2: Why do multi-step targets outperform imitating successful actions? 

Lazzati et al. (2026) traces the benefits of action chunking in robot imitation learning (Zhao et al., 2023) to delayed prediction: a prediction of at has a delay k if made without the last k observations. Since a chunking policy predicts at from ot, o t−1, . . . , it acts as an ensemble of delayed predictors: a single delay recovers much of the benefit but falls short on harder tasks, while an ensemble over randomized delays matches chunking. In our setting, the k-th term of the forecasting loss in Eq. (4) is a delay-k prediction. Each action is thus trained at every delay up to K, as in chunking. The variants in Table 5 differ in which delays they train: success-filtered SFT and K = 0 train only delay 0, on πθ directly and under the forecasting instruction (Sec. 5.1) respectively; the invalid-action filter replaces each rejected K = 0 target with the next valid action, a single delay set by an environment-specific signal; and C OPE trains all delays up to K. Relative to GRPO, they change the success rate by −7.5, +2 .0, +5 .0, and +8 .0 points. 

Delay 0 carries little information about success . Success filtering keeps detours, suboptimal or invalid actions that the agent later recovers from, since they barely affect success. At such a step, the delay-0 target is the detour itself, while the recovery that decides success appears only at later delays. Imposed on πθ directly, the delay-0 target speeds up early training but later hurts it (Fig. 5). 

A single delay versus an ensemble over delays . The filter confirms that a delayed target helps (+2 .0 → +5 .0, Fig. 5), but it needs an environment-specific signal and covers only detours that the environment rejects. C OPE needs no such signal and trains every action at all delays, reaching +8 .0:delay 0, a single delay, and the full ensemble rank in the same order as for robot policies (Lazzati et al., 2026).

Behavior that succeeds on the easy tasks, such as refusing, therefore transfers to look-alike tasks that require action; these then succeed even less often, which skews the targets further toward leaving the database unchanged. GRPO is less exposed to this loop, since failed refusals on such tasks receive negative advantages, whereas the forecasting loss learns only from successes. 

9 ABLATION STUDIES 

Ablation on forecasting horizon K. The horizon K is the maximum future offset: the forecast chunk contains the current-action target at k = 0 and future-action targets through k = K. Short horizons keep the future targets close to the current history but capture less multi-step structure. Conversely, long horizons introduce irreducible noise, as the action k steps ahead depends increasingly on the intervening observations, which the forecast does not see. In Fig. 6, we compare the K = 0 

invalid action filtered baseline with forecasting horizons K ∈ { 1, 2, 4}. Every K ≥ 1 setting outperforms GRPO and the K = 0 baseline. K = 2 performs best, while K = 4 declines slightly as noise from distant predictions begins to dominate. 

Ablation on forecasting coefficient λ. Another important hyperparameter is the coefficient λ for the action forecasting loss. In Fig. 7, we sweep λ from 10 −4 to 10 −1 while fixing K = 2 . Notably, every tested value of λ consistently improves upon the GRPO baseline throughout training. We find that λ primarily controls the pace of learning: larger values accelerate early gains, whereas smaller values follow a similar but delayed trajectory. The two largest coefficients converge to similar peak scores, while the smaller ones still remain above GRPO. We adopt λ = 10 −2 for all agentic tasks.

10 CONCLUSION 

We introduced C OPE, which trains a language agent to forecast its own future actions alongside acting. Rather than fitting noisy observations, C OPE trains on the success-filtered action chunk 

at:t+K , combining the current-action target with forecasts up to K steps in the future. Across five benchmarks, C OPE improves aggregate results over RL baselines. It also improves sample efficiency with little computational overhead. We hope this work motivates further methods for sample-efficient long-horizon agentic RL.

ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work introduces C OPE, a training algorithm for improving the sample efficiency of reinforcement learning for LLM-based agents; it is a methodological contribution rather than a deployed system, and its societal impact is largely mediated by how downstream practitioners apply it. All experiments are conducted on standard agentic benchmarks (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, τ 2-bench, and AppWorld) using open-weight or proprietary models strictly for research evaluation; the work does not involve human subjects, crowdsourcing, or the collection of new personal data. By coupling action forecasting and policy optimization within a single backbone, C OPE can reduce environment-interaction costs without training or serving a separate world model, lowering the barrier for resource-constrained groups to study long-horizon LLM agents. We also note that, like other advances in sample-efficient agentic RL, more capable autonomous agents could in principle be misused for tasks such as automated misinformation or unauthorized web automation; practitioners building on COPE in higher-stakes domains should pair it with appropriate safety evaluation, deployment-time monitoring, and human oversight. 10 Preprint

B EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

B.1 BASELINE METHODS 

We compare our method with prompting and training baselines spanning verbal self-reflection, rejection-sampling fine-tuning, online reinforcement learning, and observation-space predictive supervision. 

Reflexion. Reflexion is a prompting baseline that asks the agent to verbally reflect on a failed trial and uses the resulting reflection to guide a subsequent attempt, without updating model parameters. We apply Reflexion to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld. 

RFT. Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) samples trajectories, retains those that succeed, and fine-tunes the policy by maximum likelihood on their action tokens. Unlike GRPO, it treats the retained trajectories as supervised targets rather than optimizing a group-relative policy-gradient objective. We evaluate RFT on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld. 

GRPO. GRPO is an online reinforcement learning baseline. For each training query, the actor samples a group of trajectories from the current policy and optimizes the policy using group-relative advantages. Different from critic-based PPO, GRPO estimates advantages within each sampled group and therefore avoids training an additional value model. In our experiments, GRPO is applied to ALFWorld, WebShop, SciWorld, τ 2-Bench, and AppWorld. 17 Preprint

AppWorld numbers reported by G2PO. The published AppWorld RL baseline numbers reported by G2PO use only difficulty-1/2 tasks: 72 training tasks and 105 tasks from test_normal , whereas we train on all 90 tasks and evaluate the complete test_normal (168 ) and test_challenge 

(417 ) splits. The reported setup also randomly samples its validation pool and differs from ours in prompt, history, and output formats, generation limits ( 512 versus 1,024 tokens per turn), and training length ( 50 versus 100 steps). These published numbers are therefore not controlled comparisons; nevertheless, both GRPO (ours) and C OPE achieve higher normal-split success rates despite our broader evaluation set. 

RWML. RWML is a two-stage world-model-based baseline. In the first stage, the backbone is trained on pre-collected (ht, a t, o t+1 ) triplets to predict the next state, using rewards derived from matching the predicted and actual states. In the second stage, the resulting model is optimized with standard online GRPO on the task reward. Unlike ECHO and C OPE, its world-model objective is applied only before policy optimization rather than alongside it. 

ECHO. ECHO is a world-model-augmented reinforcement learning baseline that combines the GRPO loss on action tokens with an auxiliary cross-entropy loss on environment-observation tokens. It reuses the same rollouts, model backbone, and forward-pass logits as policy optimization, thereby providing dense predictive supervision without additional environment interactions or a separately pretrained world model. Unlike RWML, which learns its world model from offline trajectory data before policy optimization, ECHO couples observation-space world modeling directly with online policy updates. We use the same GRPO hyperparameters for ECHO and set the environment-prediction coefficient to 0.01 on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld. 

EMPG. EMPG is an entropy-modulated policy gradient baseline that re-calibrates the learning signal based on step-wise predictive uncertainty. Unlike standard policy gradients, where update magnitude is implicitly coupled with action entropy, EMPG amplifies updates for confident correct actions, penalizes confident errors, and attenuates updates from uncertain steps. An additional future-clarity bonus encourages the agent toward lower-entropy next states. In our experiments, we apply EMPG on top of GRPO and evaluate it on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SciWorld. Score. The score measures the average environment reward or normalized task score. Different from purely binary task completion, some benchmarks provide graded feedback that reflects partial progress during task execution. For example, an agent may receive non-zero reward for completing intermediate steps even if the final task is not fully solved. Therefore, we report the average score to capture such process-level progress. 

Success rate. The success rate measures the fraction of tasks that are fully completed. A task is counted as successful only when the environment evaluator determines that the final goal has been achieved. This metric directly reflects end-to-end task completion ability. For benchmarks with subcategories, we compute the same metrics within each group. Except for 

τ 2-Bench, the overall result is computed over the full evaluation set and is therefore a count-weighted average over all instances. For τ 2-Bench, we instead report the unweighted arithmetic mean of the retail, airline, and telecom domain-level results.
