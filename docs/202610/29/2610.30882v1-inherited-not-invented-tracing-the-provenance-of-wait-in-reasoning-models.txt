Abstract 

Reinforcement learning (RL) is credited with teaching reasoning models to write wait be-fore reconsidering a step, yet large RL ac-curacy gains do not require the word. This view can confuse what RL discovers with what a model inherits from its training data. We trace the word through open models’ train-ing histories, reading it with label-free indices of frequency, position, form and usage and an injected-error test, all computed from sam-pled outputs. We find that zero-RL almost never coins the marker: on bases that lack the word, RL raises MATH-500 accuracy substan-tially while wait stays near absent. It arrives with training data: mid-training in OLMo-3, and fine-tuning on reasoning traces installs it in most models whose base lacks it. Where the base already uses it, both released RL-Zero recipes (OLMo-3, MiMo) multiply it, whereas KL-free GRPO from the same base does not; OLMo-3’s recipe wears down its form and makes it a more sensitive but more liberal alarm; RL after supervised fine-tuning barely changes it, and our GRPO recipe erases a marker seeded by fine-tuning. Finally, we show that marker frequency alone does not indi-cate RL-induced reasoning and caution against LLM-as-a-judge revision metrics, as automated judges can reverse human-labelled trends.

2 Background and Related Work 

Pre-RL origin of reflective markers. Outcome-based RL with verifiable rewards trains a model on its own sampled solutions and rewards only the final answer (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). Technical reports have described reflection emerg-ing during these training runs, measured by count-ing words like wait , recheck and verify (Guo et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2025). Two lines of work qualify this timeline: base models exhibit reflective behaviour before RL (Liu et al., 2025d; Essential AI et al., 2025; Yeo et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025), and verifiable rewards mostly sharpen this existing capability (Yue et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b; Shao et al., 2026); mid-training dictates RL responsiveness (Wang et al., 2025d; Zhang et al., 2026), and distilled models use hes-itation words far more often than zero-RL mod-els (Hu et al., 2025b). While these works have achieved commendable progress in locating the be-haviour before RL, the developmental origin of the marker form remains open . We date the ar-rival of the form within a single open lineage and track it through RL from bases with and without it. 

Functional interpretation and temporal dynam-ics. The functional interpretation of linguistic markers in final models remains actively disputed. Post-answer reflections are mostly confirmatory (Kang et al., 2025), many reasoning steps are deco-rative (Zhao et al., 2025a), and suppressing marker tokens costs RL-trained models little (Wang et al., 2025a), yet inflections occur where probes register belief shifts (Boppana et al., 2026). Interventions have tested these tokens by deleting, appending or inserting markers (Qian et al., 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2025). Chain-of-thought monitors and uncer-tainty heuristics treat these words as evidence of reasoning (Korbak et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025; Pandey et al., 2026), while behaviour labels of-ten rely on LLM judges of uneven validity (Zheng et al., 2023; Bavaresco et al., 2025). These studies examine final models only, without the axis of train-ing time. We introduce this temporal dimension, identifying where a word enters a model’s history and measuring how each training stage alters its meaning for future model deployment. 

Grammaticalization as a tracking framework. 

Grammaticalization provides a measurable frame-work from historical linguistics to explain how a word becomes a grammatical marker (Hopper and Traugott, 2003). The process requires a source con-struction already in use; it then raises the marker’s frequency (Bybee, 2003), fixes its position, wears down its form (attrition) and binds it to neigh-bouring material (Lehmann, 2015). Researchers can measure these parameters directly from oc-currences, just as lexical-semantic-change meth-ods quantify change from usage (Hamilton et al., 2016; Giulianelli et al., 2020), making them suit-able for tracking model checkpoints. Language also changes under optimisation in learning agents: communication optimised for reward drifts from natural language (Lewis et al., 2017; Lazaridou et al., 2020), and o3 has developed hard-to-read terms that grew more frequent over RL (Schoen et al., 2025). We using these grammaticalization parameters as a rigorous vocabulary and construct a set of predictions fixed before the main run. 

3 Method 

To analyse language evolution during training, we treat the samples of one model across its check-points as a diachronic corpus. We construct a marker’s history from its occurrences through the four stages introduced in Figure 2. 

Reasoning lineages To isolate the origins of rea-soning markers, we analyse three distinct classes of model history. (i) Fully open lineages with all stages released: OLMo-3 7B from pretrain-ing through mid-training to base (Team Olmo et al., 2025), followed by RL-Zero or SFT/pref-erence/RL tuning (OLMo-3 Think; 32B replica-tion: OLMo-3.1 Think). (ii) RL-Zero runs from marker-free bases: our GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math (1.5B with two seeds, 7B), plus DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) and Open-Reasoner-Zero (Hu et al., 2025a) on Qwen2.5-32B. (iii) Fine-tuned descendants on reasoning traces from Qwen2.5-32B (QwQ-32B, R1-Distill), Phi-4, Qwen3, Llama, GLM, MiMo, and SmolLM3. Sections A.1 and B.2 detail all 120 endpoints and recipes. Each checkpoint evalu-ates MATH-500 problems (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024) using fixed decoding per series, sampling 2–8 responses per problem.

Where the Marker Exists, Released RL-Zero Recipes Multiply It. Both released RL-Zero recipes multiply the marker. Figure 3 follows the two OLMo-3 trajectories. The Qwen2.5 GRPO runs emit fewer than 100 occurrences at every checkpoint and are omitted. Along OLMo-3 RL-Zero, the rate of wait rises from 4.9 to a peak of 17.0/ 1k tokens at step 1900, ending at 12.4 at the released final checkpoint (Figure 3a; Holm-adjusted p < 0.001 ). Over 6 decoding seeds, the final rate is 12.68 ± 1.55 (Table 2). The size of the rise depends on decoding: at temperature 0.6 instead of 1.0, the final checkpoint writes 76.5 oc-currences per 1k tokens compared to 5.1 for the base, and 29.1% of its responses reach the length limit (base 17.8%). We therefore compare rates only within a series at fixed settings. The rise also depends on the recipe: run for 400 steps from the same base and evaluated identically, our KL-free GRPO recipe does not multiply the marker, ending at 0.81 times the base’s rate on all responses and 0.93 on finished ones, against 1.58 for the released run, with disjoint intervals at steps 200 and 400. With a small KL penalty, the registered outcome is undetermined (Section D.2). RL-Zero on another base that already writes the marker also multiplies it (MiMo-7B: 4.95 to 6.62). RL after fine-tuning on traces in Phi-4 (1.56 to 2.58), DeepScaleR (3.65 to 4.07 among finished responses) and Light-R1 (2.79 to 3.21) shows similar amplification. 

Our GRPO recipe erases a marker seeded by fine-tuning. In a registered transplant, we fine-tune Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on 512 correct traces whose sentences open with Wait, and then train it with our GRPO recipe. After fine-tuning, the model writes wait 7.52 times per 1k tokens. Af-ter 400 RL steps, it writes none (0 occurrences in 3,066,514 tokens). Accuracy rises from 41.0% to 77.6%, and responses shrink from 2,466 to 767 to-kens. As wait disappears, the neutral Next, grows 4.83-fold in the same runs. When the seed traces carry Next, instead, RL keeps 0.12 of it. This run has a confound: 48.4% of the fine-tuned responses reach the 3,072-token limit and earn no reward, so RL also shortens responses. The arm seeded with 64 traces, whose fine-tuned responses reach the limit less often (18.5%), loses the marker as well (1.60 per 1k after fine-tuning; 2 occurrences in 2,523,676 tokens after RL). The marker falls just as fast among finished responses (6.91 to 0.0 per 1k). 5 Discussion 

RL reshapes inherited markers; it rarely coins them. A rise in wait during RL does not show that RL introduced the marker; the starting check-point may already use it. In every lineage traced but one, the marker possesses a prior source: mid-training data, a reasoning-rich pretraining mixture, or fine-tuning on traces. Reports of an “aha mo-ment” should state whether the base already writes the marker, and how often. Marker counts alone therefore cannot show that reflection was newly acquired. 

Marker frequency and meaning shift across training. Marker frequency and meaning shift across training, which undermines uncertainty heuristics that read wait as a fixed signal of doubt. Along OLMo-3 RL-Zero, the marker fires more often after errors and correct steps alike. The ratio rises, so when it fires it remains a strong signal, but its overall frequency changes. RL after supervised fine-tuning leaves the alarm almost unchanged. A marker’s evidential weight requires direct measure-ment on the specific checkpoint, for example with the label-free injected-error test of Section 3. 

Automated behavioural judgments require hu-man validation. Judge-labelled behaviour rates scale, but one of our judges reversed the human-labelled trend despite moderate item-level agree-ment. Direct marker measurements must therefore support lexical claims, and behavioural judgments need human validation. 

Language change under optimisation is recipe-specific rather than a single cline; Section D.1 re-lates our results to grammaticalization theory. 

6 Conclusion 

We find that zero-RL almost never coins the reflec-tion marker wait across the training history of open models. From bases lacking the marker, zero-RL raises accuracy and keeps the marker below 0.05 per 1k tokens in most runs. The marker arrives in-stead with mid-training, reasoning-rich pretraining, or fine-tuning on traces. When pretraining or mid-training supplies the marker, both released RL-Zero recipes multiply it, while KL-free GRPO from the same base does not. OLMo-3’s recipe erodes its form and makes it a more liberal alarm. In OLMo-3, RL after supervised fine-tuning changes its rate little, and RL erases a marker seeded by light fine-tuning. Understanding reasoning traces starts with knowing where their words originate. By mapping these developmental trajectories, we establish that training data seeds reflection markers while specific RL recipes dictate their ultimate survival guiding the future reasoning model training. Limitations 

Our provenance evidence is observational. The compared lineages differ in data and recipe along-side the marker. The zero-RL result rests on 18 runs from Qwen2.5, Mistral and Qwen3 bases. Each open checkpoint series constitutes a single training run, so its intervals capture evaluation sampling variance rather than training variance. All RL runs involve 0.5B–32B models trained with outcome rewards on English mathematics. Frontier-scale RL, other domains, other languages, and process rewards remain untested. Our measurements capture the lexical form rather than underlying reflective behaviour. Amodel can reflect without writing wait , and mark-ers outside our inventory escape detection. Judge-based quantities agree weakly to moderately with the human consensus labels (Section D.3). The audits are consensus labels on a sample, with no independent double annotation, so inter-annotator agreement is not reported; because they remain descriptive, our headline claims rest on label-free indices and pre-registered tests. The alarm test cov-ers only the arithmetic errors we inject. Several pre-dictions we registered did not hold (Section D.2). 

Ethical Considerations 

Artefacts. We use publicly released checkpoints, datasets and code under their licences (Sec-tion A.2); no model was trained on personal data by us, and our runs use a public mathematics dataset. Code and data are at https://anonymous.4open. science/r/wait-provenance/ : the provenance map, the result keys, the label-free index suite and the three expert-consensus audits, under CC-BY-4.0 (Section A.2). 

Human participants. No crowd workers were involved. In three blind validity audits of model-generated mathematics, the third with further marker occurrences (Section D.3), labels are con-sensus labels from a panel of ten annotators with PhD-level training in related fields, agreed through discussion; an author recorded them with a soft-ware tool that suggested no answers. All other reference labels were produced by LLM raters and are described as such. 

Dual use. Showing that a reasoning marker changes its evidential weight with training could help someone evade a monitor that reads such mark-ers. The effect we report is a drift that arises with-out any adversary, and the remedy is simple and public: calibrate any marker-based reading on the checkpoint being read. We therefore see the benefit to monitor builders as outweighing the risk. 

AI assistance. AI assistants were used for code writing and polish but the whole research is design and checked by researchers.

B.1 From Parameters to Measurements 

This section maps each grammaticalization param-eter to the index that measures it. B.2 Method Details 

This section details the models, data, indices and alarm test of Section 3. 

Models and checkpoints. We need RL trajec-tories whose intermediate checkpoints are public, from bases that differ in origin. The primary series is OLMo-3-7B RL-Zero-Math, trained with RL di-rectly from the fully open OLMo-3 base (Team Olmo et al., 2025); a fully open base, with its pre-training checkpoints, lets us test rather than assume where the marker comes from (S1-pre), a ques-tion raised for Qwen bases, whose RLVR gains depend on behaviours already present before RL (Shao et al., 2026). The second series, OLMo-3-7B-Think, applies RL to a model that already reasons after supervised fine-tuning and preference tun-ing, so the markers exist at t0 and we test whether the cline continues. For provenance we compare these trajectories with released endpoints grouped by their documented recipe (Table 13): zero-RL runs from bases that do not use the marker, our own GRPO runs among them, trained with verl (Sheng et al., 2025) on Qwen2.5-Math (Yang et al., 2024) and Qwen3-4B bases; descendants of one base trained on reasoning traces, QwQ-32B (Qwen Team, 2025) and R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (Guo et al., 2025) from Qwen2.5-32B; and the pretraining and mid-training branches of the OLMo-3 base (S1-pre). OLMo-3.1-32B-Think replicates the Think trajectory at 32B, and gpt-oss-120b at three rea-soning efforts is an endpoint without a released base. A secondary GRPO run from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (no licence metadata; robustness only) and the supervised fine-tuning trajectory of the OLMo-3 thinker (same base, no RL) serve as controls. We use 96 checkpoints in total. A checkpoint is any evaluated point of a training series; an endpoint is a released or final model, of which the provenance map lists 120; the zero-RL count uses base–final pairs (18 runs from 9 bases); identifiers, revisions and licences are in Section A.1. 

Data and sampling. A seeded-marker control. To test the prove-nance claim causally, we registered a transplant experiment before running it. A base that does not use the marker (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) is first fine-tuned on a small number N ∈ { 64 , 512 } of correct reasoning traces in which sentences open with Wait, , and then trained with the same GRPO recipe as our zero-RL runs. Comparing the marker’s rate after fine-tuning with its rate after RL separates what the traces install from what RL amplifies, and the Next, arm separates the form of an opener from its function. The seed traces are short, finished and use wait only as a sentence-initial opener. Results are in Section 4 and Section D.2. 

Interventions. The operator test inserts a form after a sentence boundary at which the prefix is correct and the checkpoint’s own next sentence continued rather than revised, so the insertion is counterfactual. Arms are Wait, (the operator form),  

> Hold on,

and Hmm, (members of its paradigm), the neutral filler Next, (the reference), Okay, and no insertion. Hmm, is itself a reflection marker (Qian et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026) and Okay, is the opener cue of concurrent work (reasoning-cues, 2026), so neither can serve as a null; we keep Okay, for com-parability with that work. A secondary set adds a random function word of matched length, which tests whether any insertion perturbs the model. The base arm is crossed: prefixes derived from base traces and from late traces are each contin-ued by the base and by the late policy, so a null in the base model cannot be an artefact of an off-distribution prefix. The outcome is whether the first three sentences revise the step, judged by an open-weight model that never sees the in-serted form (it is stripped before judging, for ev-ery arm), and whether the revision makes a cor-rect value wrong. D.1 Grammaticalization and What RL Could Do to a Marker 

This section summarises the theory and the predic-tions we derived from it. 

Grammaticalization. A lexical item grammat-icalizes when, in particular contexts, it comes to serve a grammatical function (Hopper and Trau-gott, 2003); the textbook case is the English mo-tion phrase going to becoming a future marker. Lehmann (2015) makes the process measurable with six parameters on two axes. Along the 

paradigmatic axis, a grammaticalizing sign loses 

integrity (its meaning bleaches), gains paradig-maticity (it joins a small, closed set of alternatives), and loses paradigmatic variability (its use becomes obligatory in its slot). Along the syntagmatic axis, its scope shrinks, its bondedness to neighbouring material grows, and its syntagmatic variability falls (its position becomes fixed). Bybee (2003) identi-fies frequency of use as the driver: repetition auto-mates a construction, habituation bleaches its mean-ing, and the frequent form becomes autonomous from its source. Hopper (1991) adds principles that hold during the change, of which two matter here: 

layering , the coexistence of old and new uses of a form, and persistence , the survival of traces of the old meaning in where the new form may occur. Discourse markers such as well or I mean develop through closely related paths (Brinton and Traugott, 2005). 

What outcome-based RL could do to a marker. 

The theory supplies the vocabulary for this process. Its first lesson, that a grammatical marker needs a 

source construction already in use, is the one that organises our findings (Section 4). Under outcome-based RL such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), all tokens of a sampled trace share its advantage. A marker that precedes a useful move, such as a re-check that sometimes catches an error, is therefore reinforced wherever it occurs in successful traces, not only where an error was present. What RL reinforces is the contingency ‘marker → move’, not ‘error → marker’. Repeated over many up-dates, this raises the marker’s frequency, which is Bybee’s driver. It concentrates the marker in the slots where the move pays off (fixation), makes it the default entry to the move (obligatoriness, a smaller paradigm), and loosens its dependence on the presence of an error (bleaching). We define two quantities for a marker m at checkpoint θt. Its 

report meaning is how much m tells about an error, 

Pt(error | m) − Pt(error ). We call m an operator 

when its effect on the model’s next move is procedu-ral and independent of its lexical meaning; its oper-ator force is the causal effect of emitting m on that move, Pt(revise | do( m)) − Pt(revise | do( m0)) 

for a neutral filler m0 (in our experiments Next, ). Section 4.1 measures the first with injected errors. In all but one of the runs we observe, outcome-based RL does not coin the reflection marker wait. Where the base model lacks it, zero-RL leaves it absent while accuracy rises; the marker enters with mid-training or with train-ing on reasoning traces; and where it is present, RL changes its frequency and form in a way that depends on the recipe and on the marker’s starting state. 

Rival accounts. Two alternatives predict the same frequency curves and different everything else. ❶ Honest report : RL improves error detec-tion and the marker reports it. Then the marker’s sensitivity d′ rises with its frequency, and inserting it after a correct step changes little, because the pol-icy checks the state rather than obeying the word. 

❷ Ritual : noisy credit assignment stamps in con-structions that do nothing, like the adventitious re-inforcement of Skinner (1948). Then the marker is frequent but causally inert under insertion and dele-tion, and rituals should be rarer when larger groups make advantage estimates less noisy. A third con-trast is internal to linguistics: frequency drives bleaching in grammaticalization (Bybee, 2003), whereas the law of conformity states that frequent words change less (Hamilton et al., 2016). The two make opposite predictions about which markers change fastest. 

Measurement. Section 3 defines the indices for-mally, and Table 11 in Section B.1 maps every parameter to its measurement. Before the main run we fixed six predictions with their falsifiers; Section D.2 states them and reports every outcome. 

Scope of the theory import. Grammaticalization is used as a source of predictions about a training process, not as a claim about how people process language or about the history of English. Its vo-cabulary (source, frequency, attrition) organises our measurements; most of its specific predictions about RL fail on our data, and we do not claim that RL is grammaticalization. 

Discussion: Language change under optimisa-tion is recipe-specific. Language change under optimisation operates as recipe-specific pressures on training data rather than a single cline. Grammaticalization or frequency? Frequency alone predicts only that a marker is used more often. Grammaticalization predicts, beyond fre-quency, that the marker becomes fixed in position, obligatory in its slot and one of fewer alternatives, that its link to errors weakens while its causal force grows, and that these changes do not reverse (unidi-rectionality, a strong tendency in language change; Haspelmath, 1999). Each of these can be measured on checkpoints and each can fail. Six predictions were derived from the thesis. Each is evaluated per series and holds if it holds on at least two of three series; the falsifiers and the order in which the primary tests are run were fixed before any result (Section D.3).

D.3 Statistical Procedures 

This section describes the resampling units, inter-vals and tests behind every reported number. 

Resampling unit. Occurrences are nested in traces and traces in problems, and every checkpoint of a series is evaluated on the same problems (the insertion experiment also uses the same prefixes for every arm and for the base model). All intervals are therefore 95% percentile intervals from a clus-ter bootstrap over problems (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993; Field and Welsh, 2007), with B = 2 ,000 

replicates for curves and B = 10 ,000 for the pri-mary estimands. One replicate resamples problem identifiers once and recomputes the statistic at ev-ery checkpoint, so differences and trends between checkpoints are paired. Series are never pooled. The open series each come from a single training run, so their intervals cover evaluation sampling only; training-seed variation is estimated only for our own GRPO runs. 

Trends and entropies. Trend claims use the Spearman correlation between step and index over the checkpoints of a series, with its bootstrap in-terval; no test is run at individual checkpoints. En-tropies are computed on the same number of occur-rences at every checkpoint (rarefaction, 100 sub-samples) with the Miller–Madow correction (Panin-ski, 2003), because the plug-in estimate is biased at small counts and the counts grow with RL. 

Signal detection. Hit and false-alarm rates re-ceive the log-linear correction before the probit transform (Hautus, 1995); d′ = z(HR) − z(FA) 

and c = − 12 (z(HR) + z(FA)) (Green and Swets, 1966; Macmillan and Creelman, 2004). H2 is a non-superiority claim: it holds only if the upper end of the 90% interval of ∆d′ lies below +0 .25 .

Insertion effects. The primary estimands are the risk difference RD op , the risk ratio and the odds ratio of revision after Wait, against the neutral filler 

> Next,

, with cluster-bootstrap intervals over prefixes. The risk difference is the headline scale because selecting cut points at which the model continued pushes the reference rate toward zero, where odds ratios become unstable; odds ratios use Firth’s penalty when a cell has fewer than five events (Firth, 1993). The amplification over the base model, Ramp = RD op (last )/RD op (base ), is eval-uated in the equivalent linear form RD op (last ) −

1.5 RD op (base ) > 0 whenever the base effect is close to zero. In the crossed design (prefix source ×

continuing policy), a GEE logistic model with pre-fix clusters estimates the arm × policy interaction adjusted for prefix source; a logistic mixed model with a random intercept per prefix is a secondary check. 

Tests. We registered a fixed order of primary tests per series, with Holm’s correction within steps (Holm, 1979). In the event we report trend tests (Spearman correlation with step and its permutation 

p-value) with Holm’s correction over the primary indices, and interval estimates for everything else; the registered gate sequence was not applied, and no per-marker test batteries were computed. 

Judge validity. Revision labels come from an open-weight judge (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, fixed before any audit; gpt-oss-20b, a different fam-ily, is reported beside it everywhere). We distin-guish consistency , agreement with reference la-bels produced by LLM raters (two Claude tiers on a 60-item overlap, inter-rater agreement 71.7, 

κ = 0 .54 ; Qwen3-32B on all items; adjudicated), from validity , agreement with the human consen-sus labels. A panel of ten annotators with PhD-level training in related fields labelled two blind audits drawn from the same pools, agreeing on one consensus label per item through discussion: 60 sentence-function items (audit 1; 59 labelled) and 40 insertion-outcome windows balanced by the judge’s own label (audit 2; items marked un-sure are included, and excluded as a sensitivity check). On the binary revise decision, audit 1 gives 

κ = 0 .19 for Qwen2.5-7B and 0.63 for gpt-oss-20b, and audit 2 gives 0.3 and 0.43 (sensitivity 26.8 %, specificity 93.9 % for the primary judge). The extended discussion of related work. 

Where reflection comes from. The “aha mo-ment” of R1-Zero, in which reflective words be-come several times more frequent and wait appears abruptly (Guo et al., 2025), has been questioned on the level of behaviour. Self-reflection already ap-pears in DeepSeek-V3-Base and in Qwen2.5 bases and does not raise accuracy by itself (Liu et al., 2025d,c); self-correction emerges during pretrain-ing (Essential AI et al., 2025), error correction is present in base models (Yeo et al., 2025), and re-flection is latent in pretrained Qwen2.5 (Gandhi et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). A second line finds that RL with verifiable rewards sharpens what the base already does rather than expanding it: it nar-rows the base’s distribution (Yue et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a), converges on output modes learned in pretraining (Zhao et al., 2025b), amplifies base be-haviours even under spurious rewards (Shao et al., 2026), and can be matched by sampling from the base (Karan and Du, 2026); prolonged RL may still extend the set of solutions (Liu et al., 2025b). On the other side, strategic phrases have been treated as tokens that RL discovers (Wang et al., 2026), and behaviours including self-verification are reported to emerge in zero-RL at the trillion-parameter scale (Tang et al., 2026), without the base’s own rate of the marker. These works locate the behaviour 

before RL. We follow the form : across one base’s pretraining and mid-training branches to where it enters, between zero-RL and trace-trained descen-dants of one base, and along RL from bases with and without it. DAPO’s case study of an emerg-ing “wait a moment” (Yu et al., 2025) is, at our measured rates, an isolated case. 

Distillation, mid-training and training dynamics. 

Distilled models use hesitation words such as wait 

and logical connectors far more often than zero-RL models (Hu et al., 2025b), discourse tokens differ between small-scale fine-tuning and large-scale post-training (Hwang et al., 2026), and model diffing suggests that RL teaches when to use mech-anisms the base already has while fine-tuning on traces installs new ones (Venhoff et al., 2026). Mid-training decides how well a base responds to RL (Wang et al., 2025d), and in controlled pipelines what RL can extend depends on exposure during pretraining and mid-training (Zhang et al., 2026). Checkpoint suites made such questions answerable for pretraining (Biderman et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2023), with word-level learning curves (Chang and Bergen, 2022; Chang et al., 2024) and linguis-tic regularities tracked across checkpoints (Kugler, 2025; Paape, 2026); open pipelines with released data and intermediate checkpoints extend this to post-training (OLMo et al., 2024; Team Olmo et al., 2025), and diversity collapse has been traced along OLMo 3 lineages (Karouzos et al., 2026). RL updates stay close to the base in KL and touch sparse subnetworks (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Mukher-jee et al., 2025), concentrate on high-entropy fork-ing tokens (Wang et al., 2025c) and reduce output diversity (Song et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025). We bring these strands to a single lexical form whose arrival can be dated within one open lineage and whose later reshaping can be mea-sured on public RL trajectories. 

Reasoning markers and their manipula-tion. Along zero-RL, Open-Reasoner-Zero and SimpleRL-Zoo track reflection keywords (Hu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2025), and SimpleRL-Zoo finds keyword counts and judged behaviours moving apart (Zeng et al., 2025); comparing whole training stages, RL suppresses exploratory steps such as hy-pothesising and backtracking (Maniparambil et al., 2026). Studies of final models disagree about what markers do: post-answer reflections are mostly confirmatory (Kang et al., 2025), self-affirming reflections are pervasive (Liu et al., 2025a), a large share of steps is decorative (Zhao et al., 2025a), thinking tokens trigger unnecessary reflection (Ding et al., 2025; Long et al., 2026), and markers are “surface cues” whose suppression need not remove reflection (Yu et al., 2026); yet inflections track probe-measured belief shifts (Boppana et al., 2026), and mid-trace shifts do not become more frequent across GRPO checkpoints (d’Aliberti and Ribeiro, 2026). “Aha” has also been described as anthropomorphic tone plus adaptive uncertainty (Yang et al., 2025b). Interventions delete markers, append or insert them, resample sentences or steer along latent directions (Wang et al., 2025a; Qian et al., 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2025; Ghosal et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026; Bogdan et al., 2025; Macar et al., 2026; Venhoff et al., 2025; Ward et al., 2025; Troitskii et al., 2025), and engineering work schedules or learns reflection tokens (Fan et al., 2026; Ringel et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b). Suppressing thinking tokens harms accuracy in Qian et al. (2025) but costs RL-trained models little in NoWait (Wang et al., 2025a), so deletion alone cannot tell what a marker does. Clusters of sentence-initial pivots have been called “reasoning operators” (Lee et al., 2026). Unlike these works, we follow markers along training with label-free diagnostics beyond frequency, and run an insertion test at every checkpoint with base-model and neutral-filler arms (Section D.2). Concurrent work releases rollouts in which trace openers are inserted into base models on the same OLMo-3 checkpoints (reasoning-cues, 2026). 

Monitorability, hedges and judges. Chain-of-thought monitoring reads traces for the model’s intent and treats this monitorability as fragile (Ko-rbak et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025); traces are not always faithful (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Arcuschin et al., 2025), legibility is not interpretability (Du et al., 2026), and whether RL erodes monitorability is contested (Guan et al., 2026; MacDermott et al., 2025; Little, 2026; Jose, 2025; Xiong et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a). Verbalised uncertainty can be taught and calibrated (Lin et al., 2022; Mielke et al., 2022; Kadavath et al., 2022), epistemic markers shift both model accuracy and user reliance (Zhou et al., 2023, 2024), and hedges and self-checks in reasoning traces serve as single-pass uncertainty signals (Pandey et al., 2026; Vanhoyweghen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Devic et al., 2025). Hid-den states reveal errors that the text does not (Zhang et al., 2025a; Yuan et al., 2026), and trace-reading overseers shift their criterion rather than their sen-sitivity (Chen et al., 2026b). Behaviour labels in this literature often come from LLM judges, whose validity is uneven (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Bavaresco et al., 2025): step-level error judg-ing is hard even for strong models (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c), and judges penalise expressions of uncertainty (Lee et al., 2025). Our results inform all three: the words monitors and un-certainty heuristics read are inherited from earlier training, the evidential weight of wait shifts with the recipe and the checkpoint, and judge-labelled revisions are not identified against human labels in our setting. 

Language change in linguistics and in learning agents. Discourse markers form a functional cat-egory (Schiffrin, 1987; Fraser, 1999), and prag-matic markers arise through grammaticalization and subjectification (Brinton, 1996, 2017; Trau-gott, 1989; Traugott and Dasher, 2001); whether discourse markers grammaticalize or rather prag-maticalize is debated (Heine, 2013; Degand and Evers-Vermeul, 2015). Grammaticalization theory describes how content words become grammatical markers (Hopper and Traugott, 2003; Lehmann, 2015; Bybee, 2003; Heine and Kuteva, 2002; Haspelmath, 1999), and lexical-semantic-change methods measure change from usage embeddings (Hamilton et al., 2016; Giulianelli et al., 2020; Ku-tuzov and Giulianelli, 2020; Schlechtweg et al., 2020; Giulianelli et al., 2023; Kutuzov et al., 2018; Tahmasebi et al., 2018; Periti and Montanelli, 2024), including the degree of grammaticalization (Schlechtweg and Schulte im Walde, 2018; Nagata et al., 2026). NLP work that learns from discourse markers treats them as stable signals (Nie et al., 2019; Sileo et al., 2019). In learning agents, com-munication for a reward drifts away from natural language or invents codes (Lewis et al., 2017; Kot-tur et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2019; Lazaridou et al., 2020; Lazaridou and Baroni, 2020); efficiency pres-sures shape emergent lexicons (Chaabouni et al., 2019; Rita et al., 2020); KL penalties (Ziegler et al., 2019), grounding (Lee et al., 2019) and iterated learning (Lu et al., 2020) counter the drift; and simulations with neural and LLM agents study lexical change and its limits as a model of hu-man language evolution (Ren et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Kouwenhoven et al., 2025; Galke et al., 2022; Stengel-Eskin et al., 2026). Reasoning models show related changes: R1-Zero mixes lan-guages (Guo et al., 2025), and o3 developed a hard-to-read dialect whose terms became more frequent over RL (Schoen et al., 2025). Outcome-based RL of a pretrained model is a large natural-language case of such change, observed on successive check-points of one model: in it a marker is inherited and then reshaped in a recipe-dependent way, rather than coined.
