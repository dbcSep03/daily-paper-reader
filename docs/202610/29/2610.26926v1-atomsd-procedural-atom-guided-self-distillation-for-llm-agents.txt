Large language models (LLMs) possess broad factual knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities, but solving complex tasks also requires procedural knowledge about how to act. Existing approaches often represent procedural knowledge through trajecto-ries or bundled skills, limiting flexible reuse across interaction histories. This paper introduces Procedural Atoms , fine-grained and independently applicable units that represent Intent , When , Do , and Avoid guidance. Building on this representation, we propose AtomSD , an Atom -guided Self-Distillation framework in which the learner continually extracts Procedural Atoms from its own successful trajectories and distills them into the evolving policy through Atom-conditioned supervision and task-reward optimization. Comprehensive experiments show that AtomSD consistently outper-forms strong reward-based and distillation methods, exceeding the state-of-the-art baseline by up to 9.31 points in average performance. Further analyses demonstrate that the proposed Procedural Atoms provide effective guidance, highlighting the value of atom-level procedural knowledge for policy learning. § GitHub 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have acquired extensive factual knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities, enabling them to tackle increasingly complex tasks across diverse domains (OpenAI et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026b). However, knowing the relevant facts does not necessarily imply knowing how to act. For example, a model may understand the properties of every reagent in a scientific experiment, yet still fail to execute the experimental procedure correctly. 

Such failures reflect the difference between declarative knowledge about a domain and procedural knowledge or planning knowledge about how tasks within that domain should be carried out. It becomes particularly consequential when successful behavior depends on sequences of interdependent decisions rather than isolated answers. In such contexts, an agent must determine which operations advance a goal, when their prerequisites hold, how intermediate results constrain later actions, and how its procedure should adapt as conditions change (Shridhar et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023a; Yu et al., 2026b). Therefore, learning reusable procedural knowledge is an important step toward translating the broad capabilities of LLMs into reliable performance in complex tasks. Toward this goal, existing approaches leverage interaction trajectories to construct and transfer reusable procedural knowledge, broadly following two directions. (1) Organizing trajectories into reusable guidance . Prior work transforms previous interactions into reflec-tions, workflows, skills, or conditional rules that can guide subsequent behavior through prompting (Wang et al., 2025; Mi et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Such representations abstract beyond individual actions and make past experience accessible

As shown in Figure 1, complete trajec-tories retain instance-specific objects, observations, and action sequences, while workflows and skills often bun-dle multiple operations into a single unit of guidance. Both can limit trans-fer across interaction histories: a new execution may require only a subset of the operations in a previous proce-dure, or may need to compose operations learned from different experiences. This calls for finer-grained procedural units that can be independently reused and flexibly composed across interaction histories. (2) How should procedural knowledge evolve with the learner? Ex-isting approaches often rely on separately maintained skill libraries or external models to derive and update guidance from interaction trajectories (Mi et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026). However, reusable procedural knowledge should ideally co-evolve with the policy itself: as the learner acquires new capabilities and succeeds on new interaction histories, the knowledge available for subsequent learning should be updated accordingly. In a word, the aforementioned limitations motivate us to ask: what form of procedural knowledge can be extracted from previous interactions, and continuously distilled as the learner evolves?

Distillation framework that couples continual procedural abstraction with online policy learning. To provide a reusable unit of procedural knowledge, we define a Procedural Atom as a fine-grained and independently applicable unit of procedural knowledge. As illustrated in Figure 1, a Procedural Atom consists of (i) Intent , specifying what the procedure is intended to accomplish; (ii) When , defining its applicability conditions; (iii) Do , specifying an ordered procedure; and (iv) Avoid , identifying operations or failure modes to avoid. With this goal-directed and condition-aware representation, individual Atoms can be selectively applied and flexibly composed across different interaction histories, rather than reused only in complete and inseparable trajectories or bundled workflows. In this way, procedural knowledge can better adapt to new interactions. To enable procedural knowledge to co-evolve with the learner, AtomSD establishes a self-improving loop through two complementary processes. (1) Online procedural abstraction .

At each training iteration, the student acts as its own knowledge extractor, abstracting suc-cessful on-policy trajectories into independently applicable Procedural Atoms. By sharing the resulting Atom set within the same task, knowledge extracted from one trajectory can guide other interaction histories, including both successful and failed ones. As the policy evolves, subsequent extraction continually refreshes the procedural guidance to reflect the learner’s growing capabilities. (2) Atom-guided self-distillation . An Atom-conditioned teacher, derived from the student, evaluates the original on-policy trajectories, transforming self-extracted procedural knowledge into token-level supervision across different interac-tion histories. Through joint reward optimization and self-distillation, this guidance is progressively internalized into the policy, whose continued improvement in turn supports further procedural knowledge discovery. Comprehensive experiments on widely used benchmarks (Shridhar et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023a; Guo et al., 2025b) demonstrate that AtomSD consistently outper-forms six strong reward-based and distillation baselines, while also showing competitive performance against advanced API-based models such as DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI 2AtomSD: Procedural Atom-Guided Self-Distillation for LLM Agents et al., 2026) and GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026). AtomSD exceeds the state-of-the-art baseline in average performance by up to 9.31 points, with improvements extending to unseen tasks. Further analyses show that fine-grained Procedural Atoms provide compact and effective guidance, validating the design of Atom-guided self-distillation. Our key contributions are as follows: • This paper introduces Procedural Atoms , fine-grained and independently appli-cable units that support selective reuse and flexible composition of procedural knowledge across interaction histories. • AtomSD combines online procedural abstraction with Atom-guided self-distillation, allowing procedural knowledge to be progressively internalized into the policy while co-evolving with the learner. 

2 Procedural Atom-Guided Self-Distillation 

As shown in Figure 2, this paper proposes Procedural Atom-Guided Self-Distillation as a self-improving loop in which procedural knowledge is continually extracted from on-policy interactions and internalized into the evolving student policy. We define Procedural Atoms as fine-grained functional units of procedural knowledge that capture guidance across four aspects: Intent , When , Do , and Avoid . As training proceeds iteratively, the student policy is updated at each training iteration based on its newly generated on-policy interactions. 2.1 Online Procedural Abstraction 

We represent an interaction trajectory τ as a task together with the sequence of observations and actions encountered during execution. From its own successful trajectories, the evolving student serves as the procedural knowledge extractor, abstracting reusable procedures into independently applicable Procedural Atoms. 

Atom Structure Formally, an Atom ω is represented as 

ω = ⟨ιω, Cω, Pω, Bω ⟩ , (1) where ιω denotes the procedural intent; Cω = {cω,1 , . . . , cω,nC } specifies the conditions under which the Atom applies; Pω = ( pω,1 , . . . , pω,nP ) defines the ordered procedure to execute; and Bω = {bω,1 , . . . , bω,nB } captures optional failure modes or inappropriate operations to avoid. Here, the elements of Cω, Pω, and Bω denote the conditions, operations, and avoidance items, respectively, while nC, nP, and nB denote their respective counts. Together, these components characterize what to achieve, when to apply the Atom, how to act, and what boundaries to respect. 3AtomSD: Procedural Atom-Guided Self-Distillation for LLM Agents 

Atom Example The following example illus-trates how a Procedural Atom ω instantiates the four components defined above. 

Intent (ιω ): Clean a held object using a designated cleaning facility. 

When (Cω ): The agent holds an object requiring cleaning; a suitable cleaning facility is available. 

Do (Pω ): Navigate to the cleaning facility if not already there; execute the cleaning action with the held object and the facility. 

Avoid (Bω ): Attempting to clean without reach-ing the facility; cleaning an object that is not cur-rently held; using an incorrect or unavailable cleaning method.

3.2 Main Results 

Table 1 summarizes the main results, with four key findings. (1) AtomSD improves beyond reward- and demonstration-guided training. AtomSD consistently outperforms GRPO, OPSD, and GRPO+OPSD in average performance at both model scales, exceeding the strongest baseline by 9.31 and 5.42 points at 4B and 9B, respectively. This highlights the value of Procedural Atoms beyond reward-only learning, demonstration-guided distillation, and their combination. (2) AtomSD provides broader gains than skill-level distillation. 

AtomSD outperforms Skill-SD and SDAR on most tasks at both scales, with particularly pro-nounced gains on ScienceWorld. These results suggest that even skill libraries constructed by stronger external models do not necessarily provide more effective guidance, highlight-ing the value of fine-grained Procedural Atoms. (3) Improvements generalize to unseen tasks. AtomSD leads on ScienceWorld unseen at both model scales, with improvements also extending to ALFWorld unseen. These results suggest that procedural knowledge learned through AtomSD can generalize beyond seen tasks. (4) AtomSD is competitive with strong LLMs without inference-time reflection. AtomSD surpasses Reflexion by 22.91 and 20.85 points at 4B and 9B, respectively. AtomSD-9B further exceeds DeepSeek-V4-Pro and trails GPT-5.5 by only 5.39 points in average score, suggesting that internalizing Procedural Atom guidance can reduce reliance on inference-time reflection while narrowing the gap to substantially stronger LLMs. We further provide qualitative case studies in Appendix D.1. 

3.3 Effects of Atom Components and Joint Optimization 

Figure 3 examines the contributions of Atom components and joint reward optimization. 

(1) The complete Atom representation provides more effective guidance. Including Intent improves the average score by 15.0 points at 4B and 1.4 points at 9B, while the complete representation outperforms removing both Intent and Avoid by 8.9 and 13.4 points, respectively, with similar trends observed in the training rewards. These results highlight the value of organizing procedural guidance within the complete Atom representation. (2) Explicit avoidance boundaries improve performance at both scales. Removing Avoid reduces the average score by 13.1 points at 4B and 14.0 points at 9B. This further highlights the importance of explicitly representing what to avoid, rather than distilling successful operations alone. (3) Joint optimization strengthens Atom-guided learning. Combining Atom-guided distillation with task rewards improves average performance by 25.3 and 25.1 points over Atom-only training at 4B and 9B, respectively. This supports the design choice of jointly internalizing procedural guidance while optimizing for task completion. 3.5 Analyses Training Dynamics Analysis Figure 6 (top) shows that AtomSD’s advantage emerges as training progresses. OPSD improves rapidly early on at both model scales, but its gains sub-sequently plateau, whereas AtomSD continues to improve and reaches the highest reward toward the end. Figure 6 (bottom) further shows that these gains accompany relatively low token usage and fewer interaction steps during later training at both scales. These results show that Procedural Atoms improve performance with more compact trajectories. 

Distillation Strength Analysis Figure 7 shows that increasing λ from 0.001 to 0.01 or 0.1 consistently reduces late-stage training rewards and average evaluation performance at both model scales. This suggests that Atom-guided distillation is more effective when properly balanced with reward optimization rather than simply strengthened, consistent with recent findings on self-distillation in agentic RL (Lu et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026). 

Teacher Update Analysis Figure 5 (middle) compares EMA with fixed and fully synchro-nized teachers. EMA achieves the highest average evaluation performance at both model scales; the fixed teacher remains competitive, while full synchronization yields lower late-stage training rewards at both scales. These results suggest that EMA balances preserving prior teacher knowledge with incorporating student progress. 

4 Related Work 

Experiential Memory and Procedural Knowledge Experience-based agents transform in-teraction histories into reflections, insights, and reasoning memories for reuse in subsequent decisions (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2026), while others leverage embeddings, KV cache, or special tokens to represent memories (Yu et al., 2025; 2026c; Wu et al., 2026b). Furthermore, prior work organizes histories into reusable workflows, conditional procedures, and explicit rules, enabling more selective reuse across interac-tions (Wang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026a; Mi et al., 2026). A complementary line of work models environment dynamics and action consequences to support planning (Qiao et al., 2024; Kim & Hwang, 2025; Liu et al., 2026b). AtomSD instead uses procedural abstractions as privileged context. Condition-aware Atoms guide policy learning and are progressively internalized into model parameters, without requiring external guidance at inference. 

On-Policy Distillation On-policy distillation applies teacher supervision to student-generated trajectories, internalizing privileged knowledge into model parameters (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026a; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026a). Privi-leged knowledge can be supplied as explicit textual context or encoded into learned latent representations (Liu et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). AtomSD uses indepen-9AtomSD: Procedural Atom-Guided Self-Distillation for LLM Agents dently applicable Procedural Atoms as the unit of privileged supervision, enabling guidance to be composed according to the student’s current interaction history rather than being tied to a specific demonstration trajectory or workflow. 

Skill-Guided Agent Learning Skill-augmented reinforcement learning optimizes agents’ ability to exploit and improve external skill libraries (Wang et al., 2026b; Xia et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). Skill-conditioned distillation instead uses skill guidance as teacher-side context to train policies that operate without external skill inputs at inference (Lu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2026). AtomSD constructs procedural guidance online from the evolving student’s successful trajectories, without relying on a preconstructed skill library or a separate external model for knowledge extraction. By continually decomposing trajectories into independently applicable Atoms rather than bundled skills, it supports selective reuse and flexible composition while allowing procedural knowledge to co-evolve with the learner. 

5 Conclusion 

This paper presents AtomSD, a self-improving framework that transforms successful on-policy trajectories into Procedural Atoms and distills their guidance back into the policy, allowing procedural knowledge to co-evolve with the learner. Comprehensive experi-ments and analyses show that AtomSD consistently improves agent performance, while fine-grained Procedural Atoms support selective reuse and flexible composition across interaction histories. Our results highlight atomized procedural knowledge as an effective bridge between raw interaction histories and parametric policy learning.

D.1 Procedural Dependency Patterns 

Across evaluation trajectories, we observe several recurring forms of procedural depen-dency that distinguish successful agent behavior beyond simply executing plausible action sequences. We highlight four representative patterns: (1) observation-conditioned branching ,(2) constraint-driven revision , (3) subgoal-state dependency , and (4) feedback-guided API recov-ery . Table 6 summarizes one representative case for each pattern, followed by detailed trajectory-level analysis. 

Observation-Conditioned Branching This pattern arises when the appropriate down-stream action depends on information revealed only through an intermediate observation. In contrast, unsuccessful baseline trajectories exhibit errors such as incorrect thermometer use, repeated invalid actions, and interactions with unrelated objects, failing to obtain the measurement needed for the subse-quent placement decision. Constraint-Driven Revision This pattern captures situations in which an initially plausible choice must be revised after newly observed information reveals a constraint violation. In WebShop task 1451 , the requested sweater must satisfy multiple constraints, including color, size, fit, and a price below $40. AtomSD first inspects a $100 candidate, recognizes the price mismatch, returns to search, and selects a cheaper product before configuring the requested color and size, obtaining a score of 100. By comparison, failed baseline trajectories exhibit incomplete constraint satisfaction, such as purchasing after selecting the color without configuring the requested size. The case highlights the ability to revise an intermediate decision rather than persist with a candidate that no longer satisfies the task constraints. 

Subgoal-State Dependency This pattern occurs when a later subgoal depends on a state established by an earlier operation. In an ALFWorld heat-and-place task, the potato must be heated before it can satisfy the final placement goal. AtomSD first acquires the potato, executes heat potato 1 with microwave 1, observes confirmation of the state change, and then places the heated potato in the garbage can, completing the task in eight steps. Meanwhile, failed baseline trajectories include irrelevant object manipulations that divert execution from the required acquire–heat–place sequence. This case illustrates the need to preserve state dependencies across subgoals, ensuring that the output of one procedure satisfies the requirements of the next. 

Feedback-Guided API Recovery This pattern arises when an API response is uninfor-mative or inconsistent, requiring the agent to revise its query strategy based on returned feedback. In StableToolBench task G1_instruction/32617 , the user asks for all products in the Clothing category and their total count. AtomSD initially receives an empty re-sult, then inspects the available categories and adjusts its requests until it retrieves nine valid products together with a reported count of nine. It subsequently verifies the result through additional pagination checks and grounds its final answer in the returned API data. Unsuccessful baseline trajectories instead exhibit continued querying despite obtaining nonempty results, without consolidating the retrieved information into a final answer.
