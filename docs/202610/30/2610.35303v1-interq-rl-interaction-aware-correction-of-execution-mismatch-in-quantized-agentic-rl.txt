## ABSTRACT 

Multi-turn agent reinforcement learning (RL) relies on long closed-loop rollouts, making generation and KV-cache costs a major bottleneck. Quantized rollout re-duces these costs, but its low-precision executor also serves as the behavior pol-icy and can induce execution mismatch with the learner. Existing quantized-RL methods are designed primarily for single-turn generation. They align the learner forward with quantized rollout or correct the current-token rollout–learner ratio, without modeling the policy history that produces each agent state. Even after quantization alignment, residual rollout–learner mismatch accumulates along the state-reaching policy history, so a current action may look locally aligned de-spite a mismatched history. We introduce InterQ-RL (Interaction-aware Quan-tized Reinforcement Learning) to correct this post-alignment mismatch. It applies bounded execution-prefix correction over the state-reaching policy history, allo-cates correction strength across action segments by effective support under a fixed displacement budget, and requires no additional model forward pass. We eval-uate dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) backbones from 4B to 30B on ALF-World, WebShop, and ScienceWorld, with all quantized arms sharing the same rollout with 8-bit weights, activations, and KV cache (W8A8-KV8). Across four environment–backbone settings, InterQ-RL achieves the strongest quantized end-point result, matching BF16 to within 0.7 points and exceeding it in two settings. It matches or exceeds the learning speed of alignment alone while avoiding its late performance regression. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

On-policy PPO/GRPO optimizes language-agent outcomes using fresh trajectories from the current policy at every update (Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024). These trajectories can span multiple turns of search, environment interaction, and tool use (Yao et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025). To scale this data-generation loop, modern systems decouple rollout generation from learning (Sheng et al., 2025; Kwon et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025). As interactions grow longer, autoregressive generation and the expanding KV cache become major compute and memory bottlenecks on the rollout side. W8A8 reduces linear-operator cost, and KV8 reduces the cache footprint of long-context rollout (Micikevicius et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2026). Yet the low-precision rollout executor is also the behavior policy. When the executor and learner evaluate the same checkpoint along different numerical paths, they can define different action distributions. We call this execution mismatch , which violates the behavior–old-policy consistency assumed by PPO/GRPO (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024). Prior quantized-RL work addresses training–inference mismatch (Zhong et al., 2026) in two ways: alignment matches the learner forward to quantized rollout (Gu et al., 2026; Xi et al., 2026a), whereas correction clips or reweights the rollout–learner ratio at the current token (Yao et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b; Qiu et al., 2026). Both lines focus on single-turn generation. They do not address residual rollout–learner mismatch that remains after alignment and accumu-lates over the policy history of multi-turn agents. In a closed-loop agent, earlier actions determine later states. Along the same realized trajectory, shared environment-transition factors cancel, but the ratios of policy actions remain (Sutton & Barto, 2018; Precup et al., 2000). The method first uses quantization-aware training (QAT) to align the learner forward with rollout quan-tization. Each policy-generated action is treated as one allo-cation segment. We characterize interaction-structured execution mismatch in quantized agentic RL: W/A+KV alignment leaves residual rollout–learner mismatch that compounds along the state-reaching policy history. 2. We propose InterQ-RL, which applies bounded execution-prefix correction over the state-reaching policy history, allocates correction strength across action segments by effective support under a fixed displacement budget, and requires no additional model forward pass. 3. Using a common W8A8-KV8 rollout, we evaluate three agentic environments and 4B– 30B dense and MoE backbones. Across all four settings, InterQ-RL achieves the strongest quantized endpoint result; its AULC is within 0.33 of QAT on ALFWorld and highest among quantized arms in the other three settings.

## 3 INTER Q-RL: A LIGNING EXECUTION AND CORRECTING INTERACTION 

## HISTORY 

InterQ-RL first aligns the quantization semantics of learner and rollout, then constructs a bounded correction surrogate by detached reweighting of the base PPO/GRPO loss. Scope determines which policy history leading to the current state enters the surrogate; allocation determines how fixed correction strength is distributed across action segments. InterQ-RL addresses these choices with execution-prefix correction and support-aware allocation, respectively (Figure 2). 3.1 ALIGNING THE POLICY THAT GENERATED THE DATA 

The first stage reproduces the rollout quantization semantics in the learner forward. Rollout uses blockwise W8A8 linear operators and stores K/V in FP8 E4M3 with per-token-per-head scaling. Standard QAT covers the W/A path. For the KV path we match the learner quantize–dequantize rule to the rollout cache-write kernel, at the same points in the layer. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Settings and comparisons. We evaluate on three multi-turn agent environments: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), and ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022). ALF-World and WebShop both use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and report on 134 valid unseen tasks and 500 official test tasks, respectively. Because Qwen3-4B provides limited learning signal on the more demanding ScienceWorld setting, we use Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, covering dense and MoE backbones (Yang et al., 2025); endpoint evaluation uses 1,819 official test instances. All runs use GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). Endpoint performance and training dynamics. InterQ-RL gives the strongest quantized result on every metric in Table 1, including the four that count fully completed tasks. It improves over QAT by 6.0 and 2.8 points on ALFWorld success and WebShop score and by 2.3 and 0.6 on the two ScienceWorld macro scores, and over the strongest of Flash-RL, AIS, and QuRL by 1.2 to 6.9 points. It matches BF16 within 0.7 points on ALFWorld success, 0.3 on WebShop score, and 0.5 on WebShop success, and exceeds it by 0.9 and 2.9 on the ScienceWorld macro scores and 2.0 and 2.6 on the solved rates. Figure 5 places the ALFWorld endpoint in its training context. QAT reaches 95.5% at step 300 but falls to 89.6% by step 400, while InterQ-RL is still at 95.5%. The endpoint separation shows that interaction correction sustains task performance. Flash-RL drops from 84.3% at step 370 to 39.6% at step 400. QuRL falls to 6.0% at step 270 as its training reward falls from a peak of 0.992 to 0.141, and terminates at step 278. AIS avoids collapse but learns more slowly and unevenly: it reaches 69.4% at step 150 versus 88.1% for InterQ-RL, drops from 79.9% at step 210 to 67.9% ten steps later, and reaches a 92.3% last-six endpoint versus 96.4% for InterQ-RL.

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Quantized LLM reinforcement learning. Quantized LLM RL builds on weight, activation, and KV-cache quantization (Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), and spans learner–rollout alignment and mismatch correction. FP8-RL quantizes roll-out W/A and the KV cache (Qiu et al., 2026). QaRL and Jet-RL align the learner forward with quantized rollout (Gu et al., 2026; Xi et al., 2026a). Flash-RL, AIS, and QuRL control training– inference mismatch through clipping, adaptive weighting, or update-aware quantization (Yao et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b), all in single-turn generation. InterQ-RL addresses residual execution mismatch along the policy histories of multi-turn agents. 

Policy optimization under distribution shift. Off-policy RL corrects distribution shift through importance weighting (Precup et al., 2000; Munos et al., 2016; Espeholt et al., 2018). LLM meth-ods span response-level objectives, cumulative-token ratios, prefix surrogates, and whole-sequence weighting or masking (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b; Lei et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026a). These methods address general rollout or policy-version mismatch at different granularities. InterQ-RL instead targets quantization-induced mismatch that remains after align-ment and allocates bounded correction across action segments by effective support under a fixed displacement budget. 

Agentic reinforcement learning. GiGPO and LOOP extend policy optimization to multi-turn in-teraction, targeting credit assignment and training efficiency (Feng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025); Agent-R1, AgentGym-RL, and SkyRL-Agent provide open training frameworks (Cheng et al., 2026; Xi et al., 2026b; Cao et al., 2025). InterQ-RL addresses a complementary execution-level source of distribution shift: mismatch introduced when the behavior policy runs in low precision and propa-gated through action-conditioned history. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We identified interaction-structured execution mismatch in quantized agentic RL: even after W/A+KV alignment, residual rollout–learner mismatch compounds along the state-reaching pol-icy history. InterQ-RL applies bounded execution-prefix correction along this history and allocates correction strength across action segments by effective support under a fixed displacement bud-get, reusing existing log probabilities without another forward pass. Across four settings spanning three environments and 4B–30B dense and MoE backbones under a common W8A8-KV8 rollout, InterQ-RL achieves the strongest quantized endpoint result in every setting, matches BF16 to within 0.7 points and exceeds it in two. C.3 QUANTIZATION AND CORRECTION CONFIGURATIONS 

Every quantized arm uses the same rollout stack: blockwise FP8 W8A8 linear operators and a per-token-per-head FP8 KV cache. QAT and InterQ-RL reproduce both rules in the learner forward, as specified in §A. For the ALFWorld design analysis, we additionally combine Flash-RL’s TIS and AIS with this learner configuration while retaining their correction rules and reported parameters. InterQ-RL retains verl’s default per-token log-ratio safety bound B = 20 .Baseline implementations follow each method’s published objective, using reported parameter val-ues where specified. Flash-RL applies token-local TIS to the rollout–learner ratio with bound C = 2 

(Yao et al., 2025). AIS keeps a truncated weight, at bound C = 5 , and gates how strongly it is applied by a per-batch coefficient that interpolates between the uncorrected and the fully weighted gradient; the coefficient combines an effective-sample-size term, a log-probability divergence saturating at 

δ = 0 .02 , and a variance-amplification term with tolerance γ = 1 .2 (Zhou et al., 2026). QuRL has two components that act at different levels (Li et al., 2026b). Adaptive Clipping Range (ACR) scales the PPO upper bound by the gap between the quantized and the full-precision policy, 

(1+ ϵ)/r i,t , which presumes a learner left at full precision. Our implementation follows Equation (9) of QuRL and does not apply dual-clip. Because QuRL does not specify the truncation cap C, we use C = 2 in all QuRL runs. Update-Aware Quantization (UAQ) rescales the base model once before training, at the published scale s = 1 .5, so that each RL update is large enough for the rollout quantizer to capture rather than round away. UAQ needs the RMSNorm in front of a linear to absorb the rescale. In an MoE block that norm is shared with the router, and rescaling it would change routing, so on Qwen3-30B-A3B we apply UAQ to the attention group only. Table 1 evaluates this ACR+UAQ recipe, including the stated MoE adaptation. Flash-RL uses verl’s built-in rollout importance-sampling path. No public implementation of AIS or QuRL was available during our experiments; we therefore reimplemented both methods from their papers and will release our implementations with the code.
