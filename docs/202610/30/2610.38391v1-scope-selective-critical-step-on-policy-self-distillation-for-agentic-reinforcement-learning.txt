Abstract 

Outcome-based reinforcement learning (RL) improves long-horizon LLM agents through task-level feed-back, but provides limited supervision over intermediate decisions. Our analysis reveals an outcome– step mismatch : successful trajectories can contain erroneous steps, while failed trajectories may include correct decisions. To provide finer-grained guidance, recent methods incorporate hindsight skills into on-policy self-distillation. However, we further find that applying trajectory-level skills across all steps can introduce off-target distillation signals at decisions unrelated to the intended corrections. Moti-vated by these observations, we introduce SCOPE , a framework for Selective Critical-Step On-Policy Self-Distillation . SCOPE first equips the policy with error-localization and skill-generation capabilities through lightweight supervised initialization. During online RL, the policy analyzes its own trajectories, generates step-specific corrective skills, and distills their guidance exclusively at selected error steps. A bounded signed distillation coefficient stabilizes auxiliary updates while preserving both reinforcement and suppression, and a success-relative pairwise length reward encourages efficient task completion. Experiments across three smaller backbones on ALFWorld and WebShop demonstrate consistent im-provements. With Qwen2.5-3B, SCOPE achieves 97.7% and 85.2% success, outperforming SEED by 5.9 and 6.3 percentage points, respectively. On Qwen3-32B, SCOPE achieves 99.2% ALFWorld success versus 85.2% for GRPO, while reducing average interactions by 65.0%.

1arXiv:2610.38391v1 [cs.AI] 28 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

LLM agents solve tasks through sequences of tool calls and environment interactions [12, 20, 38]. Reinforce-ment learning (RL) improves these decisions using task feedback [8, 17, 27]. In particular, GRPO [22] converts the outcomes of sampled trajectories into relative advantages, providing a scalable training signal without step-level annotations [15, 27, 30]. However, an outcome reward reveals whether a task was completed, with little indication of which intermediate decisions were sound or required correction. This distinction matters because trajectory success and step quality do not always agree . A successful agent may recover from invalid or mistaken actions, while a failed agent may make useful progress before a consequential error. We examine this outcome–step mismatch in annotated WebShop trajectories. Figure 1(a) shows 29.8% erroneous steps in the successful group and 42.4% correct steps in the failed group. These ob-servations motivate identifying errors independently of the final outcome. When an outcome-level advantage is shared across a trajectory, it cannot distinguish these local differences in decision quality [5, 6, 10]. To compensate for coarse outcome feedback, recent methods introduce hindsight-based supervision to pro-vide additional guidance on agent decisions. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) [42] enables such guidance: a teacher branch receives privileged context and scores actions sampled by the ordinary policy [1, 11]. Trajectory-derived skills summarize useful hindsight [3], and methods such as SEED train the policy to gen-erate this guidance from its own experience [26, 29]. However, privileged skill context can induce off-target probability shifts at steps unrelated to the skill . A trajectory-level skill may address a particular mistake or subgoal, yet conditioning the teacher on it throughout the trajectory can also change token probabilities at other steps. EOPSA [11] highlights a related issue: privileged prompts can introduce task-irrelevant discrep-ancies that dilute useful distillation signals. In our agentic setting, Figure 1(b) shows that skill-unrelated steps account for 57.9% of nonzero sampled-token gaps. Among these off-target signals, 53.9% are negative and 46.1% are positive. Distilling across all steps assigns auxiliary updates to these probability shifts even where the skill offers no correction for the current decision. Together, these observations raise a central question:  

> Key Question.

How can an agent identify critical errors independently of trajectory outcomes and apply corrective guidance only at relevant steps? We introduce SCOPE , a framework for Selective Critical-Step On-Policy Self-Distillation . A lightweight su-pervised warm-up teaches the policy to locate errors and generate corrective skills. During online RL, the policy analyzes its own trajectories to identify critical errors in both successful and failed episodes and gen-erates corresponding step-specific skills. A skill-conditioned self-teacher then provides corrective supervision exclusively at the selected steps, aligning distillation with the decisions that require correction. During training, however, we observe occasional abrupt performance collapses with unbounded OPSD up-dates, as illustrated in Figure 1(c). Large teacher–student log-probability gaps can amplify auxiliary updates and destabilize optimization. To mitigate this instability, SCOPE employs a bounded signed distillation coeffi-cient that limits extreme updates while preserving both reinforcement and suppression. Moreover, successful trajectories may still contain redundant interactions, motivating a success-relative pairwise length reward to encourage efficient task completion. Together, these designs enable stable, targeted, and efficiency-aware agentic RL. Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop across three backbones demonstrate consistent improvements. With Qwen2.5-3B, SCOPE achieves 97.7% and 85.2% success, outperforming SEED by 5.9 and 6.3 per-centage points, respectively. On unseen ALFWorld tasks, SCOPE achieves 89.6% success (+23.2 points) with 46.7% fewer interactions. Extending to Qwen3-32B, SCOPE reaches 99.2% ALFWorld success (+14.0 points over GRPO) with 65.0% fewer interactions. In summary, our contributions are fourfold: • We reveal two limitations of trajectory-level supervision in agentic RL: outcome–step mismatch, where trajectory success does not reliably reflect individual decision quality, and off-target distillation signals induced by privileged skills at unrelated steps. 2• We propose SCOPE , a selective on-policy self-distillation framework that bootstraps critical-step analysis from supervised warm-up, generates step-specific corrective skills from online trajectories, and distills their guidance exclusively at identified error steps. • We introduce a bounded signed distillation coefficient to mitigate unstable auxiliary updates while pre-serving both positive and negative corrections, together with a success-relative pairwise length reward to encourage efficient task completion. • We validate SCOPE on ALFWorld and WebShop across three backbones and further extend it to Qwen3-32B. On unseen ALFWorld tasks, SCOPE improves success by 23.2 points with 46.7% fewer interactions. With Qwen3-32B, it achieves 99.2% seen and 94.0% unseen ALFWorld success. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents 

Reinforcement learning trains LLM agents through feedback from tool use and environment interaction [14, 31, 33]. ToolRL [17], RAGEN [27], and Search-R1 [8] apply this approach to tool selection, interactive planning, and information seeking. GRPO [22] provides a common optimization backbone by comparing trajectory outcomes within a sampled group. However, trajectory-level advantages offer limited resolution over intermediate decisions in long interactions. GiGPO [6] estimates step-level advantages through hierar-chical grouping, while GraphGPO [5] leverages state-transition graphs to refine credit assignment. Rather than directly re-estimating step-level RL advantages, SCOPE complements outcome-based RL with hindsight error localization and targeted corrective distillation. 

2.2 Hindsight Skills for LLM Agents 

Experience can guide agents through verbal reflection, reusable skills, or explicit memory, as demonstrated by Reflexion [23], Voyager [25], and ExpeL [41]. Recent methods further internalize such guidance through policy training [16, 26, 28, 36]. SDAR [13] retrieves skills from an external skill bank to provide privileged teacher context, while Skill-SD [26] derives teacher-only skills from completed agent trajectories. OPID [36] constructs hierarchical episode- and step-level skills through critical-first routing, falling back to episode-level guidance at non-critical steps. SEED [29] introduces a policy-synchronized hindsight analyzer. After a supervised warm-up, the policy acts as both actor and analyzer, generating trajectory-level skills that evolve with online training. However, conditioning distillation on such skills across all steps can introduce off-target signals at unrelated deci-sions. GRSD [43] contrasts policy-generated reflections from successful and failed trajectories to construct group-level guidance for turn-level credit assignment. In contrast, SCOPE internalizes critical-step analysis, generates step-specific corrective skills, and restricts their supervision to identified errors in both successful and failed trajectories. 

2.3 On-Policy Self-Distillation 

Knowledge distillation transfers teacher guidance to a student model [7, 9]. On-policy distillation instead evaluates student-generated sequences, reducing the distribution mismatch associated with offline demonstra-tions [1, 19]. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) constructs a teacher from the same model under privileged context [11, 42]. RLSD [35] uses teacher–student discrepancies to modulate token-level update magnitudes while retaining reward-determined optimization directions. SDAR [13] employs a sigmoid gate that em-phasizes positive teacher endorsements while attenuating negative rejections, improving auxiliary training stability. AgentOPSD [28] aggregates token-level discrepancies and recursively updates belief states for turn-level credit assignment. SOD [44] adaptively weights step-level distillation according to teacher–student divergence. However, teacher–student discrepancies do not necessarily reflect task-relevant corrections. EOPSA [11] highlights how privileged prompting can introduce task-irrelevant stylistic shifts that dilute useful distillation signals. In agentic tasks, skill conditioning may similarly affect decisions unrelated to the intended correction. 3 Method 

SCOPE supplements outcome-based RL with corrective supervision at selected error steps (Figure 2). Asupervised warm-up teaches the policy to locate mistakes and generate corrective skills. During online training, the same policy analyzes its rollouts, and a skill-conditioned self-teacher scores the sampled actions at the selected steps. Bounded signed self-distillation transfers these corrections to the student, while a success-relative length reward encourages efficient task completion.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate SCOPE through six research questions: • RQ1: Overall Effectiveness. Does SCOPE outperform outcome-based RL and skill-guided baselines across tasks and backbones? (Section 4.2) • RQ2: Error Localization. What limitations arise from trajectory-level supervision, and can SCOPE reliably identify critical error steps? (Sections 4.3 and 4.7) • RQ3: Component Contributions. How do supervised initialization, selective skill guidance, and pairwise length rewards affect performance and efficiency? (Section 4.4) • RQ4: Optimization Behavior. How do training efficiency, stability, and hyperparameter choices affect SCOPE? (Sections 4.5 and 4.6) • RQ5: Unseen Generalization. Does SCOPE generalize to unseen tasks while improving interaction effi-ciency? (Section 4.8) • RQ6: Larger-Model Scalability. Can SCOPE maintain its effectiveness and interaction efficiency when extended to Qwen3-32B? (Section 4.9) 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks and metrics We evaluate SCOPE on ALFWorld and WebShop, following the evaluation pro-tocols of SDAR [13]. ALFWorld [24] tests household task completion through text-based interaction. Each validation round contains 128 episodes from the seen split, covering six task families: Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, and Pick2. We report success rates by family and the overall success rate across the 128 episodes (micro-average). WebShop [37] requires agents to search, inspect, and purchase products satisfying a user’s requirements. We evaluate on the same fixed set of 128 tasks and report two metrics: the mean normalized task score, which measures how well the purchased product meets the requirements, and the success rate, which counts fully completed tasks. All reported scores are percentages. 

Backbones and evaluation context We use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [18], and Qwen3-1.7B [34] to examine performance across model sizes and generations. Comparisons are made within each backbone. At evaluation, SCOPE acts from the ordinary interaction history without hindsight skills or analyzer calls. For the main ALFWorld and WebShop comparison, we report SCOPE’s validation metrics logged at training step 150. 4.3 Diagnosing Trajectory-Level Supervision 

To examine the limitations of trajectory-level supervision, we analyze the alignment between task outcomes, step quality, and skill-conditioned distillation signals. Figure 1(a) reveals substantial outcome–step mismatch in annotated WebShop trajectories: 29.8% of steps in successful trajectories are erroneous, while 42.4% of steps in failed trajectories are correct. This disagreement indicates that terminal outcomes alone cannot reliably identify which decisions require correction. Figure 1(b) further reveals that skill-unrelated steps account for 57.9% of nonzero sampled-token teacher– student gaps. Notably, their negative and positive gaps have similar magnitudes to those at skill-relevant steps ( −0.378 /+0 .230 versus −0.400 /+0 .249 nats). This suggests that gap magnitude alone provides limited information about the relevance of corrective guidance. Together, these findings motivate explicit error localization and selective error-step distillation, rather than applying skill-conditioned supervision broadly. 4.5 Training Dynamics 

To examine learning efficiency and optimization stability, we compare the training trajectories of SCOPE and GRPO and investigate the impact of unbounded OPSD updates. 

SCOPE reaches strong performance with fewer updates Figure 1(c) shows that SCOPE reaches 65.1% mean success on WebShop at step 60, surpassing GRPO’s final 63.0% at step 150, requiring 60.0% fewer online updates. On ALFWorld, SCOPE first exceeds GRPO’s final success rate of 76.6% at step 25, compared with step 145 for GRPO, reducing the required updates by 82.8%. At step 180, SCOPE achieves 98.4% success, compared with 76.6% at GRPO’s final checkpoint. These comparisons measure online updates after supervised initialization, demonstrating faster performance improvement under a smaller RL update budget. 

Bounded signed distillation stabilizes training Figure 1(c) further shows that Unbounded SCOPE suffers an abrupt performance collapse after initial improvement, whereas bounded SCOPE maintains stable training. Figure 3 reveals the underlying numerical instability: sharply reduced student log-probabilities produce ex-treme teacher–student gaps, amplifying raw-gap OPSD updates during minibatch optimization. The bounded signed coefficient in Equation 8 limits this gap-driven amplification while preserving both positive and nega-tive corrections. Together, these results support stable and efficient online optimization. 

4.6 Parameter Sensitivity 

To examine the sensitivity of corrective supervision and length-aware optimization, we vary the signed-gap slope α, saturation bound c, and pairwise temperature τlen individually. The remaining parameters follow the default configuration ( α = 2 .5, c = 1 , τlen = 0 .1). Figure 6 reports overall and task-wise ALFWorld success at training step 180. 

The signed-gap slope controls corrective strength Reducing α to 1 lowers overall success from 98.4% to 89.8%, with notable declines on Cool and Look. A smaller slope weakens the coefficient for moderate teacher–student gaps, potentially limiting corrective supervision. Increasing α to 5 maintains 97.7% success, suggesting that effective learning does not depend on the exact default slope. 

Overly tight saturation limits correction Reducing c to 0.1 lowers overall success to 94.5%, with Heat drop-ping to 82.4%. This suggests that early coefficient saturation can restrict useful corrective updates. Both 

c = 1 and c = 10 achieve 98.4% overall success, indicating that SCOPE performs well across different saturation bounds. 

Moderate pairwise preferences favor performance Setting τlen to 0.05 or 0.2 yields 93.8% and 96.1% success, respectively, compared with 98.4% at the default 0.1. Lower temperatures produce sharper length preferences, while higher temperatures soften pairwise differences. 4.7 Error-Localization Capability 

Since SCOPE relies on self-generated error analysis to select critical steps, reliable error localization is essential for effective corrective supervision. We therefore evaluate the localization capability of base and SFT-initialized models on WebShop and ALFWorld, alongside a SEED-style analyzer. Figure 7 reveals three key findings. First, larger models generally exhibit stronger error-step localization, with 7B outperforming 3B across most metrics. Second, SFT substantially improves both backbones, with 3B SFT even surpassing 7B Base in micro-F1, demonstrating that smaller models can acquire effective error-analysis capabilities. Third, the SEED-style analyzer exhibits markedly weaker localization than our step-specific analyzer, particularly on ALFWorld, suggesting that trajectory-level hindsight training may be less effective for fine-grained error identification. These results support supervised initialization for reliable online self-analysis. Metric definitions are provided in Appendix B.4. 

4.8 Generalization to Unseen Tasks 

SCOPE generalizes to unseen task instances To examine whether SCOPE transfers beyond the tasks en-countered during training, we evaluate its performance on the unseen ALFWorld split, covering six household task families. As shown in Figure 8, SCOPE achieves 89.6% overall success compared with 66.4% for GRPO, a gain of 23.2 percentage points. Improvements span all six families, particularly Look (+73.3 points), Pick2 (+50.0), and Cool (+32.4). These results demonstrate that SCOPE’s learned corrective behavior generalizes to unfamiliar task instances across diverse household routines. 

Generalization gains accompany greater efficiency Beyond higher task success, SCOPE also reduces average interactions from 18.86 to 10.06 turns, a 46.7% reduction relative to GRPO. This improvement indicates that SCOPE generalizes without relying on longer interaction sequences, achieving more successful completions with fewer environment interactions. Together with the length-reward ablation, these findings support the effectiveness of efficiency-aware agentic RL.

5 Conclusion 

In this work, we identify two limitations of trajectory-level supervision for LLM agents: trajectory outcomes obscure intermediate decision quality, while trajectory-level skills can introduce off-target distillation signals. To address these challenges, we propose SCOPE , which internalizes error localization and applies self-generated, step-specific corrective guidance only at selected error steps. Bounded signed distillation controls gap-driven updates, while success-relative pairwise rewards encourage efficient task completion. Experiments on ALF-14 World and WebShop across three backbones demonstrate consistent improvements over outcome-based RL and strong performance against skill-guided methods. On unseen ALFWorld tasks, SCOPE achieves 89.6% success, outperforming GRPO by 23.2 percentage points while reducing reported interactions by 46.7%. These results highlight the importance of aligning corrective supervision with the decisions that require it, without additional guidance at inference time.

B.3 Supervised Data and Hindsight Analysis 

GLM-5.2 generates the offline annotations used for SFT. Each input contains the task description, episode outcome, and the recorded sequence of observations and responses, together with action-validity feedback when available. Successful trajectories are analyzed for avoidable errors. Failed trajectories may additionally receive up to three successful action sequences from the same task as reference context. These offline references can come from separate runs of that task; online references are drawn from the current on-policy group. The annotation configuration uses temperature 0, an output limit of 8,192 tokens, and disabled thinking. Observations are retained without character-level truncation. An annotation contains a trajectory summary and at most five skill entries, each with target-step indices, an error explanation, and a short reusable corrective instruction. Entries that require the same correction can cover multiple steps. The prompt asks the annotator to judge decisions using the information available at each step and to preserve reasonable exploration. An empty skill list is returned when no clear error is supported. The data pipeline checks the JSON structure, nonempty required fields, and valid, unique target indices before export. B.4 Error-Localization Evaluation Metrics 

We evaluate error-step localization against reference annotations. For each trajectory i, let Ei and bEi denote the reference and predicted error-step sets, respectively. Predictions are extracted from the target_steps 

fields of generated skill entries. 

Step-level metrics Micro-precision and micro-recall are defined as P = TP TP+FP and R = TP TP+FN , with counts aggregated across all trajectories. Micro-F1 is 2P R P +R , while Macro-F1 averages the F1 scores computed 21 separately for each trajectory. 

Trajectory-level metrics Exact Match measures the fraction of trajectories with bEi = Ei. Parse Rate mea-sures the fraction of outputs satisfying the required JSON schema. All metrics are reported as percentages. 

B.5 Motivation Diagnostics 

Outcome–step mismatch The WebShop study in Figure 1(a) uses GLM-5.3 to identify erroneous steps in 1,400 trajectories: 536 successful and 864 failed episodes. Local decision quality is judged separately from episode outcome: an eventual success can contain avoidable mistakes, and an eventual failure can contain correct decisions. The figure reports error and correct-step proportions separately for the two outcome groups. GLM-5.2 supplies the offline SFT annotations described above; GLM-5.3 supplies these diagnostic error-step labels. 

Off-target probability shifts The skill-influence analysis scores 33,602 steps and 4,035,655 response tokens from 177 task groups. The recorded relevance labels divide steps into 14,081 related and 19,521 unrelated steps according to the applicability of the trajectory-level skill. On the sampled response tokens, we compute the signed gap ∆ = log πT (y | h, s ) − log πS (y | h). Within each outcome and relevance group, positive and negative bars are pooled-token means conditional on ∆ > 0 and ∆ < 0, respectively. Zero gaps are excluded from these conditional means. The 57.9% off-target share divides the number of nonzero-gap tokens at unrelated steps by the number of nonzero-gap tokens at all steps. The negative and positive shares of 53.9% and 46.1% use nonzero-gap tokens at unrelated steps as their denominator. Error bars show 95% task-group bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples with random seed 0. Task descrip-tions and initial observations define the grouping proxy; each resample draws complete groups rather than individual tokens. This preserves within-group dependence when estimating uncertainty in the conditional means.
