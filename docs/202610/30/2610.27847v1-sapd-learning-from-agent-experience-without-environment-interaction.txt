## ABSTRACT 

Scaling the training of Large Language Model (LLM) agents typically requires a large collection of executable environments, whose construction, maintenance, and operation constrain the expansion of training experience. Existing offline tra-jectories offer an opportunity to reuse interaction experience, yet effectively re-fining the current policy without revisiting these environments remains challeng-ing: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can lead to catastrophic forgetting, while online reinforcement learning requires real-time interaction with the environment. To address this challenge, we propose Step-Advantage Policy Distillation (SAPD), a method for learning from offline trajectories using relative step advantages. SAPD decomposes multi-turn trajectories into individual decision contexts for on-policy distillation, allowing the student to generate responses conditioned on recorded histories, and introduces relative step advantage signals to stabilize training. The entire process requires neither new environment observations nor additional multi-turn interactions. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-based QA show that SAPD improves performance across different teacher configurations, mitigates catastrophic forgetting compared with SFT, and runs at least 4.7× faster per training step than online OPD. We will release our code soon. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have enabled LLM agents to tackle increasingly complex, long-horizon tasks. Scaling agent training across these long-horizon tasks typically re-quires diverse executable environments in which models can take actions and receive feedback (Liao et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Recent models such as DeepSeek-V3.2 and Qwen3-Coder-Next further highlight this trend through large-scale construction of executable environments for agent training (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2026). However, deploying, maintaining, and oper-ating these environments introduces substantial infrastructure demands and engineering complexity, making agent training difficult to scale across heterogeneous tasks (Liao et al., 2026). Existing offline trajectories offer an opportunity to reuse interaction experience, yet effectively refin-ing the current policy without revisiting the original environments remains challenging. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) learns from these trajectories by imitating expert actions. However, when tra-jectories span heterogeneous domains, their supervision signals may conflict, causing interference between domain-specific behaviors. Sequential SFT can further lead to catastrophic forgetting, with gains on new tasks coming at the expense of previously acquired capabilities (Lu & Thinking Ma-chines Lab, 2025). These challenges limit the use of SFT for continually expanding an agent’s task coverage. In contrast, online reinforcement learning (RL) and on-policy distillation (OPD) optimize trajectories generated by the current policy and can substantially mitigate forgetting compared with SFT (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). However, their application through online multi-turn rollouts requires continued environment interaction, retaining the associated de-

These limitations raise a central question: How can we continually improve an agent’s policy using offline interaction experience without revisiting the environment, while retaining previously acquired capabilities? 

To address this challenge, we propose Step-Advantage Policy Distillation (SAPD), which refines the student policy using offline prefixes and teacher supervision. SAPD consists of two components: of-fline trajectory decomposition and context-relative step-advantage optimization. Offline trajectory decomposition converts recorded multi-turn trajectories into decision contexts, each retaining the preceding interaction history and current observation as a fixed prefix. For each prefix, the student generates a group of single-turn responses, and a fixed teacher provides token-level supervision on the generated responses. This enables SAPD to reuse offline interaction histories while optimizing the student’s own decisions, without executing new actions or obtaining additional environment ob-servations. However, offline prefix replay faces a two-sided mismatch: recorded histories may differ from those the student would encounter through its own interactions and may also be unfamiliar to the teacher. This mismatch can introduce context-dependent errors into teacher supervision, mak-ing updates based on absolute teacher–student discrepancies less reliable. To address this challenge, SAPD introduces context-relative step-advantage optimization , which aggregates token-level sig-nals into action-level scores and compares candidate responses under the same decision context. Comparing responses under the same prefix yields relative advantages that provide decision-level guidance alongside token-level supervision. We incorporate these advantages into a joint objective to stabilize policy updates, alongside token-level OPD for efficiency and rule rewards for further stability. Experiments on three publicly available agentic benchmarks show that SAPD significantly improves performance across teacher configurations, substantially mitigates catastrophic forgetting compared with SFT, and runs at least 4.7× faster per training step than online OPD in the measured configurations. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce a framework that combines offline trajectory decomposition with policy op-timization, enabling environment-free learning while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. • We propose Step-Advantage Policy Distillation (SAPD), which constructs relative step ad-vantages to provide more stable decision-level supervision under behavior-prefix shift. • Experiments on three publicly available agentic benchmarks demonstrate improved per-formance across teacher configurations, reduced forgetting compared with SFT, and lower per-step training time than online OPD. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

LLM agentic learning. LLM-based agents are increasingly trained on long-horizon tasks that re-quire sustained interaction with external environments (Yao et al., 2023). Supervised fine-tuning and behavior cloning enable learning from expert trajectories, but can suffer from gradient interfer-ence during joint training across heterogeneous tasks (Yu et al., 2020) and catastrophic forgetting during sequential training (Luo et al., 2025). Reinforcement learning methods such as GRPO opti-mize policy-generated behavior using task feedback (Shao et al., 2024), while on-policy distillation (OPD) supplies dense teacher supervision on student-generated responses (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). However, these online methods rely on environment interaction throughout training. The deployment, maintenance, and operation of het-erogeneous environments incur substantial engineering and resource costs, limiting the scalability of these methods across multiple environments (Liao et al., 2026). 

Prefix-based OPD. OPD derives supervision from teacher probabilities rather than environment rewards, potentially enabling environment-free training. Recent work has explored reducing rollout requirements of OPD through prefix-based learning (Zhao et al., 2026; Gui et al., 2026). Reasoning-Prefix OPD shows that teacher-generated prefixes can guide subsequent student reasoning (Zhang et al., 2026). BPO reuses prefixes to explore alternative actions, but still requires environment in-teraction to evaluate their rollouts (He et al., 2026). Prefix-GRPO replays teacher trajectories and optimizes student using task rewards from environment feedback, whereas ReOPD uses teacher su-pervision on student rollouts conditioned on the teacher’s prefixes without further environment inter-action (Wang et al., 2026a; Liao et al., 2026). In practice, these approaches rely on teacher-generated 2trajectories, whose collection requires deploying and maintaining the corresponding environments, which limits their ability to learn directly from arbitrary offline interaction trajectories. 

Reliable OPD under prefix mismatch. However, replaying prefixes from arbitrary offline tra-jectories faces a two-sided mismatch: recorded histories may differ from those the student would encounter through its own interactions and may also be unfamiliar to the teacher, which can substan-tially destabilize OPD training. Recent work has investigated OPD instability under prefix mismatch and explored ways to improve the reliability of teacher supervision (Zhu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). TA-OPD shows that some token-level teacher–student discrepancies are unreliable (Wang et al., 2026b). ReNIO aggregates token signals into sample-level distillation weights, which can be too coarse for credit assignment in long-horizon tasks (Lin et al., 2026). Furthermore, SOD adjusts step-level distillation weights by comparing discrepancies across steps within the same trajectory, but these comparisons span different decision contexts and may therefore be unreliable (Zhong et al., 2026). SAPD instead computes relative advantages from aggregated action-token signals under the same offline prefix, supporting more stable credit assignment.

## 4 METHOD 

SAPD decouples policy refinement from the environment execution required by the online meth-ods in Section 3. As illustrated in Figure 1, we first decompose recorded multi-turn trajectories into a pool of decision prefixes that retain the preceding actions and environment observations. For each prefix, the student generates a group of single-turn responses, which are scored by a fixed teacher without executing their actions. We combine token-level distillation with a step-level rela-tive advantage computed by comparing aggregated teacher–student signals within the group. This provides decision-level supervision alongside fine-grained token feedback. Locally computable re-wards, such as repetition penalties and format checks, further support stable training. For example, repeatedly issuing the same action can become a spurious local optimum of the train-ing surrogate. Group-relative step advantages compare responses using the same likelihood-based signal and therefore do not explicitly rule out such behavior. We therefore introduce locally com-putable rule rewards to discourage these failure modes, including penalties for repeated actions detectable from the recorded history and checks on the generated action format. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. We evaluate SAPD on three widely used long-horizon agent tasks: ALFWorld, Web-Shop, and Search-based QA. ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) is a text-based environment in which agents follow instructions to complete household goals. It comprises six task categories: Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, and Pick2. WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) is a simulated online shopping environ-ment in which agents search a product catalog, navigate product pages, and select products and options that satisfy user-specified requirements. Search-based QA follows the setup of Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025), requiring agents to interleave reasoning with search, gather relevant evidence, and produce a final answer. It includes three single-hop datasets, Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023), and four multi-hop datasets, HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultihopQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023). Following the setup of SDAR (Lu et al., 2026), we evaluate 128 episodes per evaluation round on each of ALFWorld and WebShop, and all 51,713 questions across the seven Search-based QA evaluation datasets. 

Baselines. To evaluate the effectiveness of SAPD, we compare against the following three categories of baselines. (1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (Zeng et al., 2024) learns to imitate expert actions in fixed demonstration trajectories. As a fully off-policy baseline, SFT allows us to assess SAPD’s task performance and resistance to catastrophic forgetting under sequential task training. (2) Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) collects trajectories by having the stu-dent interact with the environment and updates the policy using advantages computed from relative rewards within each rollout group. Its reliance on extensive environment interaction provides a comparison for SAPD in both task performance and training efficiency. (3) On-Policy Distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) applies token-level teacher supervision to student-generated responses conditioned on offline SFT prefixes. OPD+Rule further incorporates rule-based rewards to stabilize training. Its objective differs from SAPD only by omitting our step-advantage policy distillation, isolating the contribution of action-level guidance. 

Implementation Details. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as the student backbone and SFT- or GRPO-trained Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct models as frozen teachers (Yang et al., 2024). The student samples eight responses per decision prefix extracted from offline SFT trajectories without addi-tional environment interaction. The training framework and experimental hyperparameters follow SDAR (Lu et al., 2026). Full details of implementation are provided in Appendix B. 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

Teacher strength alone does not determine distillation quality. On WebShop, the GRPO teacher achieves a success rate of 64.8%, substantially exceeding the SFT teacher’s 59.7%. However, the student trained with the SFT teacher reaches 59.4%, compared with 53.4% for the GRPO teacher. This difference highlights the distinction between a teacher’s standalone performance and its ef-fectiveness as a supervisor. In our experimental setting, all distillation methods condition student generation on prefixes extracted from offline SFT trajectories, and teachers provide feedback on the resulting student-generated responses. These prefixes reflect SFT behavior and may differ from the histories encountered by the GRPO teacher under its own policy. Consequently, stronger perfor-mance during autonomous execution does not guarantee more useful supervision in the distillation setting. The WebShop results therefore show that teacher selection should account for compatibility with the distillation contexts, rather than standalone task performance alone. 

Long-horizon tasks benefit more from SAPD. SAPD provides larger gains on ALFWorld and WebShop than on Search-based QA, as shown in Table 1. This difference can be understood in terms of interaction horizon: ALFWorld allows up to 50 steps and WebShop up to 15, compared with four for Search-based QA. Over longer interactions, early decisions shape subsequent observations and actions, allowing errors and mismatches between teacher and student behavior to accumulate. SAPD compares candidate actions under the same prefix, reducing the impact of context mismatch on the distillation signal. The resulting relative step-level advantages provide more stable guidance for policy optimization, contributing to larger gains on longer-horizon tasks.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented SAPD, a method for refining LLM agents from recorded interaction histories without further environment interaction. SAPD decomposes multi-turn trajectories into decision prefixes and compares student-generated responses under each shared prefix to construct relative step advantages, providing decision-level guidance alongside token-level distillation. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-based QA show improved performance over OPD-based baselines across SFT and GRPO teacher configurations, reduced catastrophic forgetting relative to sequential SFT, and at least a 4.7× reduction in per-step training time compared with online OPD under the evaluated configurations. These results demonstrate the potential of reusing recorded interaction experience to expand agent capabilities while reducing reliance on executable training environments.

Three limitations are material. First, the common-offset assumption is at the aggregate action-score level. As Equation 8 shows, a shared token error btok (h) leaves the residual btok (h)( Ni −

¯N ) when action lengths vary. Thus, action-token summation does not automatically turn a shared token error into a shared sequence error. This provides an additional motivation for the action-only scoring mask: it excludes reasoning-length variation from the aggregate error. It does not guarantee lower error for every response group, since token biases and residuals can also differ between reasoning and action segments. The bound must still account for action-length variation; fixed-length or length-controlled comparisons can isolate it empirically. Second, centering does not repair incorrect relative teacher preferences. For example, a teacher that reverses the ranking of two responses also reverses their centered advantages. Nor does centering alter qB: histories absent from the offline pool remain unavailable, and familiarity to either policy is not guaranteed. Offline replay can introduce an additional mismatch relative to online OPD, but need not be more out of distribution for every dataset or teacher. Third, the guarantees apply to the step branch, not automatically to the combined objective. Token-level OPD remains sensitive to its own residual errors, and the weights in Equation 13 determine how the branches interact. Baseline terms can also cancel in expectation in conventional score-function estimators, so the projection analysis alone does not establish a universally less biased or lower-variance update than original token-level OPD. The analysis motivates testing three separate predictions: exact invariance of the step branch to in-jected groupwise score offsets; sensitivity to response-dependent perturbations and teacher ranking errors; and the effect of standard-deviation normalization under weak within-group score differ-ences. These controls should be complemented by comparisons across trajectory sources at fixed teacher and student checkpoints, with matched local rewards and action-length statistics. Such ex-periments are needed to establish whether the structured-error regime explains practical tolerance to unfamiliar prefixes.

C.2 DISTILLATION WITH TEACHER -G ENERATED TRAJECTORIES

## D ABLATION ON STEP -A DVANTAGE SCORING 

A generated response contains both reasoning text and an executable action, raising the question of whether step advantages should be computed from action tokens alone or from all response tokens. 18 We compare these two scoring scopes using offline SFT trajectories under both SFT and GRPO teachers. Both variants apply policy updates to the complete response; only the tokens used to compute step scores differ. As shown in Table 7, action-only scoring performs better on ALFWorld and WebShop under both teacher configurations. It improves ALFWorld success from 60.8% to 69.3% with the GRPO teacher and WebShop success from 50.0% to 59.4% with the SFT teacher. On Search-based QA, the differences are small. These results support action-focused scoring and suggest that excluding thinking tokens may provide more reliable step-level guidance when prefix mismatch makes teacher supervision less reliable. 19
