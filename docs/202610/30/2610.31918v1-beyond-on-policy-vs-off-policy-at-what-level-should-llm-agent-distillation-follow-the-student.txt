ABSTRACT 

Distilling interactive LLM agents requires choosing between teacher-generated demonstrations and student-generated experience, corresponding broadly to off-policy and on-policy distillation. While both have proven effective, we argue that this distinction is inherently hierarchical: the policy that induces an inter-action history need not be the same as the policy that generates the token prefix of the current action. We introduce a hierarchical agent distillation framework that formalizes these two levels of on-policiness and enables explicit control over their pairing, mixture proportions, and training schedule. We study this problem through self-distillation, where a frozen teacher has access to task guidance un-available to the student, and conduct a three-stage controlled study across three models and four interactive environments. We find that student-induced histo-ries paired with teacher-generated action prefixes generally provide the strongest standalone training signal, whereas following the student at both levels gener-ally performs worst. Nevertheless, fully on-policy data becomes beneficial when incorporated into a mixture with other configurations. Moreover, mixtures with identical teacher/student proportions at each level can yield substantially differ-ent performance, showing that how the two levels are paired matters beyond the overall amount of on-policy data. Finally, front-loading fully teacher-generated contexts and delaying fully on-policy contexts improves over a static mixture. These findings provide practical principles for deciding where, how much, and when agent distillation should follow the student. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Knowledge distillation (KD) (Hinton et al., 2015) is widely used to post-train large language model (LLM) agents (Yao et al., 2022b), transferring task-solving behavior from a teacher to a student (Zeng et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2026). In privileged-information (PI) distillation (Penaloza et al., 2026; Alakuijala et al., 2026), the teacher receives task guidance unavailable to the student, and distillation trains the student to reproduce the resulting behavior without that guidance at de-ployment. A central design choice is which policy generates the contexts used for distillation. Off-policy approaches train on teacher-generated trajectories (Kang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b), provid-ing coherent demonstrations but limited exposure to states encountered by the student. On-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu & Lab, 2025) instead obtains teacher supervision on student-generated experience, reducing training–deployment mismatch. However, student errors can produce histories or partial actions from which even the teacher has difficulty providing useful continuations (Fu et al., 2026). Both approaches can be effective in agent training (Alakuijala et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026), raising a basic question: should agent distillation be on-policy? 

We argue that the trajectory-level notion of on-policiness commonly used to characterize distillation methods is incomplete for interactive agents. An agent’s training context contains two nested levels. At the history level , preceding interactions determine the context and environment state before the 1arXiv:2610.31918v1 [cs.AI] 22 Oct 2026 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 current action. At the action-prefix level , previously generated tokens within the current action de-termine the context for next-token prediction. Either level can independently come from the teacher or the student. For example, a teacher can generate an action from a student-induced history, while a student can generate an action from a teacher-induced history. The relevant question is therefore not only whether training is on-policy, but at which level and to what extent should distillation follow the student? 

We introduce hierarchical agent distillation , which independently specifies the history source and action-prefix source. Choosing teacher or student at each level yields four configurations, T T , ST ,

T S , and SS , under a common distillation objective. This formulation separates three design choices: 

where the student is followed, how much each source configuration contributes to training, and when 

different configurations are emphasized during learning. We study these choices through a controlled three-stage investigation. Stage 1 isolates the effects of history and action-prefix sources and their interaction. Stage 2 examines how the four configura-tions should be paired and mixed under a fixed training budget. Stage 3 studies how their allocation should change over training. We use self-distillation, initializing teacher and student from the same checkpoint while providing task guidance only to the frozen teacher, thereby controlling for archi-tecture, model size, and initialization. Across three backbones and four interactive environments, we obtain the following findings: • The value of on-policy training depends on the level. Student-induced histories paired with teacher-generated action prefixes ( ST ) generally provide the strongest standalone performance, while fully on-policy training ( SS ) performs worst across the tested set-tings. Student histories expose the teacher to more opportunities for corrective supervision, whereas student-generated prefixes can constrain the usefulness of teacher continuations. • Pairing matters beyond the amount of on-policy data. Mixtures with identical propor-tions of student-generated histories and action prefixes can perform differently depending on how the two sources are paired. The strongest tested mixtures emphasize hybrid config-urations while retaining contributions from both fully teacher-generated and fully on-policy contexts. In particular, a modest allocation to SS can improve mixed training despite its weak standalone performance. • The timing of data exposure matters. Front-loading T T accelerates early learning, whereas delaying SS improves later and final validation performance. Combining both schedules outperforms a static allocation throughout training and achieves the strongest final validation performance among the schedules tested. 

2 HIERARCHICAL AGENT DISTILLATION 

2.1 PRELIMINARIES 

An interactive LLM agent alternates between generating actions and receiving environment feed-back. Let h denote the interaction history before the current action, including the task instruction and preceding actions and observations. The action is generated autoregressively, and u denotes a token prefix within it. The student predicts the next token according to πθ (· | h, u ). The teacher additionally receives privileged task guidance g and predicts πT (· | h, u, g ). Distillation trains the student to match this informed teacher while operating without g at deployment. The contexts (h, u ) used for distillation may be generated by either policy. Teacher-trajectory con-text distillation uses teacher-generated histories and action prefixes (Alakuijala et al., 2026), whereas 

fully on-policy distillation uses student-generated histories and prefixes and queries the teacher for supervision on these contexts (Agarwal et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). We study self-distillation, initializing teacher and student from the same checkpoint and keeping the teacher frozen. The fully on-policy case therefore corresponds to on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026). 2.2 HIERARCHICAL AGENT DISTILLATION FRAMEWORK

3 SETUP OF CONTROLLED STUDY 

3.1 MODELS AND BENCHMARKS 

We use Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B (Yang et al., 2025), and Ministral-3-14B-Instruct-2512 (Liu et al., 2026a), abbreviated as Ministral-3. For each backbone, teacher and student are initialized from the same checkpoint; the teacher remains frozen throughout training. We disable thinking mode for Qwen3. We evaluate on ToolQA (Zhuang et al., 2023), OfficeBench (Wang et al., 2024), ALFWorld (Shrid-har et al., 2021), and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a). Following Alakuijala et al. (2026), ToolQA and OfficeBench use train/validation/test splits of 646/107/323 and 191/9/86 tasks. For ALFWorld, we stratify by task type to sample 3,000 training and 200 validation tasks from the original training pool and retain the original evaluation split. For WebShop, we sample 3,000 training and 200 validation tasks and use the full 500-task test set. Further details are in Appendix A.1.2. 

Privileged information construction. The teacher receives task guidance describing general so-lution strategies, tool-use tips, and common failure modes. For ToolQA and OfficeBench, we ini-tialize guidance from Alakuijala et al. (2026) and refine it with Qwen3-32B using failed guidance-conditioned rollouts. For ALFWorld, we manually draft guidance per task type from failed student trajectories, use it to obtain successful Qwen3-32B trajectories, and let GPT-5.4 refine the guid-ance from both successes and failures. For WebShop, we manually draft guidance from 50 failed trajectories, organize it by decision stage and constraint pattern, and refine it with GPT-5.4 using corresponding successful Qwen3-32B trajectories. Examples are provided in Appendix A.1.1. 3.2 TRAINING SETUP 

Agent architecture. Agents follow a ReAct-style interaction format (Yao et al., 2022b): at each step, the model generates a reasoning segment followed by an operation conditioned on the inter-action history. Operations invoke tools, execute Python code, or issue environment-specific com-mands. 

Data collection and refresh. Before training, we collect one trajectory from the initial student and cache three trajectories from the frozen teacher for each training and validation task. Student trajectories provide SS examples; ST retains their histories but regenerates the current action with the teacher. A randomly selected cached teacher trajectory provides T T examples and the histories for T S , whose current actions are generated by the student. After each training round, we refresh all student-dependent data using the latest student: we regenerate student trajectories for SS , teacher continuations at those histories for ST , and student continuations at cached teacher histories for T S .

T T continues to sample from the three cached teacher trajectories. 

Training exercises. Each exercise consists of an interaction history and one current action. We uniformly sample eight action positions per source trajectory, with replacement when necessary, so each task contributes the same number of exercises. 4 STAGE 1: S EPARATING THE TWO LEVELS OF ON-P OLICINESS 

To isolate the effects of history- and action-prefix-level on-policiness, we train one student per con-figuration, matching initialization, training tasks, and the number of exercises per task within each backbone–benchmark setting. 4.1 MAIN RESULTS 

Table 1 reports results with forward KL. All four configurations substantially improve over the initial student, but their relative performance reveals a consistent distinction between the two levels. 

The effect of student histories depends on the action-prefix source. Replacing teacher his-tories with student histories generally improves success rate when prefixes are teacher-generated (T T → ST ), but consistently hurts when prefixes are student-generated ( T S → SS ). Likewise, teacher prefixes provide little advantage on teacher histories ( T T vs. T S : at most 1.9 points), but substantially outperform student prefixes on student histories ( ST vs. SS : 3.6–14.4 points). Thus, student-induced histories are most effective when paired with teacher-generated actions. 

ST performs best overall, while SS is weakest standalone. ST achieves the highest success rate in 10 of 12 settings, the highest pass@3 in all 12, and the highest WebShop Task Score for all three backbones. The two exceptions are ToolQA, where T T exceeds ST by 0.6 points for Qwen3-8B and Ministral-3, although ST still attains higher pass@3. In contrast, SS has the lowest success rate and pass@3 among distilled variants in every setting. Its average success-rate deficit relative to

We next examine why the source choices interact and whether configurations that are weaker stan-dalone nevertheless learn complementary behaviors. Annotation protocols are described in Ap-pendix A.1.4. 4.2.1 SUPERVISION DURING DATA COLLECTION 

Student histories provide richer corrective supervision ( T T vs. ST ). For each benchmark, we sample 100 training tasks and two exercises per task. We measure Unresolved , the fraction of histories containing an uncorrected earlier error; Identified , the fraction of unresolved cases where the current action recognizes the error; and Addressed , the fraction of identified cases receiving a resolution or appropriate corrective step. Unresolved errors occur in 82.7–85.0% of ST exercises but only 14.0–19.1% of T T exercises (Table 6). Although identification is lower under ST , identified errors are addressed at comparable rates, giving ST substantially more opportunities for corrective supervision. Across training rounds, unresolved errors decrease while the teacher identifies a larger fraction of those remaining (Figure 5). 

Student prefixes constrain teacher continuations ( ST vs. SS ). We select 100 student-history exercises with unresolved errors per benchmark and compare a fully teacher-generated action with teacher continuations conditioned on the student’s reasoning, or on its reasoning plus half of its op-eration. We measure continuation Executability and Appropriateness . Both decline as more student-generated prefix is retained (Table 8); with half the operation retained, appropriateness drops from 80.2% to 56.2% for Qwen3-8B and from 83.8% to 60.2% for Qwen3-14B. Thus, student prefixes can constrain the teacher into less useful continuations even at the same history. 

History sources change where teacher and student disagree ( T S vs. SS ). At rounds 1, 3, 5, and 8, we sample 100 exercises per configuration containing an explicit error without subsequent self-correction (see details in Appendix A.1.4). We compute mean per-token KL before, within, and after the first annotated error span, then normalize the three regional means by their sum. Under 

T S , disagreement concentrates on the error span, whereas SS assigns relatively more disagreement before the error (Figure 8). Teacher histories therefore produce more error-localized disagreement, while student histories distribute disagreement more broadly across the action. 4.2.2 BEHAVIOR OF TRAINED STUDENTS 

Configurations learn different behavioral strengths. For 80 test tasks per benchmark, we an-notate one trajectory per configuration using five nonexclusive error categories: repeated failure, premature completion, rule inconsistency, operational mistakes despite correct plans, and high-level planning mistakes. SS exhibits fewer repeated failures and planning mistakes, whereas T T follows explicit rules more reliably (Table 7). 

ST is strongest at recovering from student errors. For Qwen3-14B, we resume all four trained policies from the same 100 erroneous initial-model histories per benchmark on ToolQA, ALFWorld, and WebShop. ST achieves the highest rates of both error correction and eventual task success (Figure 7). Although T T and T S outperform SS from task starts, their eventual success from erroneous histories is similar to SS . Their standard-evaluation advantage therefore does not translate into stronger recovery. ST uniquely combines exposure to student mistakes with teacher-generated corrective actions. 

Configurations also differ in learning dynamics. For Qwen3-14B on ToolQA, ALFWorld, and WebShop, T T leads during rounds 1–3, ST overtakes it by round 5 and finishes highest, while SS 

improves more gradually and continues gaining later in training (Figure 6). 6 STAGE 3: S CHEDULING THE TRAINING MIXTURE 

Stage 2 shows that mixture composition matters, but should the same allocation be maintained throughout learning? The dynamics analysis in Stage 1 suggest otherwise: T T supports rapid initial progress, whereas SS improves more slowly and continues gaining later. We therefore investigate whether emphasizing these configurations at different stages improves mixed training. We use the independent mixture, (0 .25 , 0.25 , 0.25 , 0.25) , as a balanced reference and compare its static allocation with three schedules over 8 training rounds. TT-first places approximately 80% of the total T T allocation in rounds 1–4 and the remaining 20% in rounds 5–8. SS-later reverses this split for SS , allocating 20% early and 80% late. TT-first + SS-later combines both schedules. Each targeted configuration retains its total allocation across training. We measure validation success rate after every round on ToolQA, ALFWorld, and WebShop (Figure 4). 

T T -first accelerates initial learning on all three benchmarks: by round 4, its success rate exceeds the static baseline by 8.7–13.2 percentage points. Its final advantage is smaller, at 0.7–2.0 points. In contrast, SS -later initially trails the static baseline on ToolQA and WebShop and closely tracks it on ALFWorld, but overtakes it during the second half of training and finishes 2.0–2.9 points higher. The two schedules therefore offer distinct benefits: front-loading T T improves early progress, while delaying SS yields a larger improvement in final performance. The combined schedule outperforms the static baseline at all evaluated training rounds and achieves the highest final validation success rate among the tested schedules on all three benchmarks. Al-though T T -first alone often leads early, the combined schedule ultimately surpasses it. These results suggest complementary temporal roles: teacher-generated contexts support rapid initial learning, while greater exposure to fully on-policy contexts is more beneficial later. They favor front-loading 

T T and delaying SS within a mixture, rather than maintaining a uniform allocation throughout training. 

Conclusions across the three stages. Across the three stages, effective on-policiness depends on 

where , how much , and when student-generated context is used. Student histories paired with teacher actions provide the strongest standalone performance and recovery, while mixed training benefits from retaining configurations that are weaker in isolation. Scheduling T T earlier and SS later further improves over a static mixture. Thus, effective agent distillation depends not on maximizing on-policy data, but on how it is sourced, combined, and scheduled.

On-policy distillation and imitation learning. On-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) mitigates training–deployment mismatch by obtaining teacher supervision on student-generated sequences, and has become increasingly common in LLM post-training (Lu & Lab, 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). For multi-turn agents, however, student-generated interactions may reach states for which the teacher cannot provide reliable supervision. Concurrent work, ReOPD (Liao et al., 2026), addresses this issue by generating student actions from replayed teacher histories, enabling teacher supervision without requiring new environment interactions during student training. Related challenges arise in imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011), where expert supervision is collected on learner-visited states to mitigate sequential distribution shift. Our framework distinguishes history-level and action-prefix-level on-policiness as separate but coupled design choices, thereby unifying teacher-generated, student-generated, and hybrid contexts under a common objective. This for-mulation enables controlled analysis of their individual effects, their interactions in mixed training regimes, and their allocation over the course of training. 

8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced hierarchical agent distillation framework that formalizes on-policiness at the history and action-prefix levels. Across three backbones and four interactive environments, student-induced histories paired with teacher-generated actions generally provide the strongest standalone perfor-mance. Mixture comparisons show that pairing matters even when marginal on-policiness is fixed, and that configurations weaker in isolation can improve mixed training. Scheduling experiments further show that emphasizing fully teacher-generated contexts early and fully on-policy contexts later improves validation performance over a static uniform mixture. Together, these findings move agent distillation beyond a binary on-policy/off-policy choice: effective training depends on where the student is followed, how teacher and student contexts are paired, and when each configuration is emphasized. 

Limitation. Our experiments focus on privileged-information-based self-distillation with frozen teachers initialized from the same checkpoints as the students. Generalization to substantially larger models remains to be established. Also, the mixture study identifies composition principles through targeted comparisons rather than a universally optimal allocation. 9Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 AI USE STATEMENT

Annotating issue-specific agent behavior. After selecting exercises satisfying the prerequisite of the corresponding analysis, we perform more targeted annotations of the agent’s behavior. Depend-ing on the diagnostic, we use the following procedures: • Issue recognition and corrective behavior. We ask Qwen3-235B-A22B to determine whether the reasoning segment of the current action explicitly recognizes an issue present in the preceding history. If an issue is recognized, the annotator further determines whether the action (i) resolves the issue or (ii) takes an appropriate corrective step toward resolving it. We additionally provide the task-specific privileged information used in our distillation framework as reference information to help the annotator interpret the task requirements and the intended solution procedure. Cases labeled Uncertain are reassessed by GPT-5.4. • Quality of the proposed correction. For annotations requiring a judgment about the qual-ity of an action—for example, whether a proposed correction is executable and appropriate, or whether the action constitutes a valid self-correction—we use a solution-first annotation procedure. Before evaluating the agent’s action, the annotator is first asked to indepen-dently derive an appropriate next step given the task history and available task information. It then evaluates the agent’s proposed action relative to this independently derived solution. We found this decomposition to produce more reliable judgments than directly asking the annotator to assess action quality. A.2 EXPERIMENT RESULTS
