# Abstract 

Agent harnesses have become a central component of modern agentic systems, shaping how models use tools, manage context, and coordinate long-horizon interaction. Realizing the benefits of these harnesses depends on how effectively the underlying model can make use of them, motivating reinforcement learning directly through the harness. While recent efforts have explored harness diversification to reduce specialization to particular execution settings, how to systematically construct and organize harness diversity for cross-harness generalization remains insufficiently understood. This work studies Harness Scaling as a distinct scaling dimension for agentic RL, focusing on structured variation in how policies execute and interact with the same underlying task and environment. Concretely, we construct a factorized harness space, select a compact training subset with balanced factorial coverage, and organize cross-harness training through locality-aware task assignment. Across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling consistently improves generalization to unseen harness combinations. Moreover, policies trained through structured white-box harness variation transfer effectively to unseen external black-box harnesses, outperforming policies trained through a single complex black-box harness. These results establish harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL and highlight the importance of how this diversity is organized for cross-harness generalization. 

## 1. Introduction 

Agent harnesses are playing an increasingly central role in modern agentic systems [32]. By supporting sustained interaction, tool use, state maintenance, and recovery from failures [4], harnesses have sub-stantially improved agent performance on complex, long-horizon tasks [17, 33, 39]. Yet the benefits of a capable harness depend not only on its design, but also on whether the underlying policy can use it effec-tively [15]. This naturally motivates optimizing the policy directly through the harness with reinforcement learning [37, 40], which strengthens its ability to exploit the available interaction mechanisms during task solving. However, optimizing a policy through a single fixed harness may encourage specialization to a particular execution configuration and weaken transfer across harnesses [8, 35]. Recent efforts have explored harness diversification through randomization and training across heterogeneous harnesses [10, 28]. Yet how to systematically construct and organize harness diversity for generalization beyond the training configurations remains insufficiently understood. We therefore study Harness Scaling as a distinct scaling dimension for agentic RL. Unlike task or environment scaling [3, 7, 30, 36], which expands what agents solve or where they operate, Harness Scaling varies how policies execute and interact with the same underlying task and environment, promoting generalization across execution configurations. 

1arXiv:2610.29458v2 [cs.LG] 25 Oct 2026 A key challenge in Harness Scaling is how to model harness variation and select representative configu-rations under a fixed training budget. Given the modular nature of agent harnesses [8, 24], we factorize harness variation along five components: system prompt, tool interface, observation, context management, and workflow. Each component contains multiple realizations, whose combinations define a factorial space of complete harness configurations. As variation along these components increases, the space expands rapidly, while exhaustive training can introduce substantial redundancy under a fixed budget. We therefore preserve low-order factorial structure in a compact training set, balancing individual component realizations and their pairwise combinations through an orthogonal design [9, 22]. During reinforcement learning, the same task is paired with structurally nearby harness configurations, providing controlled local variation within each task while maintaining broad coverage across the training space. To demonstrate the effectiveness of Harness Scaling, we evaluate cross-harness generalization across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms. By training across diverse harness configurations, Harness Scaling reduces specialization to any single harness and encourages more generalizable agent behavior. Empirically, it consistently outperforms single-harness RL on unseen white-box configurations. More importantly, policies trained through structured white-box harness variation transfer effectively to complex external black-box harnesses [1, 19], outperforming single-harness black-box training. These results establish harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL, while showing that effective scaling depends critically on how this diversity is organized during training. In summary, our contributions are threefold: • We systematically study Harness Scaling as a distinct scaling dimension of agentic RL, complemen-tary to task and environment scaling, and establish a controlled setting to isolate harness variation and evaluate policy generalization beyond the training configurations. • We develop a structured framework for Harness Scaling that factorizes harness variation, selects a compact training set with balanced factorial coverage, and organizes cross-harness training by assigning structurally nearby configurations to the same task across the training space. • We demonstrate across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms that Harness Scaling substan-tially improves generalization to unseen white-box and external black-box harnesses, establishing both the value of scaling across harnesses and the importance of organizing this scaling structurally. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Agentic Reinforcement Learning 

Reinforcement learning has emerged as an important paradigm for improving the capabilities of LLMs [25, 41]. Recent work has extended RL from single-turn reasoning to increasingly complex agentic settings, including tool use [2, 27], web navigation [21], software engineering [29], and professional workflows [34]. As agentic RL moves toward broader capability, recent efforts have increasingly scaled training along the 

task and environment dimensions. Task scaling [3, 6, 31] broadens the distribution of problems used for policy optimization, while environment scaling [7, 30, 36] expands the range of external settings in which agents interact and receive feedback. These directions expand what agents learn to solve and where they learn to operate, while often retaining a fixed execution harness. In contrast, we study harness diversity as an additional scaling dimension for agentic RL, focusing on policy learning and generalization across execution configurations. 

2.2. Harness Design and Optimization 

The design of an agent harness shapes how language models use tools, manage context, and perform long-horizon tasks [15, 17, 39]. Early systems relied on manually designed harnesses, while later work explored automated improvement through search, editing, and evolution [12, 14, 24]. Another line optimizes model policies through agent harnesses, including black-box reinforcement learning [8, 16, 18, 37]. ## 3. Method 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

To study harness scaling systematically, we first formalize the space of execution configurations and the generalization problem defined over it. We construct a factorized harness space, define a structural notion of proximity among its configurations, and formulate the generalization objectives that motivate the structured harness selection and multi-harness training procedures shown as in Figure 1. 

3.1.1. Factorized Harness Space 

A harness serves as the execution layer through which a policy interacts with its environment. These components govern the instructions provided to the policy, tool access, environment feedback, interaction history, and execution procedure, collectively shaping how the policy perceives, acts, and maintains state throughout task execution. This factorization provides an operational decomposition of the harness, while allowing the effects of different components to interact. 3.1.2. Generalization Objective 

The central objective of Harness Scaling is to learn policies that remain effective beyond the harness configurations observed during training. We consider two complementary forms of cross-harness general-ization: broad combinatorial generalization within the constructed harness space and transfer to complex real-world black-box harnesses beyond it. 

In-Space Combinatorial Generalization. Let Htr ⊂ H denote the harness configurations used for training. We evaluate on held-out configurations in H \ H tr , whose complete combinations are unseen during training. This setting provides broad coverage of unseen combinations within the controlled factorized space. 

Out-of-Space Generalization. We further evaluate transfer to complex real-world black-box harnesses whose execution configurations are not represented in H . This setting examines whether training over structured white-box harness variation can transfer to external execution systems beyond the constructed design space. 

3.2. Harness Scaling with Structured Selection 

As the factorized harness space grows combinatorially with its component dimensions, exhaustive training can introduce substantial redundancy. We therefore construct a compact training set that preserves broad factorial coverage under a fixed optimization budget. 

3.2.1. Factorial Coverage 

The harness space in Eq. 2 can be viewed as a factorial design, where each harness component corresponds to a factor and its realizations correspond to different levels [9, 22]. To construct a compact subset without losing basic coverage of this combinatorial space, we preserve its low-order factorial structure: the representation of individual component realizations and their pairwise combinations. These correspond to the first- and second-order projections of the full factorial space, respectively. 

Component Balance. We first require the realizations of each component to be represented evenly across the selected configurations. This ensures coverage along every component dimension without overrepresenting particular realizations. 

Pairwise Combination Balance. Component balance alone does not control how realizations from dif-ferent components are combined. We therefore additionally balance all pairwise combinations. 3.3. Reinforcement Learning across Harnesses 

Training each task under multiple harnesses exposes the policy to different execution configurations for the same underlying problem, encouraging the learned behavior to generalize beyond a single harness. We realize this over the selected harness set Htr by pairing each task with a small group of structurally related configurations. The assignment is designed to combine controlled variation within each task with broad coverage across the training harness space, and the resulting task–harness instances form the basis for rollout collection and policy optimization.

## 5. Analysis 

We further investigate behavioral patterns associated with cross-harness generalization. Specifically, we examine whether Harness Scaling is associated with stronger execution capabilities and more robust performance as the execution configuration changes. For each model scale and approach, we analyze 200 complete trajectories per evaluated harness, generated from the same set of ClawGym-Bench tasks to enable direct comparisons across approaches and harnesses. ## 6. Conclusion 

In this work, we systematically study Harness Scaling as a distinct scaling dimension for agentic reinforce-ment learning, focusing on how harness diversity can be organized to improve cross-harness generalization. We formalize harness diversity as a structured configuration space and develop a framework that integrates structured harness selection with cross-harness policy training. Across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling substantially improves generalization to unseen white-box configurations and transfers effectively to unseen external black-box harnesses. Our results further show that the benefits of scaling depend on how harness diversity is selected and incorporated into training, rather than on diversity alone. More broadly, these findings expand the scope of agentic RL scaling beyond what agents solve and where they operate to how they interact and execute, positioning harness variation as a complementary dimension for improving generalization across diverse execution systems. 10 References

## A. Harness Component Variants 

We instantiate the factorized harness space through a model-driven transformation procedure. Starting from the base harness, Opus-4.7 generates alternative realizations for each of the five components: system prompt ( 𝑆 ), tool interface ( 𝑇 ), observation ( 𝑂 ), context management ( 𝐶 ), and workflow ( 𝑊 ). Each transformation targets a single component and introduces a specified form of variation while preserving the underlying task, environment, and functionality of the remaining harness components. The transformation procedure explicitly constrains non-target components to remain unchanged in task semantics, environment behavior, and functionality, so that each resulting realization primarily reflects variation along the designated component dimension. This procedure yields three realizations per component and 35 = 243 complete harness configurations. Table 5 summarizes the resulting variants. 

System Prompt ( 𝑆 ). This dimension varies how the same operational requirements are presented to the policy. The transformed realizations range from direct instructions to product-oriented and narrative formulations while preserving the underlying execution requirements. 

Tool Interface ( 𝑇 ). The base interface provides five functional tool families: file reading/writing/editing, Bash execution, code interpretation, search, and web fetching. The transformation varies how these tools are exposed while preserving their underlying functionality. Semantic aliases retain partial cues to tool 15 usage, whereas opaque identifiers substantially reduce such cues; distractor tools further increase interface complexity. 

Observation ( 𝑂 ). This dimension varies the representation of tool feedback. Simulated infrastructure or structured execution metadata is introduced around the original output, while the task-relevant tool result remains unchanged. 

Context Management ( 𝐶 ). This dimension varies how interaction history remains accessible during long-horizon execution, ranging from full retention to in-context summarization and more aggressive externalized compaction. Further details on how compaction participates in policy training are provided in Appendix C. 

Workflow ( 𝑊 ). This dimension varies the procedural structure of task execution, ranging from free-form interaction to explicit planning and periodic progress verification while preserving the underlying task and available actions.

## C. Implementation Details 

C.1. Training Training Configuration. We train all models with veRL [26] using asynchronous vLLM [11] rollouts on NVIDIA H20 GPUs, using 16 GPUs for Qwen3-4B-Instruct and 32 GPUs for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct. Table 7 summarizes the main training hyperparameters. For GRPO, each update uses 16 tasks with eight rollouts per task, while PPO uses 128 tasks with one rollout per task. We use an actor learning rate of 

1 × 10 −6, asymmetric clipping ratios of 0.2 and 0.28, and no KL or entropy regularization. Dynamic batching is enabled throughout training. For PPO, we use GAE with 𝛾 = 𝜆 = 1; the critic masks observation tokens from value regression, and its value head is initialized to zero. 

Training with Context Compaction. Context compaction can split a single rollout into multiple training segments. All segments originating from the same rollout share the same rollout-level reward and participate in policy optimization, causing the number of training segments in a batch to vary dynamically. Segment expansion does not alter the rollout-level grouping used by GRPO: for a group of eight original rollouts, reward normalization is always computed over the eight rollout rewards, regardless of how many segments each rollout produces. Segments inherit the group identity and normalized advantage of their parent rollout and do not introduce additional entries when computing the group mean or standard deviation. Policy loss is aggregated with token-level mean reduction over all valid policy-generated tokens in the batch. Each valid token therefore contributes once to the loss regardless of how its parent rollout is segmented, so splitting a rollout into multiple segments does not introduce additional sample-level weight. This preserves both the rollout-level reward semantics and consistent token-level weighting while allowing all valid post-compaction segments to participate in training. 

Black-Box Baselines. For the black-box baselines, we use OpenClaw v2026.4.5 as the training harness and reserve Claude Code v2.1.205 as an unseen external harness for evaluation. ## D. Additional Analysis Details 

D.1. Behavioral Metrics 

We use six trajectory-level behavioral metrics to characterize harness-related capabilities beyond task-level performance. Instruction Following measures whether the policy correctly follows the system instructions and execution constraints specified by the harness. Interface Handling measures whether the policy correctly interprets and uses the available tool interface, particularly when tool names or schemas differ from the base harness. Observation Reading measures whether the policy extracts task-relevant information from tool feedback despite additional formatting or distracting metadata. State Tracking 

measures whether the policy maintains an accurate understanding of task progress, intermediate results, and the evolving workspace state throughout long-horizon execution. Workflow Compliance measures whether the policy follows procedural requirements imposed by the harness, such as explicit planning or periodic verification. Error Recovery measures whether the policy recognizes execution failures, adapts its strategy, and continues toward task completion rather than repeating or propagating the same error. 

D.2. Metric Evaluation 

We evaluate the six capabilities from complete agent trajectories using GPT-5.4 as the trajectory judge. For each model scale and each of the 18 held-out in-space harnesses, we collect 200 complete trajectories for both Single-Harness RL and Harness Scaling on the same 200 ClawGym-Bench tasks. For each trajectory, the judge is provided with the task instruction, harness specification, and complete interaction history, including policy actions, tool calls, and tool observations. Each capability is scored independently on a five-point scale. A score of 1 indicates that the corresponding capability is largely absent or repeatedly violated; 2 indicates substantial deficiencies; 3 indicates partial or inconsistent satisfaction; 4 indicates generally correct behavior with minor deviations; and 5 indicates consistent and effective satisfaction of the criterion throughout the trajectory. The judge assigns each dimension separately rather than producing a single overall trajectory score. We use the same judging prompt, rubric, and judge model across both approaches and all harness settings. For each behavioral dimension, we first average scores over the 200 trajectories within each held-out harness, and then average equally across all 18 harnesses.
