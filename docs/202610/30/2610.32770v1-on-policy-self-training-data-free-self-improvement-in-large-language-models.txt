## ABSTRACT 

On-policy self-distillation enables post-training foundation models without rely-ing on stronger external teachers. However, existing approaches rely on ground-truth question-answer pairs, human-curated problem sets, or other supervised sig-nals. In this work, we reformulate on-policy self-distillation as a self-training framework, driven by self-generated question-answer pairs and privileged reason-ing demonstrations. We push supervision to the limit: guided solely by coarse, high-level topic descriptions, the model synthesizes its own curriculum of queries and privileged reasoning traces without access to any human-curated prompts or answers. We find that self-distillation on fully synthetic privileged information en-ables a model to improve across multiple domains while retaining its capabilities on domains it is not self-trained on. We demonstrate the generality of this frame-work across text and multimodal domains, achieving consistent self-improvement on standard language benchmarks and Visual Question Answering. Our findings demonstrate that foundation models possess rich latent capabilities that can be amplified without external task supervision or curated problem distributions. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training is a primary driver of capability gains in large language models (Ouyang et al., 2022), and its paradigms differ along two axes: whether training trajectories come from the model itself, and how dense the learning signal is. Supervised finetuning (SFT) imitates curated demonstrations, providing dense token-level targets on teacher-forced trajectories the model never visits at inference, which induces exposure bias (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024) and erodes existing capabili-ties (Shenfeld et al., 2026b). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains on the model’s own rollouts (Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024), avoiding this mismatch but compressing supervision into a single scalar per trajectory. On-policy distillation (OPD) combines the two: the student samples its own trajectories, and a teacher supplies dense next-token targets at the states it visits (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) removes the need for a stronger teacher by constructing teacher and student from the same parameters under asym-metric conditioning (Zhao et al., 2026a; Shenfeld et al., 2026a). Across this progression, external supervision is relocated rather than removed: SFT requires reference solutions, RLVR gold answers, OPD a stronger teacher, and OPSD a privileged context, typically the ground-truth solution. Curating this required supervision at scale is costly: process-level annotation of a single mathemat-ics corpus required about 800,000 human step labels (Lightman et al., 2024), and open instruction datasets rest on crowdsourcing efforts spanning over 13,000 volunteers (Köpf et al., 2023). This cost has motivated a shift toward synthetic supervision, in which the model generates its own instructions (Wang et al., 2023b), rationales (Zelikman et al., 2022), or pseudo-solutions (Li et al., 2026). How-ever, these methods still start from human-written problems, so human curation still decides which problems the model trains on and, in turn, what it can learn. In language models, self-training is an established approach to self-improvement (Zelikman et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2024; Zuo et al., 2025). Yet a language model can pose problems, not only solve the ones it is given (Zhao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Therefore, we investigate whether a language model can self-train without labels or a curated problem set, requiring only topic descriptors from which it generates its own training problems. In this work, we propose On-Policy Self-Training (OPST) , an approach to self-train a language model with dense supervision derived from self-generated privileged information (Figure 1). We remove both ground-truth answers and the curated problem set: conditioned only on a coarse topic descriptor, the model synthesizes both a query and the privileged information (PI) used to train on it. Since the generated question-answer pairs are no longer human-curated, the quality of this data can be noisy. Hence, we filter the generated data along two axes: Pseudo-verification ensures that a problem is well-formed, requiring the model to reproduce its answer in a fresh context and under a paraphrase. Further, we apply a consensus gate to ensure that the problem is informative, discard-ing problems whose answer the student already consistently reproduces across on-policy rollouts. Because the gate is evaluated against the current student, the difficulty of the retained curriculum implicitly evolves as the student model improves. On the quality-filtered samples, we train the model with on-policy self-distillation (Figure 2), where the teacher provides high-quality token-level supervision to the student, limiting catastrophic forgetting of existing capabilities acquired from supervised data (Shenfeld et al., 2026b;a). These effects come from only the teacher being conditioned on the privileged information, while the student receives the question alone. This asymmetry is crucial in a purely synthetic setting. SFT on the generated answer, or RL reward-ing agreement with it, treats the generated answer as the optimization target, which bounds the su-pervision by the quality of the generation. We show that providing the synthetic answer as context to the teacher instead enables it to produce a reasoning rollout from its own parametric knowledge that serves as an enriched learning signal to the student. The student’s supervision is therefore extended by what the teacher can infer from the privileged, generated context, exceeding what the generated answer alone provides. Iterating this loop improves the model over successive rounds, while an exponential moving average (EMA) teacher stabilizes against self-induced distribution drift. In our experiments, we show that OPST improves language and multimodal reasoning without human-curated questions or answers, outperforming alternative training strategies. On Gemma 4 E2B (Gemma Team et al., 2026), self-training increases MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a) accu-racy by +10 .2 points, with consistent gains on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), GPQA (Rein et al., 2024), and HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019). We also find that self-training in-domain transfers to out-of-distribution domains, with performance either remaining stable or also improving. Applied to vision-language models with unlabeled images alone, Visual OPST improves VQAv2 accuracy by +6 .9 points.

LLM Self-Training. Early self-training methods filter solutions by agreement with a reference answer (Zelikman et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024) or by self-consistency across samples (Huang et al., 2023), and label-free reinforcement learning has since replaced the reference answer with majority votes (Zuo et al., 2025) or the model’s own confidence (Zhao et al., 2026b). These methods remove answers but not problems: each still trains on a curated problem set, which must be collected anew for every task (Singh et al., 2024). Self-play methods let a proposer generate tasks, seeded by as little as a topic description and checked by a code executor or by majority voting over the solver’s answers (Zhao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). Self-generated problems, however, carry information that self-play reduces to a scalar reward: the answer and rationale the model produces while posing them. Used as privileged context for a self-distillation teacher, it instead yields dense, token-level supervision. On curated problems, Li et al. (2026) show that self-generated answers are considerably more effective as teacher context than as a reward, and Zhang et al. (2026) combine self-play with self-distillation for search agents. While their method requires no external training data, question construction is grounded in documents retrieved from an external corpus, whereas OPST generates problems from the model’s parametric knowledge alone. Whether a model can improve from problems it poses itself, with dense token-level supervision and without retrieval or other external grounding, has to our knowledge not yet been studied for general reasoning.

Removing human supervision removes not only human data curation but also quality assurance. Generated samples can contain ill-posed questions or incorrect candidate answers, and training on them propagates these errors. At the same time, not every well-posed sample is worth training on: if the student already solves a problem reliably, the teacher conditioned on z produces nearly the same next-token distribution as the student, and the sample contributes little beyond its generation and rollout cost. We therefore filter any generated sample for two criteria: Pseudo-verification first estimates whether a sample is well-formed, and the consensus gate then approximates whether it is informative for the current student. 4Preprint. 

Pseudo-Verification. We evaluate if a given generated sample possesses two properties of a well-posed problem: its answer should be reproducible without the generator’s own intent as context, and it should not depend on the particular wording of the question. EMA Teacher. The teacher is initialized from the student, ϕ0 = θ0, and updated after every gradient step as ϕk = (1 − β) ϕk−1 + β θ k. Since the teacher produces the dense supervision, it must remain stable. With β = 1 , a single noisy batch immediately changes the targets for subsequent batches, and errors can reinforce themselves. The EMA instead averages out such fluctuations over roughly 1/β steps. Averaging the student’s weights also yields a more accurate model than the student itself (Table 6d), consistent with momentum teachers in vision (Caron et al., 2021). For this reason, the teacher is the model we evaluate. 5Preprint. 3.6 ITERATIVE SELF -T RAINING 

A single round of OPST is limited by the quality of the data and supervision that the initial model provides. We therefore repeat generation, verification, and training over N rounds. The student and teacher weights carry over from one round to the next. For each additional round, πgen generates a new training set, from which we remove problems that duplicate those of earlier rounds. The iterative nature of our loop lets student and teacher co-evolve. Training on new data further improves the student, and through the EMA update, also the teacher. A stronger teacher, in turn, benefits the next round in three ways. It provides more accurate token-level targets on the student’s rollouts. As the generator, it produces better problems and privileged information. And as the verifier (Section 3.4), it judges its own data more reliably, so fewer incorrect samples reach training. Because the teacher is an average over many training steps, only improvements that persist across batches enter this cycle. 3.7 VISUAL OPST OPST is modality-agnostic and can be extended to the multimodal setting by adding an unlabeled modality. We therefore explore this for vision-language tasks by adding images to the process. Visual OPST uses a collection of unlabeled images I and visual topic descriptors Tvis , such as 

“Spatial Relations” , “Object Counting” , or “Attribute Recognition” . Unlike the text setting, it requires unlabeled images as input, from which we generate the same data as detailed in Section 3.3. 

Visual Curriculum Generation. Given an image I ∈ I and a topic τ ∈ T vis , the generator produces a question about the image together with privileged information, (x, z ) ∼ πgen (· | I, τ ).We consider two forms of privileged information. In the first, z is a detailed scene caption that the generator writes for I, describing its objects, attributes, and spatial arrangement. In the second, we prompt the model to detect the objects in the scene, from which we bootstrap bounding-box grounded spatial relations. The privileged caption informs the teacher rollout with explicit visual details that the student must extract from the image itself, while the detected spatial relations help ground the teacher with actual object positions. Both forms can be combined by conditioning the teacher on the caption and the spatial relations. 

Visual Verification. Visual verification tests whether the candidate answer is supported by the image rather than by a hallucinated premise. ## 5 ABLATIONS 

Quality Filtering. We ablate our quality filters by adding them incrementally, starting from OPST on all generated samples. Without filtering, OPST improves over the base model by +5 .2 points (Table 5a). Pseudo-verification adds +2 .7 points and the consensus gate a further +2 .3.

Privileged Information. In Table 5b, removing privileged information shrinks the gain over the base model from 10 .2 to 3.1 points, and a placebo from a different problem on the same topic yields only 2.7. The rationale alone recovers 8.3 points and the answer alone 7.1. The gain therefore stems from sample-specific privileged information, with the rationale carrying more signal than the answer; the residual gain without it likely reflects self-distillation towards the EMA teacher. 

Robustness to Wrong Answers. In Table 5c, we disable both filters and replace a fraction p of the candidate answers with wrong ones. At p = 75% , OPST loses only 1.9 points and remains 3.3 points above the base model, whereas SFT loses 5.7 points and falls 4.4 points below it. Conditioning the teacher on the generated answer, rather than imitating it, therefore limits the influence of incorrect generations. Further ablations are provided in Appendix A.2. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced On-Policy Self-Training (OPST), which post-trains a model on self-generated prob-lems and privileged information, seeded only by coarse topic descriptors. We showed that condi-tioning the teacher on the generated answer, rather than training on it, lets the teacher fill in omitted reasoning steps and limits the influence of potentially noisy data. We found that OPST outperforms SFT and GRPO on the same generated data and methods that rely on curated questions, and extends 9Preprint. to multiple domains and vision-language models, demonstrating that models can self-improve from self-generated data alone. 

Limitations. OPST can only amplify existing knowledge, and since pseudo-verification measures self-consistency rather than correctness, consistent errors still enter training. Our topic descriptors are hand-chosen, and our experiments are limited to small models. Promising directions include label-free stopping criteria and tool- or retrieval-grounded verification. A.1 SELF -D ISTILLATION OBJECTIVES ON GENERATED DATA .Unlike a ground-truth reference solution, generated privileged information z = ( r, a ) can be am-biguous: the rationale r may miss important aspects, and the candidate answer a may be partially or fully incorrect. Therefore, training a model on z directly inherits these defects. SFT on the generated pairs maximizes 

log πθ (z | x) = 

> |z|

X

> j=1

log πθ (zj | x, z <j ), (8) which supervises the model only on the tokens of z. Critically, it treats every token as correct, including any potential omissions and errors. OPSD instead uses z as context: at every state y<t 

the student visits, the teacher supplies a full next-token distribution, so supervision covers the entire student rollout rather than the |z| tokens of the generation. Crucially, the teacher produces this rollout from its own knowledge under the exposure to the generated privileged information. This leads to a more nuanced, higher-quality supervision, since the teacher reweights the model’s own next-token distribution by how consistent each continuation is with the privileged information. This has three consequences for generated data. First, z need not spell out a step for the teacher to supervise it: tokens that move the rollout toward a are upweighted, so the teacher completes reasoning that r

omits. Second, the target is expressed in the model’s own distribution rather than in the surface form of z, so the student does not imitate the phrasing or formatting of the generation. Third, the prior limits the influence of a faulty z: an error shifts the target only to the extent that the model finds it plausible, and where the prior favors a different step, the teacher retains that step instead of copying the error. The student therefore learns from what the model can infer given z as a hint, not from z itself. Consequently, conditioning the teacher on z yields dense, prior-grounded token-level supervision that filters generation noise, making on-policy self-distillation well suited for learning from purely synthetic data. A.6 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

We follow the task formulations of the EleutherAI lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2023) and adapt them to the Gemma 4 chat template. Without thinking, we score all multiple-choice benchmarks, i.e., MMLU, GPQA Diamond, HellaSwag, ARC-Challenge, and Winogrande, by log-likelihood over the closed set of answer options. No answer is therefore parsed from generated text. We use the harness defaults, i.e., 5-shot prompting for MMLU, zero-shot prompting for the remaining multiple-choice benchmarks, and length-normalized accuracy where the harness specifies it. GSM8K is evaluated with 8-shot chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022). With thinking, multiple-choice answers are parsed from the final Answer: X line. GSM8K is graded by exact match of the final numerical answer, and MATH-500 by symbolic equivalence of the final \boxed{} expression using SymPy. For all models and benchmarks, both before and after self-training, the rate of parsing failures and invalid answers is 0%. Performance differences in the thinking regime therefore do not stem from changes in answer formatting. All remaining settings follow the harness defaults. A.7 OPST P ROMPT TEMPLATES
