## Abstract 

On-policy distillation (OPD) improves language-model post-training by letting the student learn from its own sampled trajectories under teacher supervision, aligning the training process with inference-time behavior. However, the rollout policy in OPD is usually treated as fixed: each round samples directly from the current student. We show that this choice can create a compounding teacher–student mis-match, because it requires the teacher to provide effective supervision on whichever trajectories the student samples, including ones less reachable by the teacher itself. On such trajectories, teacher supervision can become noisy or even misleading, leading to undesirable student patterns such as repetition. Moreover, since the updated student in turn defines the rollout policy of subsequent rounds, these pat-terns persist and potentially amplify over training. This failure mode suggests that improving OPD requires treating the rollout policy itself as an optimization target, rather than only modifying the distillation objective. We propose Mixture Proposal On-Policy Distillation (MPOPD), which derives a student-anchored, teacher-guided rollout distribution from a local trust-region objective based on relative teacher sup-port. The resulting target is a geometric mixture of the student and teacher policies, and we introduce a proposal policy that approximates this mixture, enabling rollout generation in a single forward pass. Experiments on 16 math, knowledge, and medical reasoning benchmarks show consistent gains over OPD and recent variants. On math reasoning, our method improves average accuracy by 6.91 over OPD on Qwen3-4B-Base while reducing average generation length by 41%, suggesting that rollout policy design provides an effective way to improve OPD. Code is available at https://github.com/csr16/MPOPD .

## 1 Introduction 

On-policy distillation [10, 1, 34] has emerged as an important post-training paradigm for large language models. Unlike standard knowledge distillation [12, 13, 61, 16] or supervised fine-tuning [44, 4], which train the student on teacher- or expert-generated data, OPD lets the student sample its own trajectories and then distills token-level teacher supervision on those trajectories. This makes the training distribution better match the student’s inference-time behavior, and has led to strong results in summarization, translation, and mathematical reasoning tasks [54, 34, 49]. Recent large-scale models including Qwen3.5, MiMo-V2, and DeepSeek-V4 have therefore adopted on-policy distillation as a core component of their post-training pipelines [46, 51, 6]. The fine-grained, on-policy supervision is what makes OPD powerful, but it also exposes OPD to a potential teacher–student support mismatch. Since trajectories are sampled from the student, the teacher is expected to provide token-level supervision not only on responses it would naturally generate, but also on student trajectories that often have low probability under the teacher. On such low-reachability trajectories, the teacher’s conditional token distribution can become noisy or even

We verify this compounding effect empirically. At se-lected training steps, we use the teacher to complete a student trajectory and a teacher trajectory from the same prompt. 2 Fig. 1 shows two trends under OPD. First, the similarity between the two teacher completions drifts downward over training, with the 10th percentile drop-ping more sharply, indicating that a small but growing tail of student trajectories is rarely reachable under the teacher. Second, this drift also degrades completion qual-ity. The fraction of prompts where the teacher’s com-pletion from a student trajectory receives a lower PRM reward than its completion from its own trajectory grows steadily under both gap thresholds. Both trends are con-sistent with the mechanism above: once the student places probability on trajectories the teacher would rarely reach itself, subsequent rounds reinforce them, and the teacher– student mismatch persists across iterations. To break this loop, we propose to optimize the rollout policy directly, shifting probability toward trajectories the teacher is more likely to reach while remaining close enough to the current student to maintain the on-policy nature. Concretely, we introduce a reweighting function based on relative teacher support, which upweights trajec-tories the teacher supports more strongly than the current student. To stay close to on-policy behavior, we constrain this reweighting to a local trust region around the current student. Solving this problem yields a geometric mixture of the student and teacher policies, with a single scalar interpolating between on-policy and teacher-guided sampling. However, computing this mixture online requires running both the teacher and student at every decoding step, which is costly at scale. We therefore train a proposal policy to approximate it, so that rollouts need only a single forward pass. This gives our Mixture Proposal On-Policy Distillation (MPOPD), where the rollout policy is first derived as a geometric mixture of the student and teacher and then approximated by a proposal policy for efficient generation. Prior efforts to improve OPD have mainly modified the distillation objective [21, 24, 57, 26], or adjusted the rollout policy via heuristic sampling [53, 10]. In contrast, we treat the rollout policy itself as an explicit optimization target, derived from a local trust-region principle and approximated by a learnable proposal policy for scalable generation. Fig. 1 shows that our method avoids the compounding mismatch, with 10th-percentile similarity 0.30 higher and PRM score gap fraction 18 points lower than OPD. We further evaluate MPOPD on 16 math, knowledge, and medical reasoning benchmarks. Across these tasks, MPOPD consistently outperforms OPD and recent variants. On math, it improves average accuracy over OPD by +1.46 on Qwen3-1.7B-Base and +6.91 on Qwen3-4B-Base, while reducing generation length by 41% at the 4B scale.

## 3 Mixture Proposal for On-Policy Distillation 

3.1 Reweighting by Relative Teacher Support 

The reweighting framework in §2 requires a metric that determines which tokens should receive more probability. Here, we choose the relative teacher support : for a token v at prefix (x, y <j ), define 

st(v, x, y <j ) = log π⋆(v | x, y <j ) − log πt(v | x, y <j ).

A high st means that the teacher assigns larger probability to v than the student does. Upweighting such tokens follows two intuitions. First, high teacher probability suggests that the teacher can provide more effective supervision, avoiding noisy supervision on tokens the teacher itself rarely generates. Second, low student probability suggests that the student has not yet fully learned these tokens; if the student already assigns high probability, further emphasizing it provides less benefit. Reweighting by st therefore prioritizes tokens that are both well supported by the teacher and not yet captured by the student. 3Having specified the reweighting signal, we next control how strongly it shifts the rollout distribution away from the student. Baselines. In addition to standard OPD, we compare against three recent variants: ExOPD [57], which applies an extrapolative supervision relative to the student base model; EOPD [24], which augments OPD with an entropy-aware forward-KL term at high-entropy teacher positions; and VETO [21], which uses a geometric mixture of the student and teacher as the distillation target. 

Implementation details. All methods are trained using the verl [43] framework with vLLM [28] as the rollout generation backend. Local-support methods use teacher top-16 support. The proposal policy ˆqt is a full model initialized from the current student at each round. All experiments are conducted on GH200 GPUs. 4.3 Training Efficiency 

Since our method trains an additional proposal policy, we compare its training cost with existing OPD variants. Fig. 4 reports the wall-clock efficiency and step-time breakdown. Although our method introduces an additional proposal policy update, it maintains a favorable accuracy-time trade-off throughout training, with only about 13% extra wall-clock time on average. Note that VETO is trained for one extra epoch to reach its optimal performance, which accounts for its longer wall-clock time. The step-time breakdown further shows where the extra cost comes from. Our method spends more time on model training due to the additional proposal-policy update, but it partially offsets this cost through faster rollout generation, as it samples fewer degenerate long responses. Table 3 further examines whether the improvement is simply due to extra optimization compute. It reports the average performance on math benchmarks along with average per-round rollout generation time, model training time, and peak memory, comparing our method with OPD using one or two student update steps per round. Doubling the number of OPD student updates per round nearly doubles the training time from 75 .00 s to 150 .01 s, but only changes average accuracy from 28 .46% to 

28 .59% . In contrast, our method reaches 35 .37% average accuracy with 131 .12 s of training time per round, lower than OPD with two updates. The additional proposal policy adds only modest memory overhead ( 141 .99 vs. ∼124 GB) and is partly offset by faster rollout generation ( 94 .68 vs. ∼130 s) since MPOPD generates shorter responses. Together, these numbers indicate that the gain comes from improving the rollout distribution rather than merely spending more compute on student updates.

## 5 Related Work 

Reasoning in Large Language Models. Recent progress on LLM reasoning [35, 60, 63] has been driven by two closely related post-training paradigms. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards optimizes the policy against sparse outcome-level feedback, and has emerged as the dominant recipe behind recent reasoning-focused models [42, 59, 64, 39, 13, 45], yielding strong gains on mathematical, coding, and agentic reasoning [7, 67, 52, 65, 66]. Distillation instead supervises the student with dense token-level signals from a stronger teacher [17, 41, 25], and has become a core component of reasoning-oriented post-training pipelines [51, 6, 46], often paired with distilled reasoning corpora [12, 61, 33, 2] or used to transfer reasoning across scales and modalities [4, 37]. 

On-Policy Distillation. On-policy distillation lets the student generate its own trajectories and receive token-level teacher supervision [1, 10, 34], addressing the train–test mismatch of off-policy distillation [27, 11, 48, 32]. Owing to its efficiency in transferring strong capabilities from large teachers into mid-scale students, OPD has been explored along several directions, including cross-family distillation [36], multi-teacher guidance aggregation [9, 50], black-box distillation [58], and self-distillation schemes that internalize the model’s in-context capabilities into its parameters [56, 15, 62, 20]. Despite this progress, recent studies have reported failure modes such as repetition, overlong generations, and entropy collapse [8, 24, 26, 31]. Existing remedies mainly modify the supervision target, via alternative divergences [1], extrapolative or entropy-aware objectives [57, 24], and target reformulations [21, 26], or adjust the rollout policy through heuristic teacher–student interleaving [10, 53]. In this work, we instead study the rollout policy itself in OPD. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We study OPD and identify a compounding teacher–student mismatch: student rollouts can drift into low teacher-reachability regions, where noisy supervision reinforces degenerate patterns across rounds. To address this, we introduce MPOPD, which formulates the rollout policy as a local trust-region problem under relative teacher support, yielding a geometric mixture of the student and teacher. We then approximate this mixture with a learnable proposal policy that generates rollouts in a single forward pass. Across 16 math, knowledge, and medical reasoning benchmarks, MPOPD consistently outperforms OPD and recent variants. Diagnostics show that these gains come from shifting rollouts toward higher teacher-reachable trajectories, suggesting that the rollout policy is a critical design choice for improving OPD. 9Acknowledgments

(a) Do the gains depend on the decoding budget? Training rollouts are capped at 4K tokens, whereas the main evaluation allows up to 16K, so part of the gap could reflect how each method extrapolates beyond its training length. We therefore evaluate all methods under matched decoding budgets, where Acc@4K, Acc@8K, and Acc@16K denote accuracy with a maximum generation length of 4K, 8K, and 16K tokens. MPOPD outperforms OPD and VETO at all three budgets, with gains of 5.00 and 2.37 already present at the training-matched 4K budget. The improvement therefore cannot be attributed solely to extrapolation beyond the training length. 

(b) Is the gap at 16K explained by termination? Since several baselines frequently reach the 16K limit, we next examine termination behavior at this budget. The termination rate (Term. rate) is the fraction of responses that end before the limit, and Acc. | term. is the accuracy among these terminated responses. OPD and MPOPD have similar answer presence and first-answer positions, making it unlikely that OPD’s lower accuracy arises from correct answers appearing later and being truncated. OPD’s higher accuracy conditional on termination is measured on only 0.60% of its responses and is therefore unstable. 

(c) Where does the length reduction come from? The no-answer termination rate (No-ans. term.) is the fraction of responses that end before the limit without a parseable final answer, which would rise if the model stopped prematurely. For the two length columns, we split each correct response at the end of its first correct boxed answer and report the average number of tokens before and after this point. Why use a learned tilted rollout proposal? A possible alternative is to sample from the student 

πt and reweight the resulting trajectories toward the tilted target q∗

> η,t

. However, reweighting only changes the contribution of trajectories that have already been sampled; it does not change which prefixes and tokens are visited during rollout generation. If completions with large teacher–student log-ratio have low probability under πt, they may rarely appear in student rollouts, and importance weights cannot recover trajectories that were not collected. The learned proposal ˆqt instead changes the rollout distribution directly by generating trajectories from an approximation to the tilted target. The clipped weights in Eq. (7) are used only to keep the student update aligned with the OPD objective when training on proposal-generated rollouts. 

Implementation considerations. We initialize ˆq0 ← π0, and at each subsequent round initialize 

ˆqt+1 from the updated student πt+1 before training it toward the next tilted target q∗

> η,t +1

. This warm-start keeps the proposal close to the current student at the beginning of each round, so the proposal update primarily needs to absorb the teacher-induced tilt rather than re-learn the student distribution from scratch. Beyond the fixed-η scheme used in our experiments, it is natural to ask whether η could instead be adapted across rounds, which would correspond to an implicit curriculum over rollout distributions. One conceivable design is to set η so that the local KL between q∗ 

> η,t +1

and πt+1 matches a target radius, and to grow this radius only when the learned proposal tracks the tilted target stably—plausible diagnostics for such stability include the proposal-training loss, the proposal–student KL, and the clipping rate of the importance weights. Conceptually, this would tie the amount of teacher-guided deviation to the current training state rather than to a fixed schedule. We flag this as a possible extension rather than a component of our method, since our reported results use a fixed η.A separate direction concerns the parameterization of ˆqt itself. Rather than maintaining a full copy of the student, one could parameterize ˆqt with a lightweight adapter such as LoRA [18] on top of the frozen student backbone, in which case the proposal overhead would reduce to storing and updating a small set of adapter parameters. We expect this to be largely orthogonal to the rollout-policy design studied here, and leave a careful empirical comparison to future work. 

Limitations. Our study has several limitations. The learnable proposal policy ˆqt introduces modest training-time overhead, which may motivate lighter parameterizations such as LoRA-based proposals at larger student scales. We also treat the tilt strength η as a fixed scalar; adaptive schedules that tie 

η to training-state signals are a natural extension we do not pursue here. The clipped importance weights wt,j (τ ) also introduce a fixed clipping threshold C, and a more principled treatment of the proposal–student gap could further tighten the alignment between rollout sampling and the OPD objective. Finally, our empirical study focuses on the Qwen3 family, with main evaluations at the 1.7B and 4B scales across math, knowledge QA, and medical reasoning, and additional math experiments extending to an 8B student in Appendix B.3. We leave extensions to cross-family teacher–student pairs, still larger scales, and additional modalities to future work. 23 D Declaration of LLM Usage and Broader Impact 

Declaration of LLM Usage. This work studies large language model distillation, where LLMs are central experimental subjects throughout the paper: they are used as teacher and student policies, sampled to generate reasoning trajectories, queried for token-level distillation supervision, and evaluated on mathematical, knowledge-intensive, and medical reasoning benchmarks. LLMs were also used as editing tools to improve manuscript clarity, grammar, and presentation, without replacing the authors’ role in method design, analysis, or experimental interpretation. 

Broader Impact. This paper introduces MPOPD, a rollout-policy design framework for improving on-policy distillation of reasoning-oriented language models. By guiding student rollouts toward higher-teacher support trajectories while retaining the scalability of standalone generation, our method can improve the effectiveness and budget efficiency of distilling reasoning capabilities into smaller models. This may make capable reasoning systems more accessible in domains such as mathematics, knowledge-intensive question answering, and medical reasoning benchmarks. At the same time, stronger distilled reasoning models may inherit general risks associated with LLM deployment, including over-reliance on automated reasoning, hallucinated or incorrect answers, and potential misuse in settings where generated outputs are treated as authoritative. Our work does not introduce a new high-risk application setting, collect human-subject data, or release a model intended for direct deployment; rather, it proposes a training method for existing reasoning-model distillation pipelines. We therefore view its broader societal risks as largely aligned with those of existing LLM reasoning and distillation systems, and encourage downstream users to apply appropriate verification, monitoring, and domain-specific safeguards before deployment in high-stakes settings. 24 E Missing Proofs

Implication for OPD. The proposal-design problem in OPD is different. We seek a rollout policy that remains anchored to the current student while shifting probability toward teacher supported completions. This is formalized in the main text through a local KL( q∥πt) trust region and the relative teacher support score st. Under this formulation, the geometric path is not merely an interpolation heuristic; it is the variational solution induced by the chosen reverse-KL geometry. In contrast, the arithmetic path corresponds to a different notion of closeness and a different proposal objective, one that emphasizes additive coverage of the student–teacher mixture. This distinction is also consistent with the reachability-structured noise analysis. That analysis does not require a particular interpolation path; it states that a rollout policy is favorable when its induced occupancy reduces reachability-risk exposure without incurring too large a second-moment penalty. The geometric rollout policy is aligned with this analysis because it changes the student rollout 29 distribution through multiplicative reweighting by relative teacher support. Whether this reweighting reduces reachability-risk exposure depends on the covariance condition discussed earlier, not on an unconditional dominance claim over other interpolation paths. In summary, arithmetic interpolation is a principled choice for coverage-oriented objectives and bounded importance ratios. Geometric interpolation is the appropriate path for the formulation studied in this work: a student-anchored, reverse-KL-regularized proposal that tilts the current student toward tokens with larger relative teacher support. 30 H More Case Studies
